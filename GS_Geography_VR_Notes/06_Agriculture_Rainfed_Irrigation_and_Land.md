## 1. Indian Agriculture Is Rainfed (GEO-08-01)

Even after decades of independence, **most farmers still depend on the monsoon**. Class line: Indian agriculture is a **gamble in the hands of the monsoon**.

| Figure (class) | Value |
|:---|:---|
| Share of **farm holdings** with **no irrigation** (fully rainfed) | **~60%** |
| Irrigation infrastructure on agri land (your sheet; audio garbled) | **~33%** |
| **Net Sown Area (NSA)** | **~142 million hectares (ha)** (sheet ~**140**) |
| **Arable** (fertile / cultivable) land | **~154 million ha** |
| Gap (arable but not sown) | **~12–14 million ha** (dispute, fallow / rest) |
| **Gross Sown Area (GSA)** | **~1.5 × NSA** |
| India’s **total geographical area** | **~328 million ha** |
| **Reported area** (under Indian control / measured) | **~302 million ha** (rest: Pakistan / China control, unmeasurable terrain) |

**NSA** = land that is **fit for cultivation *and* actually under cultivation**. Arable includes land that *could* be cropped but is not.

**Why GSA is only 1.5× NSA:** India is tropical — temperature allows **two–three crops** a year (kharif paddy → rabi wheat → a zaid crop such as watermelon on the same 100 ha would make GSA **3× NSA**). In practice the extra seasons need **irrigation**. Monsoon rain lasts **~4 months**. Without a canal or tubewell, the farmer crops **only kharif**. That is why average intensity stays **~1.5**.

**Rainfed vs irrigated logic:** **Punjab (PB)** and **Haryana (HR)** get **&lt;75 cm/year** (semi-arid) yet grow basmati / non-basmati rice **because almost every field is irrigated**. Heavy-rain states can still be drought-prone if water **runs off** and there is no storage.

## 3. Uneven Irrigation Inside “Successful” States (GEO-08-03)

### 3.1 Western UP vs Bundelkhand

**Western UP** (rice + India’s main **sugarcane** belt) is canal-and-groundwater rich:

- **Upper Ganga Canal** — operational since **1854**
- **Lower Ganga Canal** — operational since **1872**

Most of UP from **Haridwar** toward **Varanasi** is a **flat alluvial plain**. Class exception: parts of **Sonbhadra / Churkhi in Mirzapur**.

**Bundelkhand** (specified districts of **UP + MP** — already in Lecture 2) is different: **granitic hills** alternating with flats. Granite **does not let water through** except along fissures → **slow groundwater recharge**. Canal network is thin compared with western UP. Farmers dig **farm ponds** in the field; granite holds the rain for a while, but ponds **dry in the lean season** — not perennial irrigation.

**Ken–Betwa Link Canal:** **Yamuna** in the north; **Betwa** and **Ken** in this dry belt. Class: a dam on the **Ken** (**Dhodan / Daudhan Dam**) stores Ken water → **link canal** into the **Betwa** → drinking water + irrigation for the parched belt of **MP + UP**.

### 3.2 Coastal Andhra vs Rayalaseema

**Coastal AP** (Srikakulam on the **Odisha** border down to **Nellore** on the **TN** border): **Godavari** and **Krishna** deltas have **almost merged**. For **~200 km** the **Eastern Ghats** are missing → fertile alluvium, groundwater, canals. Strong **agriculture + aquaculture**.

**Rayalaseema** (class: **Chittoor, Anantapur, Kadapa, Kurnool, Nandyal**): hilly, poorly irrigated except pockets (**Nandyal valley** — rice; some Chittoor / Anantapur — sugarcane). That is why Rayalaseema is poorer than coastal Andhra.

### 3.3 Tamil Nadu: Kaveri delta vs the dry south-east

**Kaveri** enters the plains after **Mettur Dam**. After **Srirangam** it splits into distributaries — the **delta**. Irrigated since the **11th century** (**Grand Anicut / Kallanai**, Cholas). **Thanjavur, Villupuram, Tiruchirappalli** — two rice crops. Contrast: **south-east TN** drought-prone, weak irrigation — the farmer-suicide belt from Lecture 2.

### 3.4 North-East: rain without irrigation

**Assam ~220 cm/year** vs **Punjab &lt;75 cm** — yet Assam has **agricultural drought** news and **Punjab almost never does**. Punjab has canals + free/subsidised power to pump groundwater even if the meteorological monsoon fails.

**Cherrapunji ~1,100 cm/year** — **NCERT still calls it drought-prone**: water **rushes down the slope**; little is stored.

**Why canals were not built:** terrain.

- **Arunachal Pradesh** hills named after tribes: **Dafla, Miri, Abor, Mishmi**. Himalaya from **Nanga Parbat (Kashmir)** to **Namcha Barwa (Arunachal)**; three parallel ranges (**Himadri / Himachal / Shiwalik**) continue — only the *names* change with the dominant tribe.
- Hills bordering **Myanmar**, collectively **Purvanchal Hills**: **Patkai Bum, Naga, Manipur, Mizo / Lushai**, plus **Barail**.
- **Meghalaya**: **Garo, Khasi, Jaintia** — **flat-topped plateaus**, *not* Himalaya; class: part of the **peninsular plateau** family (with Deccan, Chota Nagpur).
- **Brahmaputra** occupies a **narrow valley** between Himalaya (north) and the Meghalaya plateau (south). Hill water dumps into the river → **April–September floods** are perennial in Assam.

### 3.5 Maharashtra: 18% irrigated, rain shadow, suicides

**Western Ghats** from southern **Gujarat** to **Kerala**. **South-West monsoon** hits the Ghats:

- **Windward** (Goa, Mumbai): air **rises → expands → cools → condenses** → heavy orographic rain.
- **Leeward / rain-shadow**: air **descends → compresses → warms** → higher moisture-holding capacity → **less rain**. **Vidarbha** (cotton) and **Marathwada** (sugarcane belt in class) sit here.

**MH ~18% irrigated** + rain-shadow + monsoon failure → crop failure. Same reason MH accounts for **~35–40% of India’s farmer suicides** (Lecture 2 NCRB map). MH also has the **largest number of large dams**, but they sit in **southern / western Maharashtra** — the dry interior is left out.

Class bicycle-pump analogy: compressing air **heats** it without an external flame; the reverse (rising air) **cools** it — that is orographic rainfall.

## 4. Even Irrigated States Have a Water Problem (GEO-08-04)

Class (and a **2024** mains-style prompt they flagged): major challenges of Indian irrigation.

### 4.1 Low water-use efficiency

India uses **~80% of freshwater** in agriculture; **China ~60%**, with higher production — India **wastes** more. Canal irrigation is the worst: class **water-use efficiency as low as ~38%** (your sheet: **34%**). Water is lost at **storage, transport, and distribution**.

**Indira Gandhi Canal:** source **Harike Barrage** (where **Sutlej** meets **Beas**) → tail end **Barmer, Rajasthan**. Most Indian canals are **perennial** (water year-round), tied to a **dam / reservoir** (storage) or a **barrage** (diversion).

| Stage | Loss |
|:---|:---|
| Open reservoir | **Evaporation** |
| Long open canal | **Evaporation** again |
| **Unlined / kachcha** distribution channel | **Seepage** as well |
| **Lined / pakka canal** (concrete sides + bed) | Seepage cut; evaporation remains |

**Floating solar** on reservoirs: generates power **and** shades water → less evaporation; does not eat scarce land. **New** canal projects: class said they move toward **closed pipelines**, not open channels.

**Canal share of irrigated area:** early/mid-**1950s ~55–60%** → now **~25–26%** (sheet ~**20%**). **Groundwater / tubewells** are now the main system.

### 4.2 Warabandi / Barabandi (rotational irrigation)

Head-reach farmers steal the canal; the **tail end** runs dry. State government therefore runs **Warabandi** (also **Barabandi**):

- **Bara / bari** = **turn**
- **Bandhi** = **bund / embankment**

Each farmer gets a **date**. On your turn you throw up a mud–sand–wood bund so the channel water is diverted into *your* field; the next farmer breaks it and builds his own. **Equitable in design.**

**Catch:** the state is **not legally bound** to release water in the main canal on that date. If the canal is dry, germinating seed dies and the farmer **cannot sue**. Canal irrigation = **dependency on someone else**. A **tubewell on your own field** = you water **at will**.

### 4.3 Why tubewells won — and why that is unsustainable

Tubewells are **decentralised, convenient, cost-effective** because states give **highly subsidised or free farm power** (capital subsidy / construction support as well). Operational cost ≈ **zero**. Flooding a field is actually **faster with canal water**, but farmers still prefer the tubewell.

**Night-power trick:** daytime grid demand is high, so the farmer is told to irrigate **11 p.m.–5 a.m.** He switches the motor **on at 11, sleeps, off at 5** — the pump runs **all night**. Free power + a false sense of surplus → **over-extraction**.

**Punjab water table:** falling **70–110 cm/year** (~**1 metre a year**). Class: this is how an “easy” irrigation system makes agri **unsustainable**.

### 4.4 Flood vs micro-irrigation

Most farmers still use **flood irrigation** — class: **obsolete and wasteful**. Government pushes **micro-irrigation (drip, sprinkler)**; **high cost** confines it to a **few regions**.

**Five challenges to recite (class wrap):** (i) still not monsoon-proof / not every farmer covered; (ii) **non-uniform** distribution; (iii) **low water-use efficiency** (80% freshwater, still behind China); (iv) **groundwater overuse** from cheap power; (v) **flood** technique, drip still expensive.

## 5. Non-Mechanised: Small and Fragmented Farms (GEO-08-05)

Labour is **affordable and available** — that is *not* the main brake. The brake is **farm size**.

| Category | Holding |
|:---|:---|
| **Marginal** | **&lt; 1 ha** |
| **Small** | **1–2 ha** (both ends included) |

**Agriculture Census** is meant to be **every 5 years**. Last release **2018**, carrying **2015–16** data. No newer full census yet.

| Year | Number of holdings | Average size |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **1970–71** | **~70 million** | **2.28 ha** |
| **2010–11** | **~138 million** | **~1.15–1.16 ha** |
| **2015–16** (Census 2018) | **~146 million** | **1.08 ha** |
| **2021–22** (provisional, process started) | — | **~0.92 ha** (class also said **~0.9**) |

**2015–16:** **86%** of farmers are **small or marginal** (holding **up to 2 ha**). **66%** are **marginal** alone.

**Why a tractor still does not pay:** small revenue → no purchasing power; even with a subsidy the machine **never recovers** its capital on 1–2 acres — bullocks are rational. **Fragmentation** (inheritance): 3 acres in **three parcels** means moving the machine between plots → high operating cost. Population + inheritance → **more farms, smaller farms**.

### 5.1 India vs USA vs Japan vs China / Israel

**Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), last ~10 years:** largest arable land is **India or the USA**. Class now: **India ~154 million ha**, **USA ~151 million ha**. Population: India **~146 crore**, USA **&lt;40 crore**. USA **&gt;80% urban**; even rural Americans are mostly **not** on the farm. **USA average farm ~150 ha** vs India’s **~0.9 ha** — more than **150×**. Large farms → revenue → machines that actually get used.

**Japan:** tiny paddy plots (Hokkaido snowed under **4–6 months**). Still mechanised because **machines are designed for small fields**. India designs for **large-farmer purchasing power** (link to Lecture 2 feminisation). Uttarakhand **terrace**: you cannot put a tractor on a step — you need a **power tiller**. Class: **India–Japan agri** = copy *design to plot size*, not US scale.

**China:** bigger geographical area but west/north-west/south-west is **desert**; **NSA ~105 million ha** vs India’s **~142**. Population comparable (**India ~146 crore, China ~142–143 crore**). China still mechanises better. **Israel:** desert, high population pressure on agri land, still mechanised. Class reason: **collective farming worked** there; in India the state has pushed it since Independence and it **has not**.

Leave-the-class question: **should India emulate the USA?** US mechanisation is a **compulsion** (Civil War / slavery frame: South needed cheap cotton labour; today few rural workers, high living costs). India has cheap, available farm labour — mechanisation is **not** the same compulsion.

## 6. Land Reforms (Brief — GEO-08-06)

Four legs (plus cooperatives). **SS Sir** will expand.

1. **Abolition of intermediaries**
2. **Tenancy reforms** — (i) **rent reduction**, (ii) **security of tenure**, (iii) **ownership rights** to tenants
3. **Land ceiling**
4. **Land consolidation**
5. **Development of agricultural cooperatives / collective farming** (became a *necessity* after the other four)

### 6.1 Why zamindari existed

British wanted India as a **raw-material colony**, copying Britain’s **enclosures**: few capitalist farmers, the rest as urban industrial labour. Revenue collectors who already had men and horses were made **owners**. Class dated **zamindari / Permanent Settlement to 1790**. Revenue **fixed in perpetuity** (or for decades) so the zamindar would **invest** and behave as a capitalist farmer. In practice: **absentee landlords** — they did not till; they leased to cultivators, skimmed rent, paid the Raj. They were **intermediaries**.

Independence leadership (including **Nehru**) leaned socialist. **Directive Principles, Part IV** — class: this is the constitutional hook (Lecture 2 already had **Article 39(b)/(c)**).

### 6.2 Tenancy: security → investment → yield

A tenant who can be thrown out **will not put ₹1 lakh into the soil**. He **mines** fertility and leaves. **Security of tenure / ownership** is how you get investment and yield, not only how you stop exploitation. If a tenant has cultivated for a set period and paid the due, he should become **owner**.

### 6.3 Ceiling vs abolition of intermediaries

**Late 1960s, Indira Gandhi:** eradicate poverty via a **socialistic pattern** — bank nationalisation, other nationalisations, words added to the **Preamble**. **Ceiling** = cap on land a **family of five** may hold. Class example: **20 ha** ceiling on that class of **irrigated** land; a **100 ha** holder loses **80 ha** to **landless agri workers**.

**Difference:** abolition of intermediaries — if a zamindar **proves he is the tiller**, he could **keep** even a huge holding. **Ceiling** does not care: **above the cap, land goes**, whether you cultivate or not.

### 6.4 Consolidation = chakbandi

**Chak** = plot, **bandhi** = tying together. Two farmers each own strips on **both** sides of a canal. Officer allots **one compact block to A, one to B** (same fertility, same distance from water). That is how you fight fragmentation.

### 6.5 Why cooperatives after this

Abolition + ceiling **created an army of small/marginal farmers** with **no bargaining power**. A 100 ha landlord could not be squeezed by a middleman; **100 people with 1 ha each** can. Government: register under the **Societies Act**, pool, take the cooperative benefits. **China and Israel succeeded; India did not** — but the idea was not dropped.

## 7. Collective Farming Today: Coop, SHG, FPC / FPO (GEO-08-07)

Three legal doors:

| Form | Who | Exam hook |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Agricultural cooperative** | Farmers as members | Ownership + **one member, one vote** (farm size does not buy extra votes) |
| **Self-Help Group (SHG)** | Often landless / same socio-economic peers | Bank will not lend to one landless tenant (no collateral, can abscond). **Six people cannot all run**; peer pressure = repayment |
| **Farmer Producer Company (FPC) / Farmer Producer Organisation (FPO)** | **≥10 farmers**, register under the **Companies Act** | **Hybrid:** coop *soul* + private-company *management* |

**Coop pluses:** sense of ownership (**Amul** — millions of 1–2 animal backyard dairy farmers, not one mega-herd). Profit → **dividends** + retained expansion (fodder plant, veterinary hospital). **Equality in voting.**

**Private-company plus:** **managerial / professional expertise**. Class school analogy: government schools have land, midday meals, better-paid teachers who cleared a competition — yet parents (including government teachers) still pick **private** schools for **professional management**.

**FPC / FPO:** owned by farmers; **one vote each**; cooperative-style subsidies; **professionally managed**. Amul’s success also credited to **Verghese Kurien**’s professional leadership.

Collective farming **raises produce size** → **bargaining power** (the 5 litres of surplus milk vs Amul moving the consumer price). It also lets a **machine be used on a large pooled field**.

## 8. Custom Hiring Centres (GEO-08-08)

**Machine penetration (2020–21, class):** **~20 tractors per 1,000 ha** — too few if every farmer must *own* one. Ownership is the wrong test. **Paddy harvest** already **shares** machines: Punjab first, then the same combines move to **Haryana**.

**Custom Hiring Centre (CHC)** = a village / block / panchayat “shop” of **tractor, tiller, seeder, laser leveller, harvester**. Farmers **hire**. Can be set up by an **individual, an FPO, or a cooperative**. Rules vary by state.

**Class numbers (illustrative capital math, not a scheme gazette):** unemployed youth with **₹10 lakh** + bank loan **₹40 lakh**; **~25% capital subsidy** so he repays **₹30 lakh** not 40. **Gram Sabha** (all **18+** in the village) or a committee **caps the rent** — investor gets a return, farmer is not gouged.

**Stock:** **&gt;40,000 CHCs** in India vs **&gt;6 lakh villages**. They cluster in **developed agri belts** (**Haryana, western UP, Malwa plateau**). Missing in **tribal Jharkhand**, the **North-East**, remote tracts.

**Private “Uber for tractors”:** **Uber Tractor / Hello Tractor**; **Mahindra Tringo**; **EM3 Agri Services — Gold Farm** (Punjab) — this is the sheet box **“Bring Gold & Farm”**. Same as Ola/Uber in Delhi: the app **dies in Churachandpur, Manipur** if there is no nearby fleet. **Remote mechanisation still needs the state**, not only an app.
