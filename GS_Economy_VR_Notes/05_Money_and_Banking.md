## 1. What counts as money (ECO-10-01)

**Money** = any asset that is **liquid** for the common public. Liquid means you can use it to buy goods and services. The same idea in the other phrase: it can be used as a **medium of exchange**. Liquidity is not the same thing for every entity. Here it means liquid for households like you and me.

Minimum contents, for now:

1. **Currency** (cash, notes).
2. **Demand deposits** — the part of bank deposits that can be **withdrawn immediately**.

Illustration, not a rule: ₹50,000 in a savings account, of which the bank lets you take ₹40,000 at once. The **₹40,000** is the demand deposit. “Immediately” is not only a visit to the branch or an ATM. **Net banking and UPI** count. Whatever sits in the account and can be used when you want, digitally or in cash, is money. More pieces of the definition come later. Do not add them yet.

**Money supply** = amount of money **with the public**.

**Public**, in this topic, is anyone **except** the **financial sector** (example: **banks**) and the **government**. From the four sectors (firms, households, government, financial sector), what remains is **households and firms**. Non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) sit with the public for now. How they differ from banks is a later section.

**Trap.** In fiscal policy, “public” means the **government** (public finance, public investment, public expenditure). In money and banking it means the opposite exclusion: **not** the government, and **not** the banks.

**Home loan.** The bank does not hand the cheque to the borrower. A ₹50 lakh loan is a cheque in the **seller’s** name. The seller is a household or a firm, so a demand deposit with the public rises. **Any loan** by a bank, or by the wider financial sector including NBFCs, **increases** money supply. A digital loan does the same: the money lands straight in a public account.

**Equated Monthly Instalment (EMI)** is repayment. Paying it **cuts** your demand deposit. Money has moved from the public to the bank, so supply **falls**.

**Subsidy** to farmers: demand deposits rise, and the money came from the government, so supply **rises**. **Tax** paid to the government: supply **falls**.

**The cheque question** (he placed it a few years back; the exact paper was not named). A person deposits a cheque of ₹1 lakh and **immediately** withdraws the same amount. Money supply **does not change**. “Immediately” is the hint that the bank had no time to lend that money out. Assume the cheque moved **inside the public** (parents to you, or an employer’s salary cheque). Parent’s deposit down ₹1 lakh, yours up ₹1 lakh. Then you withdraw: deposit down, currency up, by the same amount. Currency plus demand deposits is unchanged. If the question had said the cheque came **from the government or a bank**, supply would rise. That was not given. Do not assume it.

Two checks on any “what happens to money supply” stem:

1. What happened to **currency and demand deposits together**?
2. What is the **direction**? Financial sector or government → public, or the reverse?

**Bonds and money supply** (his answer to a doubt). A **government** bond is the government borrowing from the public. The first look says supply falls. The borrowing is generally for welfare and flows back to the public, so supply **generally rises**. A **corporate** bond is less clear, because the company is itself part of the public. Largely, bonds issued and bought raise supply, because a bond is a loan. Do not treat every bond purchase as a one-line rise.

## 2. Three demands for money (ECO-10-02)

**Money demand** = the **purposes** for which money is held. Supply was “how much.” Demand is “why.”

| | Meaning | Mainly depends on |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Transaction** | Money as a **medium of exchange**. Everyday spending (a book, food). **Expected.** | **Income.** Higher income, more goods and services, higher transaction demand. |
| **Precautionary** | Money for an **unexpected** emergency. A young person’s doctor’s fee. For an elderly person the same visit may be ordinary spending — then it is transaction demand. | **Income.** You cannot predict the emergency, so income decides how much you can spend when it hits. |
| **Speculative** | Money as a **store of value**, held **for a return**. | **Bond interest rate**, and the link is **inverse**. |

If it cannot serve transaction demand, he will not call it money. That is the basic role.

**Speculative demand, precisely.** Money sitting in the bank account, earning the **deposit rate**, is speculative demand. The moment you withdraw it to buy something, it has become transaction or precautionary demand.

The deposit rate is the return money itself pays. It is **not** the main determinant. Money is not the only store of value, and several substitutes pay better: **bonds**, **shares**, **gold** and other precious metals, property, mutual funds. Comparing all of them at once is too much, so **bonds stand in for the rest**. Bonds are chosen because, like deposits, they pay an **interest rate**. Shares pay a return, but not an interest rate. Share market is a later class.

**Bond interest rate is greater than the deposit rate**, almost always. Buying a bond **sacrifices liquidity** — that money can no longer buy goods and services — so the return is higher. His realistic gap: at least **2 to 3 percentage points** between a common savings-account rate and the bond rate.

**Result.** Higher bond interest rate → lower speculative demand for money, and the reverse. **Speculative demand for money is inversely related to the bond interest rate.** Same shape as the law of demand (quantity demanded inverse to price). By that logic, the **bond interest rate is like the price of money**.

Example, not a current rate: bond rate **7%**, deposit rate **4%**. Bond rate rises to **8%**, deposit rate unchanged. A person with ₹1 lakh in the account uses ₹10,000 to buy bonds. Speculative demand has fallen by **₹10,000**. Why the bond rate moved (monetary policy) is a later class.

He also said: while the money is still in the account it is speculative; the comparison that matters for the exam line is **money versus bonds**.

## 3. Bonds — loan, not ownership (ECO-10-03)

A **bond** is a **financial instrument** used by the **issuer** to **raise funds in the form of a loan**. A financial instrument always raises money for the issuer. What the issuer gives back is what makes the instrument.

| | Raises funds? | What the buyer gets |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Bond** | Yes | A **loan**. Always like a loan. |
| **Share** | Yes | A very small **ownership** (shareholding). **Never** like a loan. |

Interview line he wanted in one sentence: both are financial instruments used to raise funds; **bonds are always like a loan; shares are never like a loan.**

A bond is a **certificate** of that loan. Two sides:

- **Issuer** — needs money. **Government** (regular fiscal deficits) and **firms** (**corporate bonds**, when they need funds for capital).
- **Holder** = investor = buyer. Must have spare money. Best everyday example: **banks** (they hold the public’s deposits). Also **households**, and **foreign portfolio investors (FPIs)** — foreign investors, mostly from developed countries, holding a mix of Indian bonds and shares because the return is generally higher than at home. More on foreign direct investment and FPI sits in the external sector. **RBI** does hold some bonds, but its main role here is **regulator**, not the model holder.

**Face value** = the **principal** of the loan raised through that bond issue. A bond of face value ₹10,000 means each unit lent ₹10,000 to the issuer. Repayment, as in any loan, is principal plus interest.

**Bond interest rate** = the return to the holder. That is why a bond is a store of value.

How it is set: **generally market-determined, at a point of time**, through **demand and supply of money** — not of bonds. That demand-and-supply of bonds comes later, and it sets the **bond price**. The interest rate is **like** the price of money. Drop the word “like” and the sentence is technically wrong: a price is rupees per unit, and money is already in rupees, so “rupees per rupee” does not work. Money has no **direct** price. The indirect price is its **opportunity cost**.

**Opportunity cost**, two definitions, same conclusion:

1. **Return on the next-best alternative.** Next-best alternative of money, in this comparison, is the bond. So the bond interest rate **is** the opportunity cost of money.
2. **What is sacrificed while holding an asset, or while making a choice.** Holding liquid money sacrifices the higher interest the bond would have paid.

The term is economic, and it also covers non-priced choices (his example: the opportunity cost of an hour). **Cost-benefit analysis is a different thing** — the costs of a project (fixed cost, then maintenance) against the return to the owner, typically infrastructure. Do not equate the two.

**Fixed, and still market-determined.** There is no contradiction. **At the moment of a successful issue**, the issuer must pay **today’s** market rate. After the bond is successfully issued, that rate is **fixed** for that bond. “Successfully” matters because issue alone does not guarantee a buyer. If a stem says only “once issued,” he still called that **correct**. Older bonds cannot have their interest rate “fall” or “rise.” A newspaper line “interest rates are falling” means the rate on **new** bonds.

**Prelims habit.** If a stem says “interest rate” and does not name which one, think **bond interest rate** (loan rate is in the same family). Do **not** assume the deposit rate. When they mean the deposit rate, they usually say so.

**Risk, in one line.** A bond pays a **fixed** return, so it sits among the **least risky** instruments. Share prices move in real time, so shares sit among the **most risky**. Nothing in the share market is called a fixed return.

## 4. Debentures, and who is paid first (ECO-10-04)

**Debentures** are similar to bonds (face value, interest rate, and the other features). They are **riskier than bonds**, so the interest rate is **often** higher. “Often” allows a rare exception. If the **same company** issues both, debenture holders get the higher rate. The extra interest is the reward for the extra risk.

**Bankrupt** here means the business is closed after very heavy losses. The firm may still have some money left. The **order in which holders are paid** is what makes one instrument riskier than another. His order:

1. **Bonds** — paid first, so least risky.
2. **Debentures.**
3. **Shares** — last. Inside shares he named ordinary and preference shareholders and did **not** teach them.

If nothing is left after the bondholders, the debenture holder is simply not paid. He had already been paid a higher interest for taking that risk. There can be instruments between these three. These three are enough.

**Collateral.** A **government** bond has **no collateral**. The sovereign authority itself is what stands behind it. A **corporate** bond may or may not have formal collateral, depending on the company. Public-sector enterprises often issue bonds without collateral of that kind.

## 5. Maturity value, and why a bond has a price (ECO-10-05)

**Maturity value** = total value at the end of the **maturity period** = **face value + the interest amount**.

Classroom example, which he called **unrealistic** (face value too low, rate too high, period too short — kept easy, and a numerical is unlikely):

- Face value **₹1,000**
- Interest **10% per annum**
- Maturity **1 year**
- Interest amount = 1,000 × 10% × 1 = **₹100**
- Maturity value = **₹1,100**

A real bond is rarely one year. He said the typical minimum is **3–4 years**, and **10–20 years** is normal. Two or three years would need compound interest. The idea does not change: keep adding the interest to the face value, and the end figure is the maturity value.

**Loans are not tradable** in ordinary cases. You repay the bank. You do not sell the loan to a friend. A loan is a two-party deal. **Bonds are tradable** — you may hold to maturity, or sell in between if a buyer exists. Shares and debentures are tradable too. Tradable things need a price.

**Bond price** is **market-determined** through **demand and supply of bonds** (not of money — that was the interest rate).

**Supply of bonds** depends on the issuer’s **need for funds**. A higher fiscal deficit means more government bonds. His illustration, not India’s deficit: at 4% of GDP the government might issue 1 lakh bonds; at 5% it might issue 1.2 lakh of the same face value. On a **new** issue the price is not fluctuating. See below.

**New bonds: price = face value.** Buy in the moment after issue and you pay the face value. Six months later the same bond may be ₹1,010. Higher and lower **prices**, and higher and lower **demand**, are always statements about **old** bonds. You cannot say the price of a new bond rose.

**Bond price often lies between face value and maturity value**, both ends included. In the example, anywhere from **₹1,000 to ₹1,100**. It is generally **nearer the face value**. A buyer near the maturity value would get little return. It is **rare, but possible**, for the price to fall **below** face value — someone who needs cash may sell at a loss.

## 6. What moves the price of an old bond (ECO-10-06)

Demand for bonds depends on interest rates. Two of them.

**Domestic interest rate** means the rate on **new** bonds. If that rate **falls**, new bonds pay less, so buyers prefer **old** bonds (their rate is already fixed). Demand for old bonds **rises**, so their **price rises**. Bond prices and the domestic interest rate on upcoming bonds are **inversely** related. The reverse (domestic rate up → old-bond demand and price down) follows.

**Foreign interest rate** means rates in the countries our FPIs come from — **developed** countries (he named the United States, Europe, Japan), not whichever country you happen to think of. If foreign rates **rise**, an American investor who might have sent $50,000 may send $30,000 or $40,000 and keep some at home. These choices are relative, not all-or-nothing. **Demand for Indian bonds by FPIs falls**, so **old-bond prices fall**.

## 7. Bond yield (ECO-10-07)

**Bond yield** = the **net / real** return. The interest rate is the ordinary (nominal) return. Once you buy second-hand, the return also depends on the **price** you paid.

**Yield = (interest amount at that time ÷ bond price) × 100.**

The interest amount is fixed, because the rate on that bond is fixed. The price moves. So **yield is inversely related to bond price**. Price down, yield up. Price up, yield down.

Same example: one buyer pays **₹1,000** just after issue, another pays **₹1,010** later. The first gets the **higher yield**. Earlier entry, lower price, higher yield — the same idea as entering a share market early. A mature buyer looks at **both** the interest rate and the price. A high rate bought at a high price can still be a poor deal.

Wherever the price section ended in “higher old-bond price,” add **lower yield**. Wherever it ended in “lower price,” add **higher yield**. That is the usual Prelims shape: which factor moves yield or price, and **which way**.

**Higher bond yield is higher borrowing cost in the future**, most clearly for the **government**, which borrows all the time. A new investor will not accept 7% when old bonds are yielding 10%. The government has to offer the higher return on the new issue too. Newspapers treat a rising yield as **bad news** for that reason. It is **good news for the bond investor**, who is earning more. Both can be true.

## 8. Government securities (ECO-10-08)

**Government securities (G-Secs)** are acknowledgements of government borrowing from banks, the public, FPIs, and others. They are **gilt-edged securities**. **Gilt** in a Prelims stem is a direct or indirect reference to government securities. Best quality, because no issuer has a better reputation for repaying the loan **with interest**. A default is theoretically possible and rare. He said it has **not happened in India since 1991**.

Anyone in need of money may try to issue a bond. Reputation is the second condition. Without it the issue does not succeed.

The G-Sec market is **regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)**. RBI may also buy some securities. Its known role is making the rules. The main rule in class: **RBI arranges primary auctions and invites primary dealers.** The notebook line is easy to misread as “sector.” He said **primary dealers**, and the main ones are **banks**.

**Primary** = new, first issue. **Secondary** = resale. This is true of bonds and shares, not only G-Secs. The auction here is generally on **yield**, and **lower yield wins**, because a lower yield is a cheaper loan for the government. His illustration: Bank A bids 7.5%, Bank B 7.25%, Bank C **7%**. The 7% bid wins, and the others buy at 7% or not at all.

**Retail Direct Scheme, 2021.** Since 2021, **retail investors** (individuals) can also buy **new** G-Secs. They do **not** bid in the auction. The amount is too small to make them primary dealers. They buy **after** the auction closes.

Two types, by maturity:

| | Treasury bills | Government bonds / dated G-Secs |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Maturity | **Under one year.** Three he named: **91 days (3 months), 182 days (6 months), 364 days.** Past **364 days**, it is not a Treasury bill. | **More than one year.** Generally at least about **3 years**. He has seen **30 to 40 years**. |
| Who issues | **Centre only**, so far. No economic reason given. Centre has been enough, because the use of these bills is limited. | **Centre and States**, to raise funds for **development** (infrastructure; he tied this to government investment / GDCF). States need loans for State-list items such as education and health. States do not lean on Treasury bills, because most of their cash need is long-term. |
| Market | **Money market** | **Capital market** |

**Why the exception.** The government wants **all** its borrowing, short and long, with the RBI, not a second regulator. Share market: SEBI. Long-term **corporate** bonds: SEBI. **Government bonds: RBI**, even though they are capital-market instruments.

**Words.** A **security**, in the financial sector, can mean any financial instrument (bond, debenture, share). A **government security** means a short-term or long-term loan taken by the government. Unless a stem says otherwise, a **bond** means **long term**.

### Lecture 1 — locks

1. Money = currency + **demand deposits** (the part you can use at once, including UPI). Public = households and firms. **Not** banks. **Not** government.
2. Loan or subsidy → supply **up**. EMI or tax → supply **down**. Cheque in, and the same sum out **immediately** → **no change**.
3. Transaction and precautionary demand depend on **income**. One is expected, the other is not. Speculative demand is **inverse** to the **bond** rate. That rate is **like the price of money**, and it exceeds the deposit rate.
4. Bonds are loans. Shares are ownership. Interest rate is fixed **after a successful issue**, and was set by demand and supply of **money**.
5. Bankruptcy order: **bonds, then debentures, then shares**. Government bonds have **no collateral**.
6. Example maturity value **₹1,100** on ₹1,000 at 10% for one year. Price of a **new** bond = face value. Old-bond price usually sits between face value and maturity value.
7. Domestic rate on **new** bonds down → old-bond price **up** → yield **down**. Foreign rate up → Indian old-bond price **down** → yield **up**. Yield = (interest amount ÷ price) × 100.
8. Gilt = G-Sec. Treasury bills: **91 / 182 / 364** days, **Centre only**. Dated securities: **over one year**, **Centre and States**. Retail Direct **2021**. G-Secs are capital market, regulated by **RBI**, not SEBI.
