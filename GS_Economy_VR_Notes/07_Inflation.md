## 1. Inflation is a general rise, measured by a basket (ECO-12-01)

**Inflation** = a **general increase in the prices of goods and services** in an economy.

Two extremes both fail.

- **One item is not inflation.** A biscuit packet moving from ₹10 to ₹15 can be a local input problem (he said the raw material may have become costlier). The sample is too small.
- **Every item is not practical.** Lakhs of goods and services exist. Tracking all of them is slow, so the data would not be frequent. An average consumer also does not buy bullets, guns, fighter jets or bombs, even though those enter GDP.

The middle path is a **reference basket** or **consumption basket**. Stuff it with goods and services that reflect the **average consumption pattern**. Each item has a price. Anything **free** is left out. That is the international standard, so **free ration** is not in the basket. Multiply and add, and you have the **cost of the basket**. When that cost rises, inflation is happening. The **percentage change** in the cost of the basket is the **inflation rate**. The same basket has become harder for an average consumer to buy.

**Who acts on the number.** The **principal** agency for price stability in India is the **RBI**. The legal mandate is the RBI’s. The government is also responsible. It is not the only one, and it is not the principal. Every **two months** the **Monetary Policy Committee** meets: **six** members, from the RBI and the government. Monetary policy here means decisions on **money supply** and the **interest rate**, taken so that prices stay stable. They need a number on the table, which is why India’s inflation figure comes **every month**. Two months of a higher rate can mean action. Two comfortable months can mean status quo.

## 2. The survey that fills the basket (ECO-12-02)

A basket is credible only if it tracks what people actually buy. Governments run a **consumption survey**. In India the name is the **Household Consumer Expenditure Survey (HCES)**. The **National Statistical Office (NSO)** is supposed to do it every **five years**. It has been delayed, once for more than ten years.

For this chapter the survey gives two facts:

1. **How much** a person spends in a month: **monthly per capita expenditure (MPCE)**.
2. **Where** that money goes: the **composition** of expenditure.

His simplified frame, **not** India’s official weights: MPCE **₹1,000**. Food **30%** (₹300), clothing **10%** (₹100), housing **10%** (₹100), fuel **5%** (₹50), education **5%** (₹50), health **10%** (₹100). Food gets the largest weight because a poorer country spends first on food. He compared India with China, not with Pakistan or Bangladesh. The official weights wait for the measurement class.

The basket is not the same in Japan, China or the United States, because per capita income differs. It is not the same after ten years. It is not the same for an average **urban** Indian and an average **rural** Indian. If the survey is late, people feel inflation in the market while the official number says there is none, because the basket is still pricing what they used to buy.

**The gap.** The basket in use until last year rested on the **2011–12** survey. A survey was done in **2017**. Results in **late 2018** showed consumption expenditure **falling**, not rising. A general election was due in **early 2019**. The data were not released. The stated reason was **quality**. Any government, he said, would have found that report card hard to carry into an election. Officially the survey was done and **not accepted**. COVID then became the excuse for further delay. The survey that was accepted is **2023–24**. The basket was updated **this year** on that base. **Surjit Bhalla**, whom he called a former government adviser, wrote in *The Indian Express* in the previous two days asking that the **2017–18** results at least be **released**, even if not accepted. He tied this to a wider worry about India’s statistical reputation, and named **P. C. Mahalanobis**.

Until that update, the old basket was showing inflation till **December last year** at about **0.25%, 0.5% or 0.75%**. After the basket was made current, the rate is around **4%, 4.5% or 4.9%**. The update is **one** reason, not the only one.

What changed in the basket, as examples: **radio** and **rope** came out. **Data charges** (life after **Jio, 2016**), **OTT** prices, and **PNG** and **CNG** as fuels beside petrol and diesel went in.

## 3. Purchasing power, and too much money (ECO-12-03)

**Purchasing power** of a currency is how much it can buy. It is the real power of the note.

**As the inflation rate rises, the purchasing power of the currency falls at the same time.** A water bottle at ₹10 becomes ₹20. Quantity and quality are unchanged. The same note now buys less, so a fixed income no longer funds the plan that was made for it.

**Money supply and inflation:** an increase in money supply **can** raise the inflation rate. The useful picture, in his words: inflation as **“too much money chasing too few goods.”**

Money’s main job is to be a **medium of exchange**, and to escape **barter**. Barter needs a **double coincidence of wants**: each side must hold what the other wants. A central bank pumps the money: **RBI** in India, the **Bank of England** in the United Kingdom, the **Federal Reserve** in the United States. The amount should match the economic activity on the ground, which GDP measures. If activity barely rises and money rises a lot, prices can rise.

Classroom economy, mechanism unchanged if you add zeros:

| | Goods produced | Money supply | Average price |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Start | 10 | ₹100 | ₹10 |
| Money doubled, goods unchanged | 10 | ₹200 | ₹20 |
| COVID-type shock, money unchanged | 5 | ₹100 | ₹20 |

In the third row factories are shut and workers have gone back to their villages. The same money is chasing fewer goods. Prices still go from ₹10 to ₹20.

Politicians want more money in the system. People look at the **nominal** amount and feel richer. Credit gets cheaper. The RBI, if it is independent, looks at the **long** damage. If it is not fully independent, it should at least have **operational autonomy**. Politicians follow the electoral cycle. Giving in can win the next election and, later, produce **hyperinflation**. Once an economy has gone through hyperinflation it is very hard to bring back. He named **Germany after the First World War** (the Treaty of Versailles forced reparations; he connected the later political collapse, including Hitler, to that breakdown), **Argentina** and **Venezuela**. A note that was meant to buy a car can, in that kind of episode, buy a candy. Germany he treated as the exception that recovered, and only after a long division.

## 4. Where inflation comes from (ECO-12-04)

Four sets. Economics, however complicated, sits on demand and supply.

| Set | Also called | What it means here |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Demand-side** | **Demand-pull** inflation | Aggregate demand rises, supply does not |
| **Supply-side** | **Cost-push** inflation | A shock cuts supply, demand does not |
| **Structural** | **Structural** inflation | The same pressure, but **long-term**. A shock is a disturbance, positive or negative. “Structural” means it will not change next month |
| **Other** | — | **Lecture 2:** cartelisation, hoarding, expectations, policy of the big developed economies, foreign-capital inflows |

**Structural**, so the word is not only about prices: failure to industrialise, too many people stuck in agriculture, weak infrastructure, and energy dependence are structural because none of them moves overnight. India imports **around 90%** of its **crude oil**. A war in the Middle East (he named Israel’s war with Iran, and the United States entering it) reaches Indian prices because that region is a source of the oil. He said GS Paper 3 asked this year what the government is doing to cut energy dependence and secure energy.

On the “other” list he gave one live line and stopped: when the **United States** raises interest rates, India feels it. When a small rich country does, India does not. About **20%** of India’s exports go to the United States, so a US tariff threat tied to Russian oil matters. The rest of that list is next class, with **depreciation**.

**Demand-pull.** In micro, one buyer and one seller settle at an equilibrium (his pen: 10 units at ₹100). In macro the same bargain is **aggregate demand** and **aggregate supply**. If supply is unchanged and aggregate demand rises, prices rise. If demand is unchanged and supply falls, prices rise.

By the expenditure method, **aggregate demand = C + I + G + NX**.

- **C** — consumption demand
- **I** — investment demand
- **G** — government expenditure
- **NX** — net exports, demand for Indian goods from the rest of the world

Anything that moves one of these can move prices. Economics is not physics: a higher income **can** raise consumption. It need not, if the person saves because a recession feels close. Prelims therefore asks what **can** happen, not what must.

Consumption depends on income, but not on the whole income. From ₹100, tax of ₹10 leaves less, and a cash transfer adds some back. The spendable amount is **income − tax + cash transfers**. A tax cut, or a larger unemployment allowance, or a larger cash transfer, can raise disposable income, then consumption, then prices. Demand can jump at once. Factories take time. That gap is why experts call unconditional cash transfers **inflationary**. He treated the rush of Ladli, Lakshmi and Mahalakshmi-type schemes just before elections, and a hypothetical doubling of salaries through the **8th Central Pay Commission**, as the same mechanism. The rupee amounts he used (₹50,000 promised, ₹10,000 now) are the illustration, not a scheme notification.

Two more demand-side factors sit beside C, I, G and NX:

- **Population.** A larger population is a larger market, so a larger chance that prices rise.
- **Money supply**, from section 3. **Black money** pushes harder. It is unaccounted, tax has not been paid, and the holder wants to turn it into an asset quickly, often by overspending. He linked more black money, and so more of this pressure, to economies with a large bureaucracy, wide discretion and weak accountability. India was his mixed-economy example. He did not call it the only reason.

## 5. Cost-push: price is cost, margin and tax (ECO-12-05)

A supplier’s price rests on three things. If any one rises, the price **can** rise. It is not automatic.

**Price = cost of production + profit margin + tax** (his example of the tax is GST).

Worked pen: cost **₹100**, margin **20%** so **₹20**, tax about **5%** of that **₹120** so about **₹6**. The consumer pays **₹126**. If the margin is pushed to **30%**, or the tax to **18%**, the consumer pays the difference.

**Cost of production** splits in two.

- **Labour:** **wages**. A cost to the firm, income to the worker.
- **Non-labour:** **land**, **capital** (physical and financial), and **raw materials / intermediate goods**. Land becomes costlier if stamp duty rises, or if buying it requires an **environmental impact assessment** and a **social impact assessment** (local jobs, tribal rights). He called those rules good policy and, for the producer, extra cost. Fuel that is costlier, at home or from abroad, raises both factory cost and transport cost.

## 6. Structural inflation, where the class stopped (ECO-12-06)

Structural inflation is the **supply-side** story **stretched over the long term**. It cannot be fixed by tomorrow’s order.

**Infrastructure deficiency and a high logistics cost.** A broken road for a long stretch wastes part of the supply. The producer recovers that waste as a higher price. The consumer pays. He treated weak and poor-quality infrastructure (roads that fail in the monsoon, a collapsed bridge, a flyover planned into a building, a four-lane road that becomes two lanes without warning) as a standing addition to the cost of doing business in India. Maharashtra and, less certainly in the audio, Madhya Pradesh were the pictures. Do not turn the anecdotes into a state-wise fact list. The lock is: logistics waste becomes the consumer’s price, and the repair is slow.

**Resource shortages.** Two kinds.

**Food.** He will say in a later class, and he previewed it now: **inflation in India is food-driven**. Food prices move first. Other prices follow. The food items he named as the culprits:

- **Pulses.** India produces less than it needs. A poor household’s protein is pulses. Output has risen since the **technology mission on pulses**, running since **Rajiv Gandhi’s** time, the **1980s**, but consumption has risen faster. The gap remains.
- **Perishables:** **tomato, onion, potato**.
- **Edible oil / vegetable oil.** About **56%** of edible oil is imported. **Around 90%** of crude oil is imported. Both oils are an import dependence. When **Malaysia** and **Indonesia** stopped palm-oil exports, Indian prices rose at once. **Russia** and **Ukraine** are major **sunflower-oil** suppliers. That war disturbed the supply for a long time.

Farmers stay with **rice and wheat** because the Food Corporation of India buys them and pays on time. They do not trust that oilseeds and pulses will get the same procurement. That is why **crop diversification** is slow. He said it was asked this year. Same crop, year after year, also mines one set of nutrients out of the soil. Diversification would help the economy and the soil. It will not be fast.

**Non-food.** **Fertilisers, crude oil, pesticides, memory chips, lithium-ion batteries.** He rejected the phrase “mean inflation.” The phrase is **memory-chip inflation**: India consumes electronic goods and does not make the chips. About **90%** of semiconductor chips are imported. An international chip-price rise lands inside India. Lithium-ion batteries are mostly imported from **China**. An electric vehicle was meant to cut fuel imports. It replaces oil imports with battery imports unless the capacity is built at home. That capacity will not appear tomorrow, and a hurried Indian battery, he said, would be costlier and less efficient than the Chinese one. This is why the shortage is structural.

**Depreciation**, and the five other factors, are **Lecture 2**. Basket weights are still closed.

### Lecture 1 — locks

1. Inflation is a **general** rise in prices. One biscuit is not enough. Every good in GDP is too many. The rate is the percentage rise in the cost of a **consumption basket**. Free goods stay out.
2. The basket comes from the **HCES**, which the **NSO** should run every **five years**. It reports **MPCE** and the composition of spending. **2017** was done and not accepted. The live base is **2023–24**. Radio and rope are out. Data, OTT, PNG and CNG are in.
3. A higher inflation rate **is** a lower purchasing power. Extra money **can** raise prices when it chases too few goods. Ten goods and ₹100 mean an average price of ₹10. ₹200, or only five goods, means ₹20.
4. Demand-pull works through **C + I + G + NX**. What can be spent is **income − tax + cash transfers**. Population, money supply and black money sit on the same side. Demand moves faster than factories.
5. Cost-push price = **cost + margin + tax**. The pen that costs ₹100, with a 20% margin and about ₹6 of tax, sells at **₹126**.
6. India’s inflation, in his preview, is **food-driven**: pulses, tomato-onion-potato, edible oil. Edible oil imports are about **56%**. Crude oil imports are about **90%**. Chip imports are about **90%**. That food-driven pattern is what he later called **skewflation** (Lecture 2).

## 7. Depreciation is imported inflation (ECO-14-01)

A producer who buys at home pays in rupees. A producer who imports pays in a foreign currency, and the currency the world takes without argument, in his telling, is still the **US dollar**. Cracks exist (BRICS, de-dollarisation). The dollar is still the principal one. The conversion is the **exchange rate**: how many rupees buy one dollar.

Worked barrel. International price stays **$100**. Only the rate moves.

| Rate | What it means | Cost of one barrel |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| **₹95** | one dollar costs 95 rupees | **₹9,500** |
| **₹90** | the rupee has become **more** valuable | ₹9,000 |
| **₹100** | the rupee has **depreciated** | **₹10,000** |

He said ₹90 “will never happen” soon, and that ₹100 “will definitely” come. Those are his forecasts, not a lock. The lock is the direction: **more rupees per dollar = depreciation**. He slipped once and called the move from ₹95 to ₹90 depreciation. The worked numbers correct it.

When the currency depreciates, the same import costs more. India is a **net importer**, so the cost of production can rise, and in ordinary circumstances the producer passes that on. In a very competitive market he may instead **cut his own profit** and leave the price unchanged. So depreciation **can** be inflationary. It is not certain. Inflation that arrives this way is **imported inflation**: the country is importing goods, and it is also importing inflation.

## 8. Cartels and hoarding (ECO-14-02)

**Cartelisation** is a few large producers in one industry **colluding** — the word is negative — to form a **cartel**, so they can restrict output and lift the price of their own product. It is artificial scarcity, often by a tacit understanding, not by an advertisement.

- **Abroad there is no law that bans a cartel of sovereigns**, so those cartels exist openly and by treaty. The famous one is **OPEC**, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries: he said about **ten** oil exporters came together in the **1960s**, led by **Saudi Arabia**, and still allocate a **quota** so that supply, and therefore the price, can be managed. Earlier, rubber-producing countries and coffee-producing countries had done the same.
- **At home, cartels are banned.** Laws that ban them are **anti-trust** laws. India’s is the **Competition Act, 2002**. The **Competition Commission of India** can act **if it has proof**: airlines (he named IndiGo and SpiceJet), cement, or steel (Steel Authority of India, Jindal, Tata) jacking up prices together, with no real shortage. A price rise from ordinary competition is not a cartel. A share-market pump and dump is not this Act. He parked that for the capital-market class.

**Hoarding** is stocking a commodity **beyond the legally permissible limit**. Inside the limit, the same act is only **stocking**. The illustration he used, not a gazette number: traders may hold up to **100 tonnes** of pulses; beyond that it is a crime. People hoard what is already short. They then release it in small doses at the higher price.

The weapon is the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**. A commodity is essential, in his definition, **when the government puts it under this Act**, because it fears a shortage. The government can then dictate production, supply, distribution, transport and storage, and can set a stock limit. Recent uses he named: **stock limits on sugar**; and, a few months before this class, during the Israel–Iran disruption of gas, a household limit of **one LPG cylinder in 25 days**. He mentioned a *Hindu* report of **22** cylinders in Delhi.

## 9. Expectations (ECO-14-03)

If almost everyone shares a rumour, the economy’s outcome changes. Salt, today **₹30 a kg**. A rumour says that by midnight it will be **₹3,000**. People buy for later as well as for today. The shortage that was only a rumour becomes a shortage, and the price does rise. That is a **self-fulfilling expectation**. Inflation that arrives this way is **expectation-augmented inflation**.

Managing inflation is two jobs: enough supply, and convincing people not to expect the jump. The second job sits with the **RBI**. Before an MPC decision it reads the **Inflation Expectations Survey of Households** (he called it **IESS**). If the survey is adverse, the press conference after the announcement is used to talk the expectation down. Words work only if the institution is **credible**, and credibility is past action.

## 10. A rich country’s rate, and sterilisation (ECO-14-04)

Only a developed economy at the centre of global finance matters. A rate change in Norway does not. A rate change at the US central bank does. The bond-market mechanics of a US **rise** wait for that class. Today’s move is a US **cut**.

Interest is the price of capital. Developing countries are **capital-scarce**, so they pay a higher rate. Developed countries are flush with capital, so their rate is lower. The labour mirror: India is labour-surplus, so wages are low; the United States is labour-scarce, so wages are high. People move toward the higher wage. Capital moves toward the higher return.

Classroom rates, which he called arbitrary but not absurd: **India 6%, United States 4%**. If the US, facing weak growth, runs an **expansionary** policy — lower rate, more money — and cuts from **4% to 2%**, the gap widens from 2 points to 4, and capital comes in harder.

The dollars cannot be spent in India. They are converted, through the RBI or an agency it authorises, at his rate of **₹95**. He mixed million and billion. The arithmetic he used is **$1 billion × 95 = ₹9,500 crore** added to the domestic money supply. He also said about **$140 billion** had come in over two months. Do not rebuild a second conversion from that.

**Sterilisation** (he also said neutralising) keeps the dollars and pulls the extra rupees back out. The RBI issues **bonds** of the same size through **open market operations**. The new rupees leave the spending stream. The dollars stay.

## 11. Deflation, disinflation, and the 2–6 band (ECO-14-05)

**Deflation** is a **negative** rate of inflation: prices in the basket are falling. **Disinflation** is inflation still positive, but **rising more slowly**. He said India, last year, was on a disinflationary path. Deflation is the one to fear.

A pen at ₹100, then ₹90, then ₹80, gives the producer a reason to cut output or to lay people off. Those people are also the buyers. Demand falls, output is cut again, and the loop tightens. That vicious circle is the **deflationary trap**. It pulls down production and growth, not only prices. Once an economy is in it, climbing out is very hard.

**Japan** is where this was first studied in modern times, so the name is **Japanification**: **deflation plus stagnation** (growth down *and* prices down). High growth in the **mid-1980s**, then a downturn, **excess capacity**, and more than **two decades** in the trap. A few years before this class there was a fear that **China** might be caught the same way, because it produces beyond its own need and has been kept going with subsidies. A government package created demand and China got out. He would not predict how long that lasts.

Inflation should be neither too high nor too low. The RBI’s legal mandate, already on the deficits note, is **4% ± 2**: ideal **4**, floor **2**, ceiling **6**.

- **Above 6%** punishes the **consumer**. Purchasing power falls.
- **Below 2%** punishes the **producer**. There is too little reason to produce more.
- **Zero, or a negative rate, is not the ideal.** The band balances the two.

## 12. Shrink, skimp, skew, and both at once (ECO-14-06)

Cost has risen. Raising the sticker price would lose a price-sensitive buyer, so the producer cuts a corner.

| Word | What stays | What changes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Shrinkflation** | The packet’s price | Quantity falls, so the **price per unit rises**. A ₹10 pack that held 10 biscuits now holds fewer. |
| **Skimpflation** | Price **and** quantity | **Quality** falls. Same money, worse good. |

His pictures: Parle-G getting smaller, and a Dairy Milk bar becoming a wafer (shrinkflation); ketchup that used to be thick and is now watery (skimpflation).

**Skewflation** is overall inflation driven by the prices of a **few** commodities, not by most of the basket. **India’s inflation is food-driven, so what India is living through is skewflation.** Food moves first. Other prices follow.

**Biflation** is inflation and deflation **at the same time**. Essential prices rise. Non-essential prices fall (OTT, gold, jewellery, recreation). It is most common in a crisis, because people stock food and medicine and postpone the rest. He named **COVID**, **2008**, and the **1929** Great Depression.

## 13. Stagflation, speed, greed, wages, assets (ECO-14-07)

**Stagflation** is **stagnation plus inflation**: growth low or stuck, prices up. Japanification is the opposite pairing: growth down and prices down. Stagflation is a **supply-side** event, so the remedy has to be supply-side. Cutting the money supply does not restart the oil.

It was first studied properly in the **United States after the 1973 oil-price shock**. The notebook has **1975**. The year he dictated is **1973**. Arab countries imposed an **oil embargo** on the US for supporting Israel. Factories stalled, oil prices rose, growth fell. The fix was to resume the oil. **India, and most of the world, had the same pairing in 2020–21**: growth negative, prices high.

**Speed.** These bands are **per month**, not per year.

| Name | Per month |
|:---|:---:|
| **Creeping** (he also said crawling) | **0–4%** |
| **Walking** | **4–10%** |
| **Running** | **10–20%** |
| **Galloping** | **20–50%** |
| **Hyperinflation** | **above 50%** |

For about the last decade India’s inflation has sat in the creeping and walking region. Hyperinflation is purchasing power turning to ash. Today he added **Czechoslovakia** beside yesterday’s Germany. Galloping is a burst, not a crawl.

**Greedflation** is inflation driven by the producer’s greed. **Profit** is the entrepreneur’s legitimate reward: legal and ethical. **Profiteering** may still be legal, but it is not ethical. Cost and markup both up **10%** can still be profit. Cost up **10%** and markup up **50%** is greed. It was widespread in COVID. An **IMF** study was **mixed** across countries and products. The **Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy (CMIE)**, a private body, found major Indian producers’ profits **more than four times** higher after COVID than before. Demand had been weak, so the jump was not a story of booming sales.

**Wage inflation** is the rate at which wages are rising. India’s official **Wage Rate Index** comes from the **Labour Bureau**. It covers only **mining, manufacturing and plantation**. It misses services, which are more than half of GDP, and most of agriculture. The index is real. It is not the whole labour market.

**Asset inflation** is the rate at which asset prices are rising. Gold has one rate, land another. He did not name an index for it.

## 14. Who gains: creditors and debtors (ECO-14-08)

With a rising inflation rate, **creditors lose and debtors gain**. Borrowers, whether a person, a firm or a government, would rather have the higher rate.

₹1,000 lent for a year comes back as ₹1,000. In the market it now buys less. The lender loses. The borrower gains.

The **largest debtor in any country is its government**. The largest in the world, in his line, is the **US government**. The largest in India is the **Indian government**. Inflation can therefore help the government as a debtor. It can make the **debt-to-GDP** ratio look more comfortable, because prices lift **nominal** GDP, the denominator, even when real debt and real GDP have not improved. Last year’s prints of about **0.25% and 0.5%** — the stale basket from Lecture 1 — worried parts of the government for this reason. A very low rate would make the ratio look worse.

The same person can be hurt in one role and helped in another. As a lender she loses. As a borrower she gains.

**Named, and not opened, when the class stopped:** savers; fixed-income earners; rich and poor; employers and employees; capitalists and labour; profit earners and wage earners; bond investors and stock investors; export competitiveness; taxpayers, through **bracket creep** and **fiscal drag**.

### Lecture 2 — locks

1. Depreciation (**₹95 to ₹100**) raises the rupee cost of a **$100** barrel from **₹9,500 to ₹10,000**. That **can** be **imported inflation**.
2. **OPEC**, from the **1960s**, is an open cartel of states. At home, the **Competition Act, 2002** lets the Commission act on proof. **Hoarding** is stock beyond the legal limit, under the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**.
3. A shared rumour can fulfil itself. That inflation is **expectation-augmented**. The RBI reads the household expectations survey before it decides.
4. A US cut from **4% to 2%**, against India’s **6%**, pulls capital in. **Sterilisation** is the RBI issuing bonds to pull the new rupees back out.
5. **Deflation** is a negative rate and can trap the economy. **Disinflation** is a slower positive rate. **Japanification** is deflation plus stagnation. Above **6%** punishes the consumer. Below **2%** punishes the producer.
6. **Shrinkflation** cuts quantity. **Skimpflation** cuts quality. **Skewflation**, in India, is food. **Biflation** is essentials up and non-essentials down.
7. **Stagflation** is weak growth with rising prices, first studied after **1973**. The monthly bands are 0–4, 4–10, 10–20, 20–50, and above 50. CMIE put post-COVID profits at **more than four times**.
8. **Creditors lose, debtors gain**, including the government. The rest of the impact list is the next class.
