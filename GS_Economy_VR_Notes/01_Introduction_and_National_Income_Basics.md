## Section A: Two Branches of Economics

*Context: "Indian Economy is like studying economics for our country called India. Most of the questions that you get in GS are around Indian economy only."*

Economics has **two fundamental branches**:

---

### 1. Microeconomics (Micro = Small)

*   **Definition:** The study of **economic behaviour from an individual perspective**.
*   **Individuals** here = **Consumers** + **Producers**.
    *   **Consumers** → those who **buy** Goods & Services (G&S).
    *   **Producers** → those who **produce & sell** G&S.
*   G&S are consumed because they provide **utility** (satisfaction).

##### Goods vs Services
| | Goods | Services |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Tangible** — can see/feel physically | **Intangible** — consumed but cannot be shown physically |
| Examples | Food items, notebooks, pens, books | Transportation, communication, software |

---

#### Demand for a G/S

*   **Definition:** Relationship between **quantity demanded** of a G/S and **its price**.
*   **Quantity demanded** = Different quantities of a G/S that a **consumer is willing to buy** (at different prices).

##### Law of Demand

> **Quantity demanded** of a G/S is **inversely related** to its price, **keeping other factors constant**.

*   Two parts:
    1.  **Inverse relationship** — Higher price → lower quantity demanded; Lower price → higher quantity demanded.
    2.  **Ceteris paribus** ("keeping other factors constant") — Other factors = factors other than price that can impact quantity demanded.
        *   Example of other factor: **Income of the consumer**.
*   *Context: "When you went to buy rice expecting ₹100/kg but found ₹200/kg, you bought only half a kg. That is the inverse relationship."*

---

#### Supply of a G/S

*   **Definition:** Relationship between **quantity supplied** of a G/S and **its price**.
*   **Quantity supplied** = Different quantities of a G/S that a **producer is willing to sell**.

##### Law of Supply

> **Quantity supplied** of a G/S is **directly related** (positively related) to its price, **keeping other factors constant**.

*   Two parts:
    1.  **Direct relationship** — Higher price → producer willing to sell more (more profit/revenue); vice versa.
    2.  **Ceteris paribus** — Other factors = factors other than price that can impact quantity supplied.
        *   Example: **Input prices** (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, water/irrigation inputs).
*   *Context: "Producers would be happier if they get higher price — which is reflected in the law of supply."*

---

#### Market / Market Mechanism

*   **Definition:** A mechanism under which the **market forces of demand and supply interact** to result in the **market price** of a G/S.
*   Demand & Supply are also called **market forces**.
*   Physical markets (vegetable market) and **digital markets** (Amazon, Big Basket) are both examples — digital markets are considered *better* examples because of larger number of buyers/sellers.

##### Market Economy

*   **Definition:** Economy where **almost all economic sectors** (e.g., Agriculture, Industries, etc.) **follow market mechanism** — i.e., prices determined through demand & supply.
*   India is **NOT** a market economy. India is a **Mixed Economy** since the **1950s** (Five-Year Plans).

##### Mixed Economy (India)

*   **Definition:** An economy where there is a **combination of both** the role of **Government** and the role of **Market** in different economic sectors.
*   Also described as: Mix of **Capitalist** (market emphasis) and **Socialist** (government emphasis) economic principles.
*   **Till 1991** → Greater role of **Government** (lesser role of market).
*   **Post-1991** → Greater role of **Market** (lesser role of government).
    *   *1991* = Year of **severe economic crisis** in India (Balance of Payment crisis). After 1991, many changes were brought — government reduced its direct role, market role increased. Still increasing decade by decade.
*   *Context: "Unless you know what is market, you can't understand Indian economy — that is the reason I did demand, supply, market in the very first section."*
*   **Examples of Government role still present:**
    *   **MSP** (Minimum Support Price) — Government determines prices for many crops, not market.
    *   Price regulation in certain industries (e.g., Khadi shops).
    *   Government is considered the **opposite** of market — it regulates/fixes prices.

---

#### Change in Demand for a G/S

*   **Definition:** Change in **quantity demanded** of a G/S **at all possible prices**.
    *   Different from Law of Demand (which links one quantity to one price level). Here: at **all** prices, quantity demanded changes.
*   **Two types:**
    1.  **Increase in Demand** → ↑ in Qty demanded of a G/S at **all possible prices**.
    2.  **Decrease in Demand** → ↓ in Qty demanded of a G/S at **all possible prices**.
*   **Cause:** Due to **change in other factors** (income, festivals, pandemics, etc.).
*   **Examples:**
    *   ↑ Income → ↑ Demand (more confidence, buy costlier products).
    *   Festival season → ↑ Demand for fruits (no matter what price, more quantity demanded).
    *   COVID pandemic → ↑ Demand for sanitizers/masks; post-pandemic → ↓ Demand.

---

#### Change in Supply of a G/S

*   **Definition:** Change in **quantity supplied** of a G/S **at all possible prices**.
*   **Two types:**
    1.  **Increase in Supply** → ↑ in Qty supplied at all prices — always due to **lower cost of production** (lower input prices, lower taxes, etc.).
    2.  **Decrease in Supply** → ↓ in Qty supplied at all prices — always due to **higher cost of production** (higher input prices, higher taxes, etc.).
*   **Cause:** Due to **change in other factors**.
*   **Example:** Fertilizer becomes costlier (due to West Asia crisis → petroleum products costlier → fertilizer costlier) → ↓ Supply of agricultural products → producer will sell less even at higher prices because higher input price neutralizes higher selling price.

---

#### Combined Relationships (Winding up Micro)

| Scenario | Effect on Market Price |
|---|---|
| ↑ Demand for G/S + **same Supply** | → **Higher** Market Price of G/S (and vice versa) |
| ↑ Supply of G/S + **same Demand** | → **Lower** Market Price of G/S (and vice versa) |

*   **Example (Demand ↑):** Rising income levels → producers aware consumers can pay more → higher market prices. Festival season → fruits costlier.
*   **Example (Supply ↑):** Good monsoon → ↑ supply of agricultural products → lower market prices. *Context: "Good monsoon expected → food prices relatively stable — they are talking about the same thing."* Bad monsoon/drought → ↓ supply → higher prices.

> **Note:** Direct microeconomics questions in UPSC are less common but not impossible. Don't go into more detail beyond this. The tools (demand–supply analysis) will be reused in other topics: demand & supply of money, foreign currency exchange rate, etc.

---

### 2. Macroeconomics (Macro = Large)

*   **Definition:** The study of **economic aggregates and their behaviour**.
*   **Aggregate** = Adding up / summing up. Whenever we aggregate something in economics → macroeconomics.

##### Examples of Economic Aggregates

| Aggregate | Examples | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **National Income** | GDP, GNP, etc. | GDP Growth Rate (e.g., 7% p.a. = for each ₹100 of G&S produced last year, ₹107 this year) |
| **Aggregate Prices / Price Index** | CPI (Consumer Price Index), WPI (Wholesale Price Index) | Higher Agg. Prices = **Inflation**; Lower Agg. Prices = **Deflation** (less common) |

*   **Behaviour** = Are aggregates going up or coming down? At what rate?
*   Macroeconomics is **directly more important** for GS exam. Micro is interesting but direct questions are less common.

## Section C: Topic 1 — National Income Accounting (Basic Macroeconomic Concepts)

---

### Four Types of Economic Sectors & Their Roles

| # | Sector | Definition / Role |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Firms (Companies)** | **Producers** of different types of G&S. Includes both private sector & public sector enterprises (SAIL, ONGC, etc. are firms, NOT government). Examples: car-making companies, software companies, fertilizer companies. |
| **2** | **Households (HHs)** | **Groups of consumers** with **similar consumption habits** (≈ family). All of us are household members. Similar food, furniture, services consumed within a family unit. |
| **3** | **Government** (Centre, States, UTs, Local) | **Four Roles:** |
| | | **(i)** As **buyer of G&S** — just like households, govt purchases goods/services for official use. |
| | | **(ii)** As **provider of services** — public administration, defence, etc. (Services where market mechanism literally cannot be applied → hence govt provides them even in market economies. This is why market economy definition uses "**almost** all sectors".) |
| | | **(iii)** **Welfare role** — Govt provides **subsidies** (discounted inputs, e.g., subsidized fertilizers to farmers), **infrastructure** (roads, schools, hospitals). |
| | | **(iv)** **Levies & collects taxes** — Govt decides who pays what tax and then collects it. **Main source of revenue** for the government. |
| **4** | **Financial Sector** | Acts as an **intermediary** between **surplus fund sectors** and **deficit fund sectors**. Best example: **Banks** — accept savings from surplus sectors, provide loans to deficit sectors. *(Note: Financial sector is technically a part of Firms — banks are also companies providing banking services. Separated here because of the unique & important role.)* |

##### Surplus vs Deficit Fund Sectors

| | Surplus Fund Sector | Deficit Fund Sector |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Revenue > Expenditure (have more than they need) | Expenditure > Revenue (have less than they need) |
| **Contribution** | **Savings** | **Loans / Borrowings** |

##### Savings & Borrowing Rankings

| Rank | Top Savers (contribute to savings) | Top Borrowers (depend on loans) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Households** (top savers) | **Firms** (main loan takers — need capital for production) |
| 2 | Firms | Government (3 of 4 roles involve spending; only taxes generate revenue) |
| 3 | Government (least saver — typically spends more than it earns → always in deficit) | Households (least borrowers) |

*   *Context: "Government typically spends more money than what it earns. So government is always in deficit. Therefore in savings, government contribution is the least."*

---

### Circular Flow of Income & Expenditure

*   **Definition:** Reflects **interrelationships** between different **economic sectors**.
*   We start with a **Two-Sector Model** (Firms ↔ Households) — captures ~80–85% of major circular flow. Government (taxes, subsidies) and External Sector (exports, imports) are added later.

---

#### Real Flow (Without Prices / Adjusted for Prices)

```
                 ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
                 │   Final G&S (→ to HHs)           │
                 │   (e.g., cars, food, services)   │
                 ▼                                  │
          ┌──────────┐                       ┌──────────┐
          │  FIRMS   │◄──────────────────────│    HHs   │
          └──────────┘                       └──────────┘
                 │                                  ▲
                 │   Factors of Production (→ to    │
                 │   Firms) / Factor Inputs         │
                 │   (e.g., labour from HH members) │
                 └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

*   **Firms → HHs:** Final Goods & Services (e.g., final cars produced by car-making firm go to households for consumption). "Final" because whenever a household gets a G/S from a firm, it is always **final**.
*   **HHs → Firms:** Factors of Production / Factor Inputs (e.g., household members provide **labour** — work in car-making companies/factories). Labour = simplest example; also capital, etc. (to be done in next class).
*   **Key Insight:** Both sectors are **mutually dependent** — "Circular flow is like democracy in economics. No one is superior or inferior." Companies depend on us for factor inputs; we depend on them for final G&S.

---

#### Nominal / Monetary Flow (With Prices)

```
                 ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
                 │   Factor Payments / Factor Cost  │
                 │   / Factor Income (→ to HHs)     │
                 │   (e.g., wages for labour)       │
                 ▼                                  │
          ┌──────────┐                       ┌──────────┐
          │  FIRMS   │◄──────────────────────│    HHs   │
          └──────────┘                       └──────────┘
                 │                                  ▲
                 │   Private Final Consumption      │
                 │   Expenditure (PFCE) (→ to Firms)│
                 │   (e.g., payment for buying a    │
                 │    book, food, metro ticket)     │
                 └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

*   **Firms → HHs:** **Factor Payments** (what firms pay for factors of production → to households).
    *   Also called: **Factor Cost** (from firm's perspective — part of production cost) = **Factor Income** (from household's perspective — it is their income/salary).
    *   *Context: "When I get salary from the institution — for the institution it is factor cost, but for me it is factor income. Same thing, perspective change."*
    *   Example: Wages paid by car company to workers.
*   **HHs → Firms:** **Private Final Consumption Expenditure (PFCE)** — expenditure made by households when they consume final G&S.
    *   *"Private"* here refers to **households** (as opposed to government).
    *   Examples: Buying a book, food item, metro ticket — all PFCE.

##### Real vs Nominal — Summary

| | Real Flow | Nominal / Monetary Flow |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Without prices / adjusted for prices | With prices / monetary value included |
| **Firms → HHs** | Final G&S | Factor Payments (Factor Cost / Factor Income) |
| **HHs → Firms** | Factors of Production (Factor Inputs) | Private Final Consumption Expenditure (PFCE) |

##### PYQ Reference
*   **Prelims question:** "Which of the following represent the **real sectors** in an economy?"
    *   A farmer **producing** a crop → **Real** (no price involved, just production).
    *   A loan given by a bank → **Nominal** (loans always in monetary value).
    *   A farmer **selling** his crop in APMC Mandi → **Nominal** (the moment price comes in → nominal).
*   *Context: "You will be lucky if you get such a question — believe me. And you can't afford to make mistakes there."*

## ECO-02-01: Factors of Production & Factor Payments

### 4 Types of Factors of Production
1. **Land:** Natural resources used as input to production (e.g., plot of land, water, minerals).
   - **Factor Payment:** Rent.
2. **Labour:** Human resource inputs to production.
   - **Factor Payment:** Wages.
3. **Capital:** Long-term, man-made resource inputs to production (e.g., machineries, factories).
   - **Factor Payment:** Interest.
   - Capital is often the **costlier factor** due to its **lumpy nature** (can't be divided easily — either a machine is bought as a whole or not at all).
   - Firms need bank loans to buy capital. These loans are sourced from savings, which are mostly done by households (HHs) in expectation of earning interest.
4. **Entrepreneurship:** Risk-taking ability in production (e.g., launching a new product).
   - **Factor Payment:** Profits. (Top-level management or founders take the risk and earn profits beyond standard wages).

## ECO-02-02: Nominal vs Real National Income & Base Year

### National Income (NI) Definition
Total money value (**Quantity × Price**) of all final goods and services produced by the residents of the country over a given period (e.g., 1 year).

### 2 Types of NI (on the basis of Money Value)
1. **Nominal NI (NI at current prices):**
   - Values current year's production at **current year's prices**.
   - Formula: `Sum of (Quantities of final G/S produced in current year × Price in current year)`
   - **Limitation:** Nominal NI can increase solely due to higher prices (inflation), even if real production is declining.

2. **Real NI (NI at constant prices):**
   - Values current year's production at **base year's prices**.
   - Formula: `Sum of (Quantities of final G/S produced in current year × Price in Base Year)`
   - **Real NI is a better indicator** for comparing NI/growth because it has "less surprises" and adjusts Nominal NI for inflation.
   - `Real NI = Nominal NI / NI Deflator` (The deflator is a measure of inflation).

### Understanding Base Year
- Base year is a reference year from the past used to calculate Real NI.
- **Rules for selecting a Base Year:**
  1. It should be a **Normal year** (with minimal economic/geographical surprises, e.g., NO drought years, NO pandemic years like 2021).
  2. It should neither be **too new** (inflation can't be properly analyzed if it's too close) nor **too old** (many new types of goods/services may not be valued correctly). Ideally, it should be revised every 7–8 years, with a minimum 3-year gap.
- **Current Base Year for India:** **2022-23** (Revised from 2011-12, implemented since 2026 based on the recommendation of the **Vishwanath Goldar Committee**).

## ECO-02-03: Final vs Intermediate Goods and Depreciation

### Types of Goods based on usage
- **Intermediate Goods:** Goods used up in the production of other goods/services.
- **Final Goods:**
  1. **Consumer Goods:** Used for final consumption by Households (HHs) or Government.
  2. **Capital Goods:** Used by firms to produce other G/S **without** loss in its identity over a short term.
- *Note:* The same good can be a final good for one entity (e.g., Household buying an item) and an intermediate good for another (e.g., a firm using it to produce something else).
- To calculate NI, we **only include the value of final goods/services** to avoid **double-counting**. Note: Sale of second-hand goods is not included.

### Depreciation of Fixed Capital
- Gradual loss in the value of capital goods due to normal wear & tear, obsolescence (mainly due to technological development), etc.
- It reduces the ability of capital to produce new goods.
- **Provisions for Depreciation:** Firms keep aside a part of the capital value every year so that new capital can be bought after complete depreciation.

#### Gross vs Net NI
- **Gross NI:** NI not adjusted for depreciation of fixed capital.
- **Net NI:** NI after adjusting for depreciation of fixed capital.
- `Net NI = Gross NI - Depreciation of Fixed Capital`

## ECO-02-04: Capital Formation & Expenditures

### 3 Types of Expenditures on Final Goods
1. **PFCE (Private Final Consumption Expenditure):** Expenditure on new final consumer goods by Households.
2. **GFCE (Government Final Consumption Expenditure):** Expenditure on new final consumer goods by the Government.
3. **GFCF (Gross Domestic Fixed Capital Formation):** Expenditure on new final capital goods by firms, government, or households (e.g., Purchase of new houses by HHs is considered infra development/capital formation).

### Gross Domestic Capital Formation (GDCF)
- GDCF reflects improvement in productivity across different sectors and is a crucial determinant of longer-term economic growth.
- `GDCF = GFCF + Change in Unsold Stocks/Inventories/Working Capital`
- **Change in Stocks (Closing Stocks - Opening Stocks):** Difference between the values of raw materials, semi-finished, and finished goods held by firms at the end of the year versus the beginning of the year.
- **Synonyms:** GDCF is also known as Investment Expenditure, Capital Expenditure (CapEx), or simply Capital Formation.

## ECO-02-05: Residents of a Country

NI measures the output produced by the **residents** of the country. There are 2 types of residents (Resident Individuals & Institutions, though mostly discussed are individuals):

### 1. Resident Individuals
Individuals who **ordinarily reside** in a country and their **centre of economic interest** lies in that country.

- **Employment / Tourist Rule:**
  - If an individual resides in a foreign country for **up to 1 year** for employment or tourism, they remain a resident of their **home country**.
  - If they reside in a foreign country for **more than 1 year** for employment, they become a resident of the **foreign country (host country)**.
- **Diplomats / Government Employees:** Government employees working in another country (e.g., diplomats) are **always** residents of their home country, regardless of duration.
- **Students:** Students studying abroad (even for 3-5 years) remain residents of their home country because their centre of economic interest (fees, expenditure) is funded by parents in the home country.
- **Citizenship vs Residency:**
  - Nationals (citizens) of a country can be non-residents.
  - Non-nationals (foreigners) of a country can be residents.
