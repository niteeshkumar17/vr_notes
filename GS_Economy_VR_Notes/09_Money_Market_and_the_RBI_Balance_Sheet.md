## 1. Treasury bills are zero-coupon bonds (ECO-16-01)

A Treasury bill was already placed in the money market. The borrower is the **government**, and in India the **central government**. The lenders are **banks**, and also the **public**. Households can buy them. Most of the stock sits with banks, which prefer government paper. They are **tradable**.

There is **no interest rate**. There is a **return**, and the return is a **yield**. Coupon means interest, so a **zero-coupon bond** is a bond with a zero interest rate. It is issued at a **discounted value** and repaid at **face value**. That is the unusual part. In Lecture 1, face value was the value at which a bond is issued. Here face value is the **maturity** value.

His example, which he called random: face value **₹100**, discounted value **₹95**. The discounted value has to be lower, or nobody buys. **Return = face value − discounted value = ₹5.** The yield is that return turned into a percentage. Both an interest rate and a yield are written as a percentage, which is why the two get mixed. A Treasury bill has only the yield. The exact sum is not needed. The **discounted value is market-determined**.

The face value is a **rounded** figure, ₹100 or ₹10,000, never something like ₹98.5. That makes the issuer’s obligation easy to add. His picture: **1,000** bills of ₹100, maturing in **91 days**, is exactly **₹1 lakh**.

**Cash management bills** are not regular Treasury bills. They are brought in for a temporary liquidity problem of the government. The borrower and the lenders are the same kind. They are occasional, not the standing instrument.

## 2. Call money (ECO-16-02)

**Call money, call loans, and call borrowings** are one instrument. They are **mostly inter-bank loans**. The borrower is **always a bank**. The lender is generally a bank. Sometimes the lender is a **financial institution that is not a bank**. His example is the **Life Insurance Corporation**. The sheet’s “RBI” in that blank is not what he said.

They solve a **short-term liquidity problem**: a sudden shortage of funds, for instance a day of heavy withdrawals. They are **not tradable**. A call loan is an ordinary loan from one bank to another. It does not change hands. Maturity typically **starts at one day**. If the need is immediate, the bank goes here, or to the repo window, and does not wait to issue a certificate of deposit.

The interest is **market-determined**. The names are the same: **call rate, call money rate, inter-bank offer rate (IBOR)**. IBOR is the more modern name.

The rate is the **price of the loan**. If it rises, either more banks want to borrow, or fewer want to lend, or both. That is a liquidity problem in the banking sector. Because government, companies, and households all borrow from banks, a long rise in the call rate is, for some economists, an **early warning** that a crisis is coming.

## 3. Certificates, papers, and bills (ECO-16-03)

| | Who borrows | Who lends | Tradable | Interest |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Certificate of deposit** | A **bank**, raising short-term funds. In the news, a bank issuing these wants money quickly | The **public**: households and companies | **Yes.** You can sell it before maturity, generally at a somewhat higher price | **Market.** The Reserve Bank **allows** a zero-coupon issue. It is **not compulsory**. It can also be an ordinary bond |
| **Fixed deposit**, the comparison | The bank | You | **Never**, short or long. The bank will not let you transfer it | The interest comes back only to you |
| **Commercial paper** | A **company**. The clearest issuers are **non-banking financial companies**. Not every company qualifies | The public | **Yes** | **Market** |
| **Commercial bill** (also **bill of exchange**, and he also said **promissory note**) | The **buyer** company | The **seller** company | **Yes** | **Market** |

A certificate of deposit is a short fixed deposit that can be sold. That tradability is the difference. Most certificates of deposit are **within a year**. A longer one exists, and is then just a long certificate rather than a fixed deposit. It is the exception, and only in this instrument. When the public is the lender, the maturity is generally **not under about three months**. A person cannot hand money over at a day’s notice. Call loans and the repo window are for that speed. Certificates of deposit are a minimum of about a week, and typically longer.

Commercial paper and commercial bills are **riskier**, because the borrower is a company. Government borrowing is the safest. Bank borrowing is safe enough, because a bank is licensed and regulated by the Reserve Bank. A company needs **prior approval from a commercial bank**, which is acting as a guarantor. A startup can issue a bill only if that reputation is already there. There is no rule that forbids it. Established companies are the usual issuers. The bank’s gain comes later: the paper comes back as security, and the bank earns the discount.

**How a commercial bill is born.** An automobile company needs parts and cannot pay now. It promises the parts company: the money, **with interest**, after some months. That promise is the bill. It is not a typical loan. The seller has not handed over cash. The buyer owes the goods. His maturity in the example is **six months**. The simplest path is that the seller holds the bill and is paid. At maturity, **whoever holds the bill** collects from the buyer.

If the seller needs cash sooner, it takes the bill to the **same bank that approved the issue**. The bank lends against it and deducts the interest **at once**. That rate is the **discount rate**: interest charged **at the time the loan is given**, not afterwards in instalments. His example: a bill of **₹100** at a discount of **3 per cent** puts **₹97** in the seller’s hand. The ₹3 is the bank’s earning. The whole step is **discounting**.

The bank, if it is itself short, takes the bill to the Reserve Bank, which deducts again. The second deduction is the **rediscount rate**, and that rate’s name is the **bank rate**.

**Bank rate** is the rate at which the Reserve Bank is ready to **rediscount commercial bills or commercial papers** brought by the banks. It is therefore a lending rate at which banks borrow from the Reserve Bank **using those bills or papers**. A seller may hold commercial paper as well as a bill, so both can be discounted and then rediscounted.

Textbooks call the bank rate long-term and the repo rate short-term. He rejected that wording inside the money market. **Both are under one year.** The bank rate is only **longer than** the repo rate: repo loans run for **days or weeks**, often **not more than a month**; bank-rate loans run for **a few months**.

The discount rate is market-determined and the bank rate is set by the Reserve Bank, so the Bank can in principle put the bank rate either side of the discount. **Generally the bank rate should be higher**, or rediscounting does not pay the commercial bank.

## 4. Repo and reverse repo (ECO-16-04)

**Repo** is his expansion of **repurchase obligation**. The aim is the same as the bank rate: a bank takes a short-term loan from the Reserve Bank. The security and the mechanism are different. The bank **sells government securities** to the Reserve Bank and **promises to buy them back later at a higher price**. The security can be a Treasury bill or a government bond, short or long. Mostly Treasury bills. There is no hard rule.

The ₹100 in the example is the **loan amount**, the value of the securities at that moment. It may or may not be their face value. Do not call it face value. **Fifteen days** is the maturity he used. “Later” is generally not more than a month, and it is under a year, because this is the money market.

Interest rates are quoted **per annum**. Newspapers often print the percentage and leave out those two words. The repurchase price is:

**100 + (100 × 15/365 × the repo rate)**

The piece in the bracket is the interest. As of this class he put the repo rate at **5.25 per cent**. Two years earlier he put it at **around 6 per cent**. Through the last year, he said, it had barely moved. A lower rate shrinks the bracket, lowers the repurchase price, and makes the Reserve Bank’s loan **cheaper**.

**Repo rate** is the rate that sets that repurchase price. It is a **short-term lending rate** at which banks borrow from the Reserve Bank **using government securities**. It is not a cheque handed over and repaid later. The loan happens by trading the securities.

**Reverse repo** is the same market turned over, so it does not get its own number. A bank in **surplus** **buys** government securities from the Reserve Bank and promises to **resell** them later at a higher price set by the **reverse repo rate**. That rate is a **short-term deposit rate** the banks earn from the Reserve Bank, the way a person earns a deposit rate from a bank.

**The repo rate is always higher than the reverse repo rate.** The gap can change. The order does not. A lending rate between two parties is always above the deposit rate. In the last couple of years he had seen the reverse repo rate stay put even longer than the repo rate. The reason is for the monetary-policy class.

**Bank rate, repo rate, and reverse repo rate are not market-determined.** The Reserve Bank sets them. Every other rate in this sitting was market-determined. Why the Bank moves them is the monetary-policy section.

## 5. What a commercial bank actually does (ECO-16-05)

Two roles, and the second needs the first. A commercial bank **accepts deposits** from the public and pays a **deposit rate**. It **gives loans** to the public and charges a **lending rate**. When those two words are used, he means the bank and the public, not the Reserve Bank’s rates.

The lending rate is **always** above the deposit rate. That gap is the bank’s main profit. His illustration, not a published card rate: a savings deposit at **3 to 4 per cent**, a loan from the same bank at **8 to 9 per cent** or more, easily double.

**Deposits are liabilities. Loans are assets.** A liability is held and does not pay the bank a return. An asset does. For **you**, the same deposit is an asset. The words below are from the **bank’s** side. He allowed rare exceptions and did not name one.

Deposits split into two **concepts**. A bank will not open an account called “demand deposit”.

| | Can it be withdrawn at once? | The account he used |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Demand deposit** | Yes | **Current account**: any amount in, any amount out, return **zero or near zero**. Also the **demand liability of a savings account** |
| **Time deposit** | No, unless some return is sacrificed | **Fixed deposit**: a higher return, and the liquidity is given up. Also the **time liability of a savings account** |

Newspapers call a savings account a demand deposit. He said that is not fully correct. His split of **₹1 lakh**: **₹90,000** can be withdrawn at once, so it is a demand liability. **₹10,000** is the **minimum balance**, or minimum average balance, and is a time liability. The larger share is why the newspapers speak loosely. Draw the minimum down and the bank can charge a **penalty**. Break a fixed deposit early and the higher rate is lost, a penalty may be added, and what is left has become a demand deposit.

**Demand deposits + time deposits = total demand and time liabilities**, the **DTL**. It is the bank’s total deposits.

**Net DTL (NDTL) = total DTL − deposits of that bank with other banks.** Those inter-bank deposits are not kept for a return. They are there for a liquidity problem: the bank can withdraw them. They are an **asset** of the bank that placed them, so they are subtracted. His example is the State Bank of India deducting what it has placed with other banks. What remains is the deposit the bank can actually use to lend.

## 6. Two ratios, and the reserves (ECO-16-06)

**Currency deposit ratio**, of the **public**, mainly individuals:

**currency held ÷ money held in demand deposits**

It is what you withdraw and keep as cash, over what you leave in the account. His example: **₹1 lakh** in a current account, **₹50,000** withdrawn at once, ratio **1**. He called that unrealistic. A realistic ratio is **less than 1**. As payments move online, people hold less cash, and the ratio **tends to fall**. Why the ratio is needed is a later class.

**Reserve deposit ratio**, of a **bank**:

**total reserves ÷ NDTL**

**Reserves** are the part of deposits **not given out as loans**. Most deposits are lent, because that is the profit. His example, which he said is not absurd for India: a ratio of **0.2** means **20 per cent** kept back and **80 per cent** lent. He called the split oversimplified. Banks do more than lend and reserve.

Two kinds of reserve:

| | What it is | Where it sits |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Required reserves** | What the bank is **statutorily** required to keep. This is the basis of the **cash reserve ratio**, not the statutory liquidity ratio. The word statutory does not by itself mean that second ratio | **With the Reserve Bank** |
| **Excess reserves** | **Mostly voluntary**, **in excess of** the required reserves. A **small** part is mandatory, and that part belongs to the statutory liquidity ratio, which is a bigger term and is **not** this class | **With the bank itself** |

**Cash reserve ratio = (required reserves ÷ NDTL) × 100.** It is a percentage because it is cash kept with the Reserve Bank. As of this class it is **roughly 3 per cent**. It was **4 per cent** until the previous year, and the Bank cut it during that year. On every **₹100** of net demand and time liabilities, **₹3** sits with the Reserve Bank and cannot be lent. **Both this ratio and the statutory liquidity ratio are checked every fortnight, 15 days.** The second ratio is still untaught. Excess reserves, he said, are generally around **4 to 5 per cent**, and not all of that is compulsory. It varies by bank.

Excess reserves look like a loss, because the money is not earning. The bank keeps them for a short-term liquidity problem. They are the option that does not have to be asked for, and that charges no interest.

The six ways he listed, and none is a copy of another:

1. Call money.
2. A certificate of deposit.
3. A loan at the **bank rate**.
4. A loan at the **repo rate**.
5. **Inter-bank deposits**.
6. **Excess reserves** — already in hand.

The marginal standing facility is another very short loan from the Reserve Bank. It is in the handout. He left it for monetary policy.

## 7. The Reserve Bank’s balance sheet (ECO-16-07)

A balance sheet is the list of liabilities and assets. For a modern organisation the two sides **match**. Inflows and outflows are his picture of why, and he said the picture is rough.

### Liabilities

1. **Currency in circulation**, the notes with the public. This is the main liability. On the note the Governor promises to pay the bearer that sum. Without the promise the note is paper. The promise is why the note is **legal tender**: legally guaranteed by the Reserve Bank, and **indirectly** by the Government of India, because the government holds the Reserve Bank’s **shares**. He was not opening the ownership debate. Modern notes have **negligible intrinsic value**. Gold and silver coins had their own. He equated **fiat** money with legal tender: fiat means by the order of the government, the same guarantee. **Fiduciary** was accepted in the same answer and not explained.
2. **Banks’ deposits** with the Reserve Bank. The cash reserve ratio is part of this. Banks can keep more than that.
3. **Governments’ deposits**, plural: **Centre and States**.
4. **Other deposits**: anything that is not a bank’s or a government’s. Most of these come from **outside India**. Other central banks, his example the central bank of Japan, and the Reserve Bank keeps deposits with them in return. The **International Monetary Fund** keeps deposits with member central banks. A central bank’s shortage is a **crisis** it is managing, a banking crisis or a balance-of-payments crisis. It is not a shortage caused by lending to households. Do not mix a central bank with a commercial bank.
5. **Other liabilities.** The one in the news is the **dividend** paid to the Government of India, a part of profit, because the shareholder is the government. The practice is **decades** old. It has been in the news because the amount has been rising. He cited **₹2.87 lakh crore**, a few months before this class, as the **highest** dividend the Bank has paid. Where the profit comes from is the asset side. He did not open the argument about whether so much should be paid.

### Assets

1. **Loans.** To **banks**, at the bank rate and the repo rate. To **governments**, Centre and States.
2. **Investments**, domestic and foreign. Domestic: **government securities**, and **other approved securities**. Approved means the Bank clears a non-government security before it buys. The care is not only profit. The assets have to be good enough to **manage a crisis**. Foreign: **foreign government securities** and **foreign currencies**, mainly of countries he listed as developed — the **United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan** — and **China**, which he said is not formally developed but matters. The point of the list is that they are unlikely to be in crisis **together**. If the dollar falls, the euro may hold, then the yen, the yuan, the pound. One layer under another. That selectivity is also why the dividend is large.
3. **Gold**, or gold reserves. When shares and bonds are doing badly, he said the gold price **stays stable**. Another layer.
4. **Other assets.**

**Deficit financing, only as this balance sheet explains it.** Before **1991**, loans from the Reserve Bank to governments kept rising. The government could run any deficit and tell the Bank to finance it. Assets jump, so liabilities must jump, and the main liability is currency, so the Bank prints more. Excess money is inflation. Printing is not itself wrong. It has to stay in line with what is produced. His illustration: goods worth **₹100** and currency worth **₹500**. The practice was **discontinued from 1997**. The monetised-deficit chapter, the committee, and the inflation examples stay the deficits note.

**Ways and Means Advances**, from **1997**, are the loans that continue. He named two differences from deficit financing. The **amount is limited**. The **interest rate is higher**. The **90 days** are the deficits note. He did not repeat them.

### What the sheet is quietly listing

- The Bank **prints and circulates** currency.
- It **manages foreign-exchange reserves**. The currencies above are that stock. The balance-of-payments chapter comes later.
- It is **banker to the governments**, Centre and States, because it takes their deposits and can lend to them.
- It is **banker to the banks**, for the same reason.
- It is the **lender of the last resort**. Normally a bank borrows against securities. In a **severe crisis**, when nobody else will lend, the bank can borrow **even without securities**. A Prelims option list he remembered put banks, government, and companies side by side. The answer was **banks only**. Households and companies **do not deal directly** with the Reserve Bank. It deals with the **government**, **commercial banks**, and **international** bodies such as other central banks and the Fund.

He pointed at a recent Prelims question on the **sources of income** of the Reserve Bank and told the room to read it. Income there means the **assets**. A loan to a company is not one. Currency in circulation is a liability, not an income. Do not reconstruct the stem.
