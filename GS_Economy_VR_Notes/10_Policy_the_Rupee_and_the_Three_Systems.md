## 1. Expansionary policy cheapens the rupee (ECO-17-01)

If expansionary policy runs too long, or too hard, **demand rises faster than production**. A home loan is approved the day it is sanctioned. The flat is not ready. His picture: a flat that was selling at **₹1 crore** goes to **₹1.2 crore or ₹1.5 crore**, and the same mismatch appears across sectors. That is **inflation**: too much money chasing too few goods. The policymakers then drop the expansionary stance and take up the contractionary one. The two stances rotate. Growth without inflation, then inflation without enough growth, then back.

Printing enough notes to hand to everyone would not end poverty. Demand would appear at once. If output did not catch up, prices would wipe the gift out. A central bank prints with inflation in mind, only as much as will not set prices off. Printing causes inflation **when production does not catch up**. If output rises with the new money, inflation need not follow.

What “expansionary monetary and fiscal policy” should mean in an answer, without rebuilding the instruments:

- an **increase in the money supply**, and
- a **reduction in the cost of capital**. Cost of capital is the **interest on the loan**.

Treat the currency as any other commodity. A larger supply pushes the price down. So expansionary policy has a **depreciating impact on the domestic currency**.

The exam trap is the long story. Heavy spending on infrastructure looks good: more factories, fewer imports, more exports, a stronger currency. That may arrive in the **far** future. The question about the currency is about the **very short run**, and the immediate effect of the spending is **depreciation**. The same for a **rate cut**. Do not re-derive each sentence. Bring it down to one test: is the step expansionary or contractionary?

Both **revenue** spending and **capital** spending depreciate the domestic currency. A larger **subsidy**, including a fertiliser subsidy, is government spending. Short run: **depreciating**, not appreciating. He is not predicting that the rupee will in fact fall. Many things move a rate, including what the other currency is doing. He is naming the pressure from **this** step.

## 2. Contractionary policy does the opposite (ECO-17-02)

**Contractionary monetary and fiscal policy** is adopted to **control inflation by curbing demand**.

**The RBI**

- **Prints less**, or stops printing for a while.
- **Raises the cash reserve ratio and the statutory liquidity ratio**, so banks have less to lend. Those ratios were named. He did not set a number.
- Sells government securities in an **open market operation**. The banks pay rupees and are left with less to lend. Buying those securities is the expansionary direction. Selling is this one. The full treatment of open market operations he still placed in the money-and-banking class.
- **Hikes the repo rate.** A hike is the newspaper word for an increase. It is an indication that commercial banks should raise their lending rates.

Credit then becomes **less available** and **less affordable**. Banks get selective. A borrower who was eligible for a **₹1 crore** home loan may be told the limit is **₹50 lakh**. People delay a purchase that is not urgent. The **24-month** laptop instalment from the previous class may disappear.

**Margin**, and he meant it for a **home loan**, not for every loan. On a **₹1 crore** flat a **10 per cent** margin means the buyer brings **₹10 lakh** and the bank lends **₹90 lakh**. If the bank is unwilling, it may lift the margin to **30 per cent**: the buyer must arrange **₹30 lakh**, and the bank lends **₹70 lakh**. Many people who had the ₹10 lakh no longer qualify. A car can be used the same way. In an expansionary mood a bank may finance a **₹10 lakh** car in full. In a contractionary mood it may ask for a **20 per cent** down payment, **₹2 lakh**, and lend **₹8 lakh**.

The rate in the headlines is the **repo rate**. Lending rates and deposit rates **align in the same direction**, sooner or later. A higher lending rate gives the bank room to pay depositors a little more. A cut in the lending rate forces the deposit rate down, because the bank is earning less on loans.

**The government** spends less, including by cutting subsidies, and **raises tax rates**. People are left with less to spend, so demand is curbed.

The formula is the reverse of the first one: **less money, and a higher cost of capital**. That has an **appreciating impact** on the domestic currency. A hike in the policy rate is the clean example.

## 3. A hike abroad can force one at home (ECO-17-03)

A few days before this class, he said, the **US Federal Reserve** had announced a **rate hike**, because inflation was troubling the American economy. A hike is contractionary, so it has an **appreciating** impact on the **dollar**. The **Bank of Japan** had announced a hike after that. If most large central banks hike and India does not, the rupee falls against those currencies as well as against the dollar.

He set India’s own inflation, for this discussion, inside a **comfortable range**. No number. Left to itself, the RBI might not hike, and might even cut, because a hike hampers growth. The last **one to one and a half years** have made the **falling rupee** the parameter that is not comfortable. Growth, he said, has been good, about **6 per cent or more**, and he cited a new print of **7.8**. External debt, as a share of GDP, he called comfortable, unlike the United States, whose external debt he put at **38 trillion dollars**. No Indian debt ratio was given.

If the Fed hikes and the RBI **cuts**, the two currencies move in opposite directions and the rupee can fall **further and sharply**. If India merely **holds the rate**, the dollar is still in a contractionary stance and the rupee is not, so the rupee is still under depreciating pressure. A cut, in his reading of the upcoming meeting of the **Monetary Policy Committee**, had **very little chance**. The live choice was to **hold**, or to **hike** because others had hiked and the rupee needed the support. Growth and inflation are not the only two reasons for a rate decision. The currency can be the third.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** a rate hike whose purpose is inflation is not the same act as the RBI selling dollars to stop a fall. The first is monetary policy. The second is a direct intervention in the rate. Section 4.</span>

## 4. Three ways to set the rate (ECO-17-04)

Who decides that **1 dollar equals 96 rupees**? Three systems. A country chooses voluntarily, according to what suits it. Advanced open economies — the **United States, European countries, the United Kingdom** — use a **free float**, also called a **flexible** rate. Emerging economies such as India use a **managed float**. **Middle Eastern** oil economies — he named the **UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar** — use a **fixed** rate. The only parity he dictated is the dirham: **1 US dollar = 3.67 UAE dirham (AED)**. Do not invent the riyal or the Qatari riyal.

| System | Who sets the price | Example |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Free float / flexible** | **Only the market**, demand and supply. The price changes every moment. The government and the central bank **do not intervene** in that price | United States |
| **Fixed / pegged** | The **government** pegs the currency to a **fixed parity**. Pegging means fixing. The market is not allowed to move it | UAE, 3.67 |
| **Managed float**, also called a **dirty float** | **Largely** the market, but the central bank **may intervene** if the move is large, either way | India |

**Dirty** is the insult from countries that float freely. They mean the currency is not purely floating, and they call the intervention a **manipulation**. India calls the same act a defence of the rupee.

A monetary-policy step is not that intervention. The Fed’s job, as he put it, is growth and inflation. A hike may lift the dollar. That is a side effect. The Fed has not been told to step in and hold the dollar up or down. **Direct** intervention is a step taken **for the rate**: the rupee is falling, so the RBI **sells dollars from the reserves**. That mechanism is already Lecture 1 of this chapter.

**Free float — advantage.** Large foreign-exchange reserves are **not required**, so that money is free to move across borders as investment. He called this the **international mobility of liquidity**, and he tied it to global growth. The currency also becomes a **trading commodity**. The price moves every moment, so there are buyers and sellers: a **bullish** view buys, a **bearish** view sells. With little official intervention, the **dollar, the euro, and the pound** are treated as **safe-haven** assets, along with gold, when equity markets are uncertain.

**Free float — disadvantage.** With nobody to step in, the currency is **prone to volatility**, and volatility is uncertainty. Emerging economies will not take the system in full for that reason. India already floats inside a broad range. The fear is a crash.

Why can the United States live with that fear? Some assets are usually in such high demand that a fall brings **fresh buying**, so they correct and do not crash. His picture, not a price series to memorise: gold went from about **₹70,000 per 10 grams** to **₹1,50,000–1,60,000**, peaked around **January this year**, and was correcting. He does not expect it back at ₹70,000, because buyers step in on the way down. Silver has the same precious-metal demand and is also an **industrial** metal, in solar panels and semiconductors. A strong company, his example **Reliance**, can fall 30 or 50 per cent and still find buyers. A firm on the way to bankruptcy does not: he named **JP**, which built the **Yamuna Expressway** and later failed, and **Unitech**. High US bond yields pull money out of equity and out of gold, because gold **pays no interest**. He also stated a **negative** link: when the **dollar appreciates, gold tends to fall**, because gold is priced in dollars. Central banks, he named the **RBI, China, and Turkey**, have been buying gold.

The same confidence attaches to the dollar, the euro, and the pound. They may correct. He does not expect **1 dollar = 96 rupees** to become **1 dollar = 40 or 30**. A rupee at 96 **can** go to **200 or 300** if the fall is not met, because the rupee is not a preferred global currency. **Internationalisation of the rupee is very low.** That is why the managed float is the suitable system: the RBI must be able to step in, in time.

## 5. Defending a peg, and what the reserves are for (ECO-17-05)

Announcing **1 dollar = 1 rupee** is easy. **Defending** the parity is the hard part, because the market keeps pushing it. The UAE is an export economy and, in recent years, an investment destination, so dollars arrive and the dirham wants to **appreciate**. They cannot allow it. The central bank **prints more dirhams** and holds 3.67. If the dirham instead wants to **depreciate**, the central bank **sells dollars from the reserves**, the same act India uses when the rupee falls. Inflation from the printing is possible, not automatic. It arrives only if production does not catch up, and they can fight it later.

India could not live on that system. The UAE’s usual pressure is appreciation, and printing one’s **own** currency has no reserve limit. India is a **trade-deficit** country, so the usual pressure is depreciation, and the defence is to **sell dollars continuously**. Those dollars are finite and can be exhausted. A peg set near the market, his illustration **1 dollar = 96 rupees**, could be held for some time. A peg far from the market, his illustration **1 dollar = 60 rupees**, would empty the reserves quickly.

**Fixed — advantage.** Foreign investors want stability, including **currency** stability. Traders want it too. His investor: **1 dollar** arrives when the rate is **₹80**, so ₹80 is what is actually invested. If the rupee then goes to **₹160**, the exit converts back to **half a dollar**. Half the capital is gone even if the Indian market did well. A fall from **₹80 to ₹100** wipes about **20 per cent**. Investors will not wait for 160. They leave in between, the leaving itself weakens the rupee, and more of them leave. That is why the RBI shows both the **intent** and the **reserves**. A crude contract signed at **₹80** to the dollar, and paid three months later at **₹100**, costs an extra **₹20** on a one-dollar parcel. The UAE trader does not face that revision. A second advantage, taken up in the next section, is that the government can **devalue on purpose** to make exports competitive.

**Fixed — disadvantage.** The government or the central bank must hold **huge** reserves to defend the parity.

**Managed float — advantage.** Less volatility than a free float.

**Managed float — disadvantage.** Large reserves, but he refused the word **huge**. Large enough to manage the currency. The two disadvantages are the same kind of burden. The fixed system’s word is **huge**. The managed system’s word is **large**.

**Reserves he quoted.** India maintains **more than 700 billion dollars**, and he placed India in the **top five**. **China** holds the largest reserves, **more than 3 trillion dollars**. China used to fix the rate and has been moving toward a **managed float**. Countries that float freely need not hold stocks of that size. He said American reserves would be **smaller than India’s**, and he put the United Kingdom around **200 to 300 billion dollars**. Those last two are his comparisons, not a table he dictated. The money sitting in the Chinese and Indian reserves is, in his line, capital that is **not** free to move as investment. He slipped once and said seven billion for India. The figure he was using is **more than 700 billion**.

## 6. Depreciation is the market. Devaluation is the government (ECO-17-06)

In a **free float** and in a **managed float**, a fall that the market produces is **depreciation**. The rise is **appreciation**. Exports become cheaper and imports costlier when the currency falls. The rise does the opposite.

In a **fixed** system the market cannot move the parity. Only the **government** can, and the act is deliberate. A fall is **devaluation**. The rise, on the board beside it, is **revaluation**. He did not spend a separate example on revaluation.

The dirham illustration: **3.67 to 3.8** to the dollar is a devaluation, not a depreciation. They would print dirhams until the new parity is the one they then announce. The purpose is the same as a market fall: **exports cheaper, imports costlier**. When the **rupee** falls, the word is **depreciation**. When a **pegged** currency is moved down by its government, the word is **devaluation**.

## 7. Open and closed are a matter of degree (ECO-17-07)

A **closed economy**, in the ideal line, has **no** economic transactions with the outside world: no imports, no exports, no cross-border capital, no international loans. It would be **self-sufficient**. In the modern world that ideal does not exist. Every country must import some **essential** items: **crude oil, foodgrains, medicines**, and also machines and technology. The practical meaning is **minimal** transactions, only what is essential.

**India was a closed economy till 1991.** Import duties were **100 per cent or more**, and many imports were banned. Foreign investment was allowed to a **handful** of companies. What came in was largely essential: crude oil, foodgrains. In **1991** the **Liberalisation, Privatisation and Globalisation (LPG)** reforms removed most of those restrictions, and India became an open economy. He was asked whether 1990 was completely closed. **Partially.** No modern economy is completely closed, and the word “closed” already means that. Do not add “partially” in the answer.

An **open economy** transacts **freely**, which means **without restrictions**. That too is an ideal. In practice some restrictions remain, but they are light beside a closed economy’s. A closed-economy duty, in his contrast, is 100 per cent or higher. An open economy still has some duty. He offered **3 per cent, 5 per cent, 10 per cent** as the light range, not as India’s tariff schedule.

Neither pole is occupied. The useful comparison is relative.

- **Singapore** is more open than India, because the restrictions there are lighter.
- The **United States today** is less open than it was **10 or 15 years** ago. He tied that to the tariffs and the visa restrictions under President Trump.
- **North Korea** comes closest to closed. Little investment goes in. The trade that exists is with **China or Russia**.
- **Iran** has been made less open by **sanctions**.

Adding restrictions over time means becoming less open. Removing them means becoming more open. **Barriers to trade** is the name for those restrictions, and it is the next class.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** expansionary depreciates, contractionary appreciates, and both judgments are **short-run pressures**, not forecasts. Depreciation is a market fall under a float. Devaluation is a government moving a peg. India’s reserves are **more than $700 billion**. China’s are **more than $3 trillion**. The dirham parity is **3.67**, not a rupee number. India was closed **till 1991**, not completely closed.</span>
