## 1. Savers (ECO-18-01)

With a rising inflation rate, **savers lose**, because the **real return** falls.

**Real return** (for a deposit, the **real interest rate**) **= nominal return − inflation rate**. The subtraction is not only for interest. Any promised return has to be read after inflation has been taken out.

His deposit. Someone puts **₹1 lakh** in a fixed deposit at **7%**. That 7% is **nominal**: the rupee balance grows at 7%. If inflation that year is **5%**, the purchasing power of the balance has been pulled down by 5, and the real return is **2%**. If inflation is **10%**, the real return is **−3%**. He said this second case was India’s position till about last year: a low nominal return, inflation still positive, and in real terms a pittance. He also once said ₹1,000 becomes ₹1,700. That is not 7%. The lock is **₹1 lakh at 7%**.

Bank deposits and the financial market compete for the same saving. If shares or bonds are promising a real return the deposit cannot match, money leaves the bank. That, in his telling, is what pushed people into a **systematic investment plan (SIP)** from the COVID years until about last year. He said “specialised investment fund” once and then corrected it. In the **last one year** the stock market’s **nominal** return itself went negative. He put that loss at about **minus 3%**, before any inflation adjustment. The promise, he said, had not held for the last **four or five years**.

## 2. Fixed incomes, and the poor (ECO-18-02)

A **fixed** income — a salary that is not revised, a pension that is not revised — loses real value as prices rise. The quality of life falls unless something compensates.

Serving **government** staff get that compensation as **dearness allowance (DA)**. Retired government staff get **dearness relief (DR)**. The audio said “DNA” and “de-earnance relief.” Both are paid **twice a year**. The **informal** sector does not get this. In the **private formal** sector a revision may come, and usually only after a year, and not every time.

**Poor people are hurt more than rich people.** Both pay the higher price of rice, wheat, sugar, vegetables and oil. For a person already at the margin it becomes a question of getting by. For a rich person the same rise is muted. His illustration, which he called a bit arbitrary and still true: **₹10,000 a month** against **₹5 lakh a month**. The poorer household can be spending about **₹8,000** of that ₹10,000 on food and essentials, about **80%**. Even if the richer household buys the best of those same things, he put the ceiling near **₹1 lakh**, about **20%** of ₹5 lakh. Raise the price of food, and the first household is in trouble. The second is not.

That ratio is **Engel’s law**, given by **Ernst Engel**. He spelt it **E-N-G-E-L**. As income rises, the **percentage** of income spent on food falls. Not the rupee amount. The geography note has the same law with a different pair of incomes. These percentages belong to this class.

The same relative hit covers three pairs he had already written down. **Labour** is hurt more than the **capitalist**. The **wage earner** more than the **profit earner**. The **employee** more than the **employer**. No one is immune. The question is who is hurt more.

His factory. The workers’ wage is **₹10,000** a month. The pen sells at **₹100**. Inflation is **10%**, and the pen goes to **₹110**. **Profit = revenue − cost.** If the same number of pens is sold at a price 10% higher, nominal profit is up by about 10%. The capitalist’s real income is protected by that rise. The worker faces the same 10% in the market, and comes back asking for the wage to rise. The owner will give something. He will not give the full 10%, because the wage is a **cost** on his side and a **liability**, not a living claim he feels bound to match. A rise of **5, 6 or 7**, and not 10, still leaves the worker short in real terms. The owner decides his own compensation and the worker’s. The first will be the larger.

## 3. Bondholders, stockholders, and exports (ECO-18-03)

**Bondholders** are hurt, because the **real value of the promised coupon falls**.

A bond, for this hour only, is a paper with promises, used by a **company** or a **government** to borrow. A firm that wants to expand and does not have the money has three broad doors: a **bank**, the **share** market (sell a part of the ownership), or a **bond**. He will not teach the bond market here. Three promises sit on the paper: how much is borrowed (the **principal**), what return is promised, and when the principal comes back.

His company needs **₹1,000 crore**. It promises **10%**, which is **₹100 crore a year for 10 years**, and the ₹1,000 crore back at the end. Share-market returns are **not** promised. A bond’s return, in the ordinary case, **is** fixed, and the principal is generally safe. Exceptions exist. If an examination does not name a special bond, assume the return is fixed. Inflation does not change the rupees of that coupon. It changes what those rupees buy. That is the adverse hit.

**Stockholders** are also hurt. **Generally**, the **short-term** investor is hurt more than the **long-term** investor. He insisted on “generally.” A short-term investor can make a quick profit, and a long-term investor can make a steady loss. He said the Indian market has been giving a **negative** return for **two or three years**, and that people who started a systematic plan two or three years ago have been the unlucky lot. Mutual-fund advertisements that start from a boom year, he said, are picking the decade that flatters them.

**Export competitiveness is ambiguous.** Two channels, and they pull opposite ways. The net depends on which is stronger, and he said he was not interested in the arithmetic.

- **Exchange-rate channel.** Higher inflation eats the purchasing power of the rupee. A holder of currencies, which are assets in the foreign-exchange market, prefers the currency whose purchasing power stays stable. Demand for the rupee falls, the rupee **depreciates**, and a cheaper rupee can **raise** exports. Through this channel, inflation can help.
- **Cost channel.** Inputs become costlier, so the exported good itself becomes costlier. India and China both sell a pen at **$1**, and both have inflation at **4%**. India’s rate moves to **10%**. Wages and inputs rise, and the Indian pen can no longer be offered at $1. He called the new price a rise of about **10%**, toward **$1.10**, and once said **$1.21**. Decimals matter in a large trade. Through this channel, inflation hurts. The domestic price is only converted into the foreign currency. If it has become costlier at home, it is costlier after the conversion.

Depreciation making exports more competitive is the exchange-rate class. He used it. He did not re-teach the rate.

## 4. Bracket creep (ECO-18-04)

Taxpayers lose purchasing power like everyone else. A **certain** class of them is hurt again, by **bracket creep**.

Inflation, or a rise in pay that only compensates for inflation, can move a person **slowly** from a **lower income-tax bracket** into a **higher** one. The higher rate means **more tax**, and the person can end up **worse off** even though the nominal income is higher. It bites only when the income is **close to the threshold**. Someone sitting well below it is not crept over.

His teaching sketch, which he told them to treat as an assumption. Threshold **₹1 lakh**. Below it, tax at **0%**. On the income **above** ₹1 lakh, **10%**. The new rate is not laid on the income from the first rupee. It is on the **additional** income, which is the slab idea from the tax class. The person earns exactly ₹1 lakh and pays nothing. Inflation is 10%. The employer raises the pay by 10%, to **₹1.1 lakh**, which fully compensates the prices. Tax now falls on the extra **₹10,000**. More tax, and no real gain.

The live parallel, from the **new income-tax regime**. Income **under ₹12 lakh** pays no tax. A taxable income of **₹11.8 lakh** pays nothing. Next year the employer moves it from **₹11.8 lakh to ₹12.25 lakh**. Tax appears. He also said “one rupee over ₹10 lakh.” The threshold he was using is **₹12 lakh**. He did not dictate the slab percentages. The mumbled rates did not land.

**Fiscal drag** stays the name from Lecture 2. He did not open it.

## 5. Where along the chain the index sits (ECO-18-05)

Four measures. Each answers a different question.

| Index | The question |
|:---|:---|
| **Producer Price Index (PPI)** | How prices are moving at the **production** point. India has **three**: output, input, and services |
| **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)** | How prices are moving at the **wholesale** point |
| **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** | How prices are moving for the **final consumer** |
| **GDP deflator** | An **overall** picture, not tied to one point in the chain. He called it the comprehensive measure. **What it is, and how the producer index changes the GDP estimate, is the next class** |

The one that matters for policy is the **CPI**. Monetary policy is aimed at keeping **CPI combined** inside **4% ± 2**. Not the wholesale index, not the producer index, not the deflator. When a newspaper or the Reserve Bank of India says “inflation” without a surname, the default in this regime is **CPI combined**, which is also called **all-India CPI**.

India has worked under an **inflation-targeting** regime since **2016**. The fuller name is the **flexible inflation-targeting (FIT)** regime. Flexible, because the target is a **band**, not a single point of 4%. CPI combined has been the target since that adoption. The legal mandate, and why 2% and 6% are the edges, stay Lecture 2.

## 6. The consumer index (ECO-18-06)

The CPI measures inflation at the **consumption** level. The basket has **both goods and services**.

India publishes more than one. The baskets are not identical, because a factory worker, a farm labourer and a rural household do not buy the same things.

- **CPI rural** and **CPI urban**, every month, from the **National Statistical Office (NSO)**. The office sits in the ministry he called the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The audio said “Programming” and “MOSB.”
- **CPI for industrial workers**, **CPI for agricultural labourers**, and **CPI for rural labourers**, from the **Labour Bureau**, in the **Ministry of Labour and Employment**. He said these three matter less. Know that they exist.
- **CPI combined**, or **CPI-C**, is rural and urban put together. This is the target.

**CPI combined** has a **base year of 2024**. It is released **every month** by the NSO. The basket has **358** items, **308 goods** and **50 services**, in **12** categories, on the **UN COICOP 2018** standards. He did not expand those letters. The sheet writes **2010**. The year he dictated is **2018**.

The twelve, in his order: **food and beverages**; **pan, tobacco and intoxicants**; **clothing and footwear**; **housing, water, electricity, fuel and gases** (CNG, PNG, LPG); **furnishing and household maintenance**; **health**; **education**; **transport**; **information and communication**; **restaurants and accommodation**; **recreation, sports and culture**; **personal care, social protection and miscellaneous**.

He does not want every weight memorised. Two he did want. **Food and beverages** is the heaviest head, at **36.75%**. More than a quarter of the basket, in his phrase. **Till last year** that weight was **45.86%**, almost half. The cut, he said, may be because incomes have risen, so the share spent on food has fallen, which is Engel’s law showing up in the basket. The old basket had **299** items: **259 goods** and **40 services**. Ten more services, and the goods count up to 308, make the 358. **CNG**, **PNG**, and **jewellery** prices, including precious metals, are in from this year.

Inflation on the **full** basket is CPI combined. It is also called **headline** inflation, and in the papers **retail** inflation, because it hits the consumer at the last point.

**Core** inflation is the long-run pattern, after the short-run noise is taken out. In India that noise is **food** and **fuel**. Food moves with the season, because almost **half** of what he called the edible land depends on rainfall. Fuel moves within a day. About **90%** of crude is imported, which matches Lecture 1. Edible oil he put at about **60%** imported today. Lecture 1 had said about **56%**. Core inflation **= headline − food − fuel**. It can also be called **non-food, non-fuel** inflation. The government publishes the headline, rural, urban and combined. On whether it also publishes core, he said both things: that **from this year** a core number has been released, and that the government does **not** release core officially and the newspapers compute it by dropping those heads. There is no single international definition. What counts as short-run noise changes by country.

**Super-core** goes one step further and also drops **precious metals and jewellery** (gold, platinum, silver, and the jewellery made from them). In the United States a still narrower cut is sometimes called super-duper core. For this class, hold headline, core, and super-core. The government does not publish that last one.

The CPI number comes on the **12th** of the month, at **4 pm**, if the 12th is a working day, otherwise the next working day. The wholesale number comes on the **14th**, in the evening. The stock market closes by **3:30**. The late hour is so the print does not hit prices while the market is still open. It hits the next day anyway. The 13th is the newspaper’s front page. The 14th can be the editorial.

## 7. The wholesale index (ECO-18-07)

The WPI measures inflation at the **wholesale** level. The basket has **957 items**, **goods only, not services**. The base year is **2022–23**. The previous base was **2011–12**. It is released **every month** by the **Office of the Economic Adviser**, in the **Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT)**, in the **Ministry of Commerce**.

The 957 items sit in three groups.

1. **Primary** articles, extracted from nature. Food (agriculture) and non-food (coal, iron ore, bauxite).
2. **Fuel and power**.
3. **Manufactured** articles, again food (a processed food) and non-food (a table, clothing). The heaviest weight is here, **around 64%**, about two-thirds.

So a jump in **food** prices moves the **CPI** more. A jump in **manufactured** prices moves the **WPI** more. That is the use of the weights.

## 8. The producer index, still unfinished (ECO-18-08)

The PPI measures inflation at the **producer** level. Three of them.

- **Output PPI.** The prices the producer **receives**. Measured at **basic price**, which is what actually reaches the producer’s pocket. Taxes that go to the government are not in it. Released in **final** form, **every month**.
- **Input PPI.** How costly it is becoming **to produce**: the rise in raw-material cost. Measured at the **purchaser’s price**. When the producer buys plastic, chips or packing, he is a buyer and faces the market price. Still at an **experimental** stage. For now it covers **manufacturing** inputs only. Primary inputs are left out, because those producers are harder to get answers from. **Every month**.
- **Services PPI.** The same question for a **service provider**, again at basic price, not at what the consumer pays. An airline ticket whose basic price is **₹5,000** may cost the passenger more once taxes and subsidies are added. What the airline keeps is the ₹5,000. Released **quarterly**. Only **seven** services. He named **banking, telecom, aviation, insurance, pensions, and securities transactions**, and said the count is seven. The seventh name did not land. The sheet’s list (bakery, dairy, petrol, dressing) does not match the speech. Do not memorise the sheet’s list.

The same office releases these: the **Office of the Economic Adviser**, DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce.

**From 2031** the producer index is **expected** to **replace** the wholesale index. He said the sheet’s “2030” as **2031**, and once slipped to “2021.” From **2026 to 2031** both will be published. After that, the wholesale series is expected to stop, leaving the consumer index and the producer index. He added that governments announce this kind of date and then do not keep it, which is why he said “expected.” Broadly, a country needs two pictures: prices for the producer, and prices for the consumer. India has had a worked-out consumer index for about a decade. It did not have a matching producer index, and it used the **WPI as a proxy**. That proxy is a bad one, for two reasons. It **omits services**, in an economy where **around 55%** of GDP, and more than half, comes from services. And it is measured at the **wholesale** point, after transport and trade margins, so it is not the production point. The two cannot be treated as the same.

The path he dictated. The **United Nations System of National Accounts, 2008**, recommended that countries measure producer-level inflation with a PPI. India set up a committee in **2014** under the statistician **B.N. Goldar**. The audio said “B and Goldar.” The committee reported in **2017**. The government sat on it. Implementation is from **2026**. He said the series has been out only **since June**. It is too new to retire the wholesale index at once, so the two run together for about five years. Producers will not freely share **input** costs, because the gap between input cost and the basic price they receive is their **margin**. That reluctance is one reason the index was late, and why the input series stays experimental. The output series can be finished, because the basic price is already known to the tax system.

The price ladder, which he tied back to the national-income class. **Factor cost**, plus **net production taxes** (production taxes minus production subsidies), is the **basic price**. The basic price, plus **net product taxes** (product taxes minus product subsidies), is the **market price**. The CPI uses the **market** price. The output PPI uses the **basic** price. His rupee sketch (factor cost 100, a production subsidy of 10, a production tax of 5, then a product tax and a product subsidy) did not land cleanly at the last step. Hold the definitions, not a final rupee.

He also said the new index improves **GDP** estimates, because it improves the **deflator**, and that the government has started using it as a deflator, including a **double deflator**, in answer to people who would not accept a recent GDP print. He did not give that print. **How** the deflator works is the next class, with the measures that control inflation, and the Phillips curve. Then the trade-agreements chapter.

## Locks

- Real return **= nominal − inflation**. ₹1 lakh at **7%**: inflation **5%** leaves **2%**; inflation **10%** leaves **−3%**. Last year’s stock-market nominal return, in his line, about **−3%**.
- **DA** and **DR**, twice a year, for government staff and pensioners. Engel: the **percentage** spent on food falls as income rises. ₹10,000 with about **80%** on food, against ₹5 lakh with about **20%**. A **10%** rise in the pen’s price does not become a **10%** rise in the wage.
- Bond: **₹1,000 crore**, coupon **10%**, so **₹100 crore a year for 10 years**, principal back. The coupon’s **real** value falls. Short-term stockholders are **generally** hurt more than long-term ones. Export competitiveness is **ambiguous**: depreciation can help, costlier inputs can hurt.
- Bracket creep needs you **near the threshold**. New regime: **under ₹12 lakh**, no tax. **₹11.8 lakh** raised to **₹12.25 lakh** starts the tax. More tax, and still worse off. Fiscal drag was not opened.
- Since **2016**, **flexible** inflation targeting. The band is **CPI combined**, not WPI or PPI. Base year **2024**. **358** items, **308** goods and **50** services, **12** heads, **COICOP 2018** (the sheet says 2010). Food **36.75%**, was **45.86%**. Old basket **299**. CPI on the **12th at 4 pm**. WPI on the **14th**.
- Headline = the full basket. Core = headline **minus food and fuel**. Super-core also drops **precious metals and jewellery**. Rural and urban from the **NSO**. The three labour indices from the **Labour Bureau**.
- WPI: **957 goods**, no services, base **2022–23** (was **2011–12**), **Office of the Economic Adviser**. Manufactures about **64%**.
- PPI replaces WPI **in expectation from 2031** (the sheet says 2030). Both run **2026 to 2031**. Output is final, at **basic price**, monthly. Input is **experimental**, purchaser’s price, manufacturing inputs only. Services PPI is **quarterly**, **seven** services. **UN SNA 2008**. **B.N. Goldar**, set up **2014**, reported **2017**, in use from **2026**, series out **since June**. Services are **around 55%** of GDP. The deflator, double deflation, the control measures, and the Phillips curve are the next class.
