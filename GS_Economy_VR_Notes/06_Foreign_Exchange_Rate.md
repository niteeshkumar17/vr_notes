## 1. The rate is a price, and it is relative (ECO-11-01)

**Foreign exchange** = **foreign currency**. For India: dollar, euro, pound, yen, yuan.

**Foreign exchange rate** = the **price or value of one currency in terms of some other country’s currency**. Any pair works: rupee–pound, rupee–yen, dollar–euro.

The expression **1 USD = INR 95** is the rupee–dollar rate. It is the price of one dollar in rupees, and equally the price of the rupee in dollars. On **20 September 2026** he put the live rate at about **₹95 to ₹96** per dollar. The **100 → 80** and **80 → 100** lines below are the worked examples, not that day’s rate.

The rate is not stuck. A change is **appreciation** or **depreciation**. The same movement is also called **strengthening** or **weakening**.

**Appreciation** = an **increase** in the price or value of a currency **relative to some other currency**.

Worked example: **1 USD = INR 100**, then **1 USD = INR 80**. Fewer rupees buy one dollar, so the **rupee has appreciated against the dollar**. It has strengthened. The value has gone up from its previous level.

**Depreciation** = a **decrease** in that relative price.

Worked example, the reverse: **1 USD = INR 80**, then **1 USD = INR 100**. More rupees are needed to buy one dollar. The rupee has fallen against the dollar.

It is a **relative** idea, not an absolute one. “The rupee has appreciated” is an incomplete sentence until you name the other currency. The rupee can appreciate against the dollar and depreciate against the euro in the same period. Appreciation of the rupee against the dollar **is** depreciation of the dollar against the rupee.

## 2. What makes the rupee appreciate (ECO-11-02)

**Thumb rule.** Any event that brings foreign exchange **in** has an **appreciating** tendency on the domestic currency. Any event that takes foreign exchange **out** has a **depreciating** tendency. “Dollar” in the lecture stands for any foreign currency.

What brings dollars in: **exports** of goods and of services (inbound tourism is an export of tourism services), **FDI**, **FPI**, **donations**, **remittances**, **external borrowings**.

- **FDI** = investment in another country in order to establish a **business interest** (a factory, manufacturing, or a service). Examples he named: Suzuki, Hyundai making cars, Vodafone, Samsung manufacturing electronics.
- **FPI** = investment in another country’s **financial assets**, shares and bonds. He also called them Foreign Portfolio Investors. In the outflow list he said **FII** in the same breath. Do not build a second definition.
- **Remittances** = money sent home by Indian migrant workers.

Dollars cannot be spent inside India. The exporter, the investor and the family receiving a remittance ask a bank to **convert dollars into rupees**. A large inflow therefore creates a large **demand for rupees**. If the supply of rupees is not enough to meet it — the RBI had printed for ordinary needs, not for this surge — the rupee behaves like any scarce commodity. Its price rises.

On the ground, with the rate at ₹100 per dollar: the person who needs rupees badly will accept **99**, then others **97**, **95**, **90**. Fewer rupees per dollar **is** appreciation.

An external loan appreciates the rupee **when the dollars arrive**. Interest and repayment later are an outflow, and that later moment depreciates it.

Four lines they can jumble in Prelims. All four are the same story. Do not memorise them as a separate list:

1. **Decrease** in the supply of the **domestic** currency.
2. **Increase** in the supply of the **foreign** currency.
3. **Decrease** in the demand for the **foreign** currency.
4. **Increase** in the demand for the **domestic** currency.

An increased supply of dollars is the same fact as a relative fall in the supply of rupees. Dollar depreciation against the rupee is an increased supply of dollars, or a reduced demand for dollars.

If the supply of rupees **is** adequate, the inflow does **not** appreciate the rupee. The lecture’s case is the one where supply is limited.

## 3. What appreciation does (ECO-11-03)

When the domestic currency appreciates:

- **Imports become cheaper**, so the change **encourages imports**.
- **Exports become costlier**, so they are **less competitive** in global markets, and the change **discourages exports**.

Both lines cover **goods and services**. Services he named on the export side: tourism in India, banking, insurance, software, information technology, business process outsourcing. For an Indian, foreign travel and foreign education become **cheaper**, because fewer rupees buy a dollar-priced course or ticket.

Worked import: a one-dollar good. At ₹100 you pay ₹100. At ₹80 you pay ₹80.

Worked export, the rate turned around. At ₹100 per dollar, **1 INR = 0.01 USD**. At ₹80 per dollar, **1 INR = 0.0125 USD** (he divided 1 by 80). The rupee price of the Indian good has not changed. The dollar price has. A foreign buyer who can still get the Chinese good at 0.01 dollar will not pay 0.0125 dollar for the Indian one.

**Foreign tourists are discouraged**, if price is the only consideration. At ₹100 they had ₹100 to spend per dollar. At ₹80 they have ₹80. A hotel tariff unchanged in rupees costs them more dollars. They may go to Sri Lanka instead. Inbound tourism is an **export** of a service, and exports have become costlier.

**Import bill.** Students said appreciation would **reduce** the bill. His answer is **can’t say**. Macroeconomics talks about **tendencies**, not certain outcomes, because other variables are in the room. Cheaper imports create a **tendency** to import more. They do not guarantee a larger bill, and they do not guarantee a smaller one. Same quantity at a lower price cuts the bill. A much larger quantity can raise it. A recession, weak domestic demand, or a better domestic alternative can cancel the tendency. If a stem says **may**, or asks for the tendency, mark it. If it says the bill **will** rise or **will** fall, do not.

The same caution on exports. They can still hold up if your currency rose **2%** against the dollar while competitors rose **5%** and **7%** (his illustration), or if the product is hard to replace (semiconductor chips, crude oil, natural gas). Exporters still **prefer a depreciated currency**. That is why complaints are made that **China** artificially **devalues / depreciates** its currency: China is an exporting nation, and a cheaper currency makes its exports more competitive. Other countries object. No year was given.

Because India is a large importer, cheaper imports after an appreciation also tend to **help control inflation**. That is a tendency, from the same import line.

## 4. What makes the rupee depreciate, and what that does (ECO-11-04)

Mirror of section 2. A **massive outflow** of dollars.

What takes dollars out: **imports rising**, **outgoing FDI and FPI** (he also said FII), **repayment of external loans**, and **interest** on those loans.

The rupee is not the preferred medium for international transactions. An importer (his picture: Reliance buying crude) asks the bank to turn rupees into dollars. An investor who is leaving first sells the shares, bonds or other assets, holds rupees, then asks for dollars. So an outflow is a **rise in the demand for dollars**. If dollars in the system are limited, people offer more rupees: **82**, then **85**, then **90**, against a rate that had been ₹80. That is depreciation of the rupee, which is appreciation of the dollar.

Four jumble-lines, again one story:

1. **Increase** in the supply of the **domestic** currency.
2. **Decrease** in the supply of **foreign exchange**.
3. **Decrease** in the demand for the **domestic** currency.
4. **Increase** in the demand for **foreign** currency.

**Effect**, the reverse of section 3, and again only a tendency:

- Imports of goods and services become **costlier** → **discourages** imports. A one-dollar good that cost ₹80 now costs ₹100.
- Exports become **cheaper** and more competitive → **encourages** exports. Foreigners spend fewer dollars on a rupee-priced good, because the dollar has appreciated against the rupee.
- A cheaper export is not a guarantee of higher sales. Inferior quality can still lose.

India cannot adopt the dollar as the domestic currency. Monetary policy would pass to the **US Federal Reserve**. The RBI would no longer print the rupee or move the interest rate.

## 5. Neither sharp move is good (ECO-11-05)

There is no outright answer to “is appreciation good or depreciation good?” The economy is a mix of sectors with different interests. His comparison: a higher **Minimum Support Price (MSP)** helps the farmer and hurts the consumer who pays more for foodgrains. Do not turn that into a new MSP note.

- **Import-dependent** sectors (raw material, components, fuel) prefer an **appreciated** currency. Cheaper imports lower their cost of production.
- **Export-oriented** sectors prefer **depreciation**.
- A sector that buys and sells only at home is largely **unaffected**.

For the economy as a whole it depends on the type. An export economy such as **China** prefers a depreciated or devalued currency. **India is a trade-deficit economy**: we import much more than we export. Depreciation makes those imports still costlier and **may** add to inflation. A student said depreciation is needed to cut the deficit because exports would rise. He refused the guarantee. Exports may rise. They may not rise by more than the extra import bill.

What can be said with certainty: **neither a large-scale appreciation nor a large-scale depreciation is good for anybody.** A sharp fall devastates import-dependent industry. A sharp rise makes exports suddenly uncompetitive.

A **gradual** move does not create panic. It gets **factored in**: business, trade and investment decisions are already taken on the assumption that the rupee is on that path. His sketch of the gradual fall: from about **₹50** a dollar, through the **₹60s, ₹70s and ₹80s**, into the **₹90s**, and now about **₹95–96**. Hypothetical, not a forecast: if the market expects **₹100** per dollar in the next **11–12 months**, crossing it on that schedule is ugly but not a panic. If the same level arrives in **two or three months**, calculations break and the market panics, because nobody had factored it in.

What is preferred is **stability**. Stable does not mean stuck at one number. It means no large fluctuations.

**Why the dollar is acceptable**, which is a different question from whether it is “strong” in value. Some currencies are priced higher than the dollar. The dollar is powerful because **nobody refuses it**. After the Second World War the United States was the leading political and economic power, and for a good part of the post-war years it handled **more than 50–60%** of global trade. It pressed oil producers, including Saudi Arabia, to sell oil in dollars, and offered security and other incentives. Every oil-importing country then needed dollars, so exporters asked to be paid in dollars too. **De-dollarisation** means moving trade off the dollar as the medium. **Russia** and **China** promote it vocally: China in its own currency, Russia in the **rouble**. The United States is unhappy, and he said it threatens **tariffs** on countries that promote this very openly. Acceptability, not the appreciation arithmetic, is the point of this paragraph.

## 6. What the RBI does when the move is large (ECO-11-06)

When the rupee fluctuates on a large scale, the RBI can step in to cap the move. The instrument is still demand and supply.

**To check a large appreciation.** The economy is full of dollars and short of rupees, so the rupee is rising. The RBI **prints rupees, releases them, and purchases the dollars**. The extra rupees cool the price. The dollars go into the **foreign exchange reserves**. Banks are glad to hand dollars to the RBI and take rupees, because their business is in rupees. This is how the reserve is built. It is an outcome of these transactions, not a separate lever.

Printing **may** be inflationary. It is not automatic. Inflation follows when the new money creates demand and **production does not come up**. Deal with the appreciation first. If inflation appears later, the RBI deals with that later. “Printing” here means **increasing the circulation**. It can be paper, and it can be digital. The billions moving in and out are largely digital transfers into bank accounts, not a ship of notes. In the United States a very small part of the stock is physical cash.

**To check a large depreciation.** The economy is full of rupees and short of dollars, so the dollar is rising. India cannot print dollars. The RBI **sells dollars from the foreign-exchange reserves and buys rupees**. Commercial banks, not foreign banks, are the counterpart: the exiting investor has rupees in an Indian bank, the bank takes dollars from the RBI and gives the RBI rupees, then pays the client. This is the case that matters more for India, because the rupee’s usual pressure is downward. He said that over the **last one and a half years** the rupee had fallen sharply against the dollar, and the RBI had repeatedly sold dollars to support it. No start-month was given.

## 7. Expansionary policy, and where the class stopped (ECO-11-07)

He used this topic to join fiscal policy, money and banking, and inflation to the exchange rate. The **effect on the rupee** was the point, and the period ended before he reached it. **Next class.**

**Monetary policy** is made by the central bank, the RBI. It works on two things: **money supply**, and the **cost of capital** (interest on a loan).

**Fiscal policy** is made by the **government**. It works on two things: **expenditure** and **receipts**. The **Union Budget** is an instrument of it: an estimate of what the government will receive (tax, non-tax, dividend) and what it will spend (revenue and capital).

**Expansionary** monetary and fiscal policy is adopted to **promote growth**. **Contractionary** policy is adopted to **control inflation**. Only the expansionary side was taught.

**Growth** = an increase in the production of goods and services. In this chapter that means **GDP growth**: GDP is the value of goods and services produced in one financial year. He set aside, for later, the point that GDP is not the only measure of well-being (Human Development Index and the rest).

Growth depends on **demand**. Demand exists only when **both** are present: **willingness to buy**, and **ability to pay**. If either is missing, demand is falling. Industries then cut production, jobs fall, incomes fall, and demand falls again. That loop is the **vicious cycle of slowdown**. If it intensifies and lasts, it becomes a **recession**. An economy does not break the loop on its own. The government and the central bank have to revive **demand** first, by putting money in people’s hands. Ability to pay generally brings willingness with it. Production then catches up.

**Expansionary monetary policy** (RBI) raises **money supply / liquidity**:

- **Printing** currency. He called this the most effective, because it adds new money. Reckless printing is bad. Printing is not always bad: as the economy grows, the circulation has to grow with it. His size picture was an economy moving from a **3-trillion** scale toward a **5-trillion** scale. That is an illustration of why the stock of money rises, not a new target.
- **Cut CRR and SLR.** CRR is the share of deposits a commercial bank must set aside and **cannot lend**, so that withdrawals can be met. Illustration: deposits ₹100, CRR **5%**, so ₹5 is locked and ₹95 can be lent. A cut to **3%** frees **₹2** more to lend, and money supply rises. SLR is the same idea and **can be kept in securities**. The full instruments wait for the money-and-banking class.
- **OMO**, and the direction that raises supply is **purchase** of government securities. OMO is the RBI **buying or selling** government securities in the open market. Open market here means **banks and other financial institutions**. The RBI buys the banks’ G-Secs and pays rupees. Banks then have funds they can lend.
- A **cut in the interest rate**. The rate he means is the **repo rate**: the rate at which the **RBI lends to commercial banks**, announced by the RBI or the Monetary Policy Committee. The cut is an **indication**, not a binding order. Banks usually follow, because if one cuts (a public sector bank first, under government pressure; he named State Bank of India, then HDFC and ICICI), competition pulls the others. Lending rates and deposit rates move the same way sooner or later.

Loans then become more **available** (banks are eager) and more **affordable** (cheaper interest). His illustration, not a scheme: a home-loan EMI falling from **₹50,000** to **₹30,000** a month brings buyers into real estate, builders start projects, and cement, steel, furniture and wiring follow. The same logic for vehicles and for a laptop paid over easy instalments. He said “22 years” once and “24 months” for the laptop. Lock **24 months** for the laptop. Do not lock 22 years.

**Expansionary fiscal policy** (government): **higher government expenditure**, both **revenue** and **capital**, and **lower tax rates**.

- Revenue expenditure he named: subsidies, salaries (including a revision for government employees), pensions, scholarships, direct benefit transfer, food subsidy.
- Capital expenditure: new projects — a highway, an expressway, a power plant, a canal. A **₹50,000 crore** project was his illustration of where the money goes: into the economy, as direct jobs (engineers, supervisors, labourers) and as demand for cement, steel, components and earth-movers.
- A tax cut leaves people with more **disposable income**. He cited the **GST rate cut announced before Diwali last year** as an expansionary move to boost demand. No notification date.

### Lecture 1 — locks

1. Foreign exchange = foreign currency. The rate is the price of one currency in another. **Always name the other currency.** ₹100 → ₹80 is rupee appreciation against the dollar. ₹80 → ₹100 is depreciation. Live range he gave: about **₹95–96**.
2. Inflow of foreign exchange (exports, FDI, FPI, donations, remittances, a new external loan) raises demand for rupees. If rupee supply is limited, the rupee appreciates. Repayment later depreciates it.
3. Appreciation **tends** to cheapen imports and make exports less competitive, goods and services both. Foreign tourists are discouraged. Foreign education and foreign travel become cheaper for Indians. Do not say the import bill **will** rise or fall.
4. Outflow (imports, outgoing FDI/FPI, loan repayment and interest) raises demand for dollars. If dollars are scarce, the rupee depreciates. Imports become costlier, exports more competitive. Tendency only.
5. No sector-blind verdict. Importers like a strong rupee. Exporters like a weak one. India runs a trade deficit, so a weaker rupee makes the import bill harder and may feed inflation. China is accused of holding its currency down for exports.
6. Sharp moves hurt somebody badly. Gradual moves get factored in. The RBI caps a rising rupee by **printing rupees and buying dollars** (those dollars enter the reserve). It caps a falling rupee by **selling reserve dollars**. Over the previous one and a half years it had been doing the second.
7. Expansionary policy revives demand: RBI raises liquidity and cuts the repo; the government spends more and taxes less. **What that does to the rupee was not taught.**
