## 1. A generalist at the head of a specialist service (GOV-03-01)

In public administration the debate is **generalist versus specialist**. An officer of the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** is labelled a **generalist**. Once the rank is **Secretary**, the same officer can be sent to education, then textiles, then finance. The career never settles in one field. A lay reading is enough: one person cannot hold the knowledge those domains each require.

The sharpest criticism, he said, comes from the office of the **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)**. The CAG is the **guardian of the Consolidated Fund of India**, and the **head of financial administration**. The sheet places the office under **Article 148**. For the **Centre** the CAG’s task, as he corrected it, is **audit**. For the **states** it is **audit and accounts**. He heads two specialist Group A cadres: the **Indian Audit and Accounts Service (IAAS)** and the **Indian Civil Accounts Service (ICAS)**. The criticism is that those specialists are led by a generalist, who may never have served in finance.

The **Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI)** — he expanded the letters as the Indian Chartered Accountant Institution — has said that appointing **former Secretaries** as CAG can compromise both the **efficiency** and the **independence** of the office. Efficiency, because a generalist is leading specialists. Independence, because those Secretaries served under the Government of India and carried an allegiance to political bosses, and they are then asked to audit that same government. His picture, not a case to memorise: a Secretary who has a good equation with a minister is later elevated, and is then the auditor of a scam in that minister’s government. IAS officers, in this telling, are the government’s most trusted lieutenants precisely because they have already served as Secretaries under successive regimes.

The appointment sits with the executive. The body he named is the **Cabinet Committee on Appointments**: **two** members, the **Prime Minister** and the **Home Minister**, and the most powerful committee for bureaucratic appointments. **Before July 2016** it had **three** members. He did not name the third. Removal is a different door. The CAG is removed in the manner of a **judge of the Supreme Court**. That needs **Parliament**. Appointment can follow executive discretion. Removal needs parliamentary competence.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** he first said the Centre gets accounts and the states get both, then he corrected it. The lock is **Centre: audit. States: audit and accounts.**</span>

## 2. Patrimonialism, and a posting that lasts 16 months (GOV-03-02)

**Patrimonialism** is an arrangement in which **power flows from a political authority**. In the Indian bureaucracy that has meant **frequent transfers**. The political boss decides the posting. The **P.C. Hota Committee** on civil-service reforms found that those transfers spoil the **positive outcomes of policy**, because the officer never gets a stable tenure.

The **Centre for Policy Research** found that the **average posting of an IAS officer is about 16 months**. A new transfer follows.

The **N.N. Vohra Committee** found that the same culture produces a **triangular nexus** of **politicians, bureaucrats and criminals**, and that the bureaucrats use the nexus for **rent-seeking**: a benefit drawn out of the arrangement. Politicians patronise bureaucrats. Bureaucrats patronise criminals.

The **Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian (2013)** also noticed the transfers. Two directions he dictated:

- a **Civil Services Board**, to decide the **transfers and postings of IAS officers**;
- **non-acceptance of oral orders**. Only written orders.

**Hota’s** remedy, separate from the judgment: if civil servants were given a **fixed term of three years**, with **annual targets**, a **quantum jump** in their functioning could be expected.

The culture also produces open conflict.

Honest officers, in his line, are not raised to the senior posts. A Khemka as CAG would be a nightmare for any government.

## 3. What the Second Administrative Reforms Commission observed (GOV-03-03)

The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission** made five observations. The shorthand is the **2nd ARC**.

1. **Overburdening of IAS officers**, specifically at the **ministerial** level: one officer, many departments.
2. **Complicated and outdated laws and rules.**
3. Civil services care about **internal decision-making**, not **result orientation**.
4. **Abuse of power.**
5. **Resistance to change.**

**Rule 9 of the Civil Services Conduct Rules** is the controversial rule, and it is often in the news because it cuts into **freedom of speech**. Any statement of **fact, opinion or expression** by a public servant must not have an **adverse critical effect** on any **policy or action of the state**. In short, you may not criticise government policy. It also produces an **opaque** administration, because officers will not speak on policy in public. It is a check on **civil-service activism**: an out-of-the-box step can be prohibited under the rule.

**Smita Sabharwal**, a senior IAS officer of the **Telangana** cadre, took to social media and criticised the Gujarat government’s handling of the **Ishrat Jahan** encounter. Senior IPS officers of Gujarat were involved, and the public prosecutor had not opposed a bail plea. She was issued a notice under **Rule 9**. Lecture 1’s “Fund Your City” stays that note.

An outdated pair: the **Right to Information Act, 2005**, beside the **Official Secrets Act, 1923**, which is still a colonial law. The Act itself is the next class. Two points only.

- If an application **exceeds 250 words**, **Rule 4** leaves it to the **discretion** of the public authority, who may **refuse** the information. There is **no standard format**, so the word limit is easy to trip. That dilutes the purpose of the law.
- The Official Secrets Act covers matters with a **direct or indirect** bearing on **national security**. The word **secret is not defined**. An action with no such bearing can still be stamped secret. That fights transparent administration and leaves the agencies a subjective power.

**Abuse of power** means misusing authority held in an official capacity. His examples:

- A senior IPS officer, an SSP, accused of beating a farmer leader **with his boot**, later attached to police headquarters. The name did not land cleanly. Do not invent one.
- A District Magistrate in **Noida**, whose father he said is the **Chief Election Commissioner**, booked a student under the **National Security Act** during a protest. The Supreme Court set that aside, gave relief to the student, and imposed a penalty of **₹5 lakh** to be deducted from the officer. The officer’s name did not land cleanly.
- He called the National Security Act, the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act**, and the Official Secrets Act **draconian**. A trial can take years to start. For the Unlawful Activities Act he gave a **trial rate of about 4 per cent**, and he insisted this is **not** a conviction rate: of 100 people arrested, about **4** see a judge.
- **Thyagaraja Stadium.** An IAS couple, both senior, asked athletes to leave the ground so the two of them could take an evening walk. The **Indian Express** put it on the front page. One was sent to **Arunachal**, the other to **Ladakh**. The woman was later given **compulsory retirement**. He thought the husband was back in Delhi as a municipal commissioner. He did not dictate their names.
- An **Additional Deputy Commissioner of Police** of Delhi Police **slapped a woman** at **Jantar Mantar** during a protest he referred to as the CJP protest.

**Resistance to change** is an **organisational culture**: maintain the **status quo**, and be adamant against any change. He tied the “punishment posting” label on the **Northeast** to that culture. Those posts lack the lavishness of Delhi or of a district magistrate’s office in the northern or southern states. A district magistrate’s office there can be a bamboo hut, and people can walk in.

## 4. Crony capitalism (GOV-03-04)

**Crony capitalism** is a **triangular nexus** of **political bosses, the business fraternity, and bureaucrats**. Together they produce **organised corruption**. This triangle is not the Vohra triangle. Vohra’s third corner is the **criminal**. Here the third corner is the **businessman**.

**Politicians and business.** At election time the bosses need funds. The **Association for Democratic Reforms**, a civil-society organisation working on electoral reform, has reported that **most** of that money comes from **corporates**. The **electoral bond** was the scheme that carried the money. Only the **State Bank of India** issued the bonds, and only on selected dates. The donor bought a bond and deposited it in the party’s dedicated account. The party had **15 days** to encash it. The donor’s name was **not** with the Election Commission. It was with the bank. In **2024** the Supreme Court, and he named Chief Justice **D.Y. Chandrachud**, discontinued the scheme because it did not secure transparency in political funding, and asked the bank to reveal the data. Most of the money was corporate, and the party in power received the larger share. He treated that second point as a fact.

His illustration of the favour that comes back, which he himself called a **coincidence**: generic medicines at the Jan Aushadhi centres were sold at about a **90 per cent** discount, a branded drug at **₹2,000** against a generic at **₹200–300**. The quality was also cheap. The Centre ordered inquiries into a number of pharmaceutical firms. Those firms bought bonds. The inquiries were dropped. Business then seeks its return as **policy intervention**.

**Politicians and bureaucrats.** Westminster political accountability means the boss controls the transfer. To get the favoured posting, the bureaucrat **aligns** with the boss. The **AGMUT** cadre — **Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, Mizoram, and the Union Territories** — is the contrast with a district magistrate of North Delhi. When the guard changes, at the Union or in a state, the news is a reshuffle. His illustrations: **57 IAS** officers transferred in Uttar Pradesh, **56 IPS** officers in another reshuffle. Their allegiance was to the previous political process.

**Business and bureaucrats.** Behind-the-scenes influence, which he translated as a **bribe**.

The **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988** is a principal law against corruption, and it has a hole: it **does not define organised corruption**. He said the term is defined in England. Where several stakeholders are involved, prosecution is hard, because the court cannot be shown a term the statute never defined. They slip the net.

**Pooja Singhal**, not Puja Khedkar. A senior IAS officer of **Jharkhand**. Charged by the **Enforcement Directorate** in a money-laundering case. Accused of misappropriating **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act** funds, and of misusing the **Chota Nagpur Tenancy Act** so that **tribal land** passed to **non-tribals**, the business side. He asked whether that chain is possible without political patronage, and answered no.

## 5. Outsiders, morale, and the CAPF Bill (GOV-03-05)

The **All India Services** are **against the son-of-the-soil policy**. An officer is domiciled in one state and serves in another. That is also how the services serve **national integration**, which Lecture 1 already has as the two masters. The cost, in this class, is **administrative efficiency**. **Language and culture** get in the way. The officer will not leave the air-conditioned office for the **grassroots**. He called that **headquarters governance**, and also the **ivory-tower** approach. Coordination with the local people suffers.

A Malayali IAS officer from **Keralam**, posted in eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, or Haryana, was his nightmare. Training includes a language course. It does not teach the regional dialect. A vacuum opens between the citizen and the administration. In the south, “you” is one word. In the north it is **tu, tum, aap**, and the wrong one can become an administrative fiasco.

**Lack of recognition in the empanelment policy** hurts **morale** and can hurt **efficiency**. He separated the two words. **Morale** is intrinsic. Honesty is morale, and it cannot be taught. **Motivation** is extrinsic. Money motivates a private job.

The **Central Armed Police Forces Bill, 2026** is the example. For posts at **Inspector General**, and at **Director General and Additional Director General**, the preference goes to the **Indian Police Service**. About **67 per cent** of the Inspector General seats, and **100 per cent** of the Director General seats, are kept for that service. The in-house entry of a Central Armed Police Force is the **Assistant Commandant**, a rank he equated with **Deputy Superintendent of Police**. Those officers generally retire at **Deputy Inspector General** or **Inspector General**. An IPS officer can arrive with only headquarters experience: a minimum of **18 years** for Inspector General, and for Director General he said **25 years or 28 years** under the IPS rules. The armed police forces are about **combat**. The outsider may have none. The in-house cadre watches its own record go unrecognised, and the efficiency of the force can suffer with the morale.

## 6. Activism, red tape, and the silo (GOV-03-06)

**Civil-service activism** is a **proactive** set of measures by civil servants to make administration **accountable, citizen-centric, honest and transparent**. It includes organising **seminars**, running **awareness campaigns**, **teaching people their rights**, and being **intolerant of anti-constitutional practices**. He corrected himself: the intolerance is of the anti-constitutional, not of the constitutional.

It is blocked by three things.

**Bureau pathology** is his collective name for **complex internal decisions**, **excessive centralisation**, and **complicated laws and rules**.

**Red-tapism** puts unnecessary hurdles in the path of a policy. The word comes from the government file, which is tied in a **red ribbon**. The file moves from office to office. The delay feeds corruption.

A **veto** is the power to say an **absolute no**. There are two kinds.

- **Vertical.** India uses Weber’s hierarchy, already in Lecture 1, and decisions follow that ladder. A subordinate’s decision can be approved up the line and still be **rejected or overturned** by a senior. The 2nd ARC said decision-making is **hierarchy-oriented** and should become **officer-oriented**: the officer should have flexibility.
- **Horizontal.** An **inter-ministerial** or **inter-departmental** policy. The officers sit at the same level. One ministry approves. Another can refuse.

**Silo mentality** is the habit of working in **compartments** rather than in **coordination**.

**Rampant corruption**, which the Hota Committee put from the **top to the bottom** of the bureaucratic ladder, sits with the Prevention of Corruption Act hole already in section 4.

## 7. Lateral entry (GOV-03-07)

The recent reforms come first. Committee reports and the 2nd ARC follow.

**Lateral entry** bypasses the regular mode of recruitment at **mid and top** bureaucratic posts, and fills them with **specialists from the private sector and the public sector**, including public-sector undertakings. In the Weberian model the top posts are reached through the Union Public Service Commission and then through experience. A lateral entrant holds the top post **without that examination**, on **specialisation** and on **experience**.

Two reasons.

**The generalist IAS.** The lateral entrant is the expert, and fills the **skill deficit**. The phrase he used for the IAS was **jack of all trades, master of none**. A person with **15 years** in a civil-society organisation knows that field better than an IAS officer posted to the related ministry for a short spell.

**The Baswan Committee** found a **shortage of IAS officers**, especially in the **BIMARU** states, which **NITI Aayog** has renamed **aspirational states** because BIMARU is derogatory. He called the new name old wine in a new bottle. Those states have a stagnant state product, weak socio-economic indicators, high poverty and high unemployment. He put **Bihar’s per capita income at ₹93,000**. Lateral entrants can fill the **gap vacancies** as well as the skill deficit.

**Dynamic policy-making.** India, in the sentence he dictated, is a **transitional** society that needs a specialised bureaucracy, with a desirable skill set, to meet global challenges. The sheet says **traditional**. The spoken word is transitional. The challenges he named are **artificial intelligence** and **cybersecurity**.

The agencies that handle cyber crime, cyber-policy research, and artificial-intelligence crime and research — **CERT-In** (Computer Emergency Response Team–India), **I4C**, and the **NCCC** — he said are headed by **IPS** officers. So is **NATGRID**, which he called a highly optimistic project of the Government of India. Recruitment is on the **generalist** pattern. An officer whose degree is an **MA in History** cannot be assumed to have the technical skill those agencies need, and the officer is not at fault for that.

The skill base outside government is thin too. The **All India Council for Technical Education** recognises engineering colleges **other than the IITs**. Its report, as he cited it, says **98 per cent** of the engineering workforce **do not know coding**. An **Oxfam** report, the year he thought was **2021**, says **85 per cent** of that workforce cannot get a good job **in their own field**. Where skill is poor, **capacity building** suffers, unemployment follows, and the demographic dividend may not be used. The answer he wanted is to **bring in specialists** who have already spent years in the domain.

**India’s past experience he called positive.**

| Name | What he assigned |
|:---|:---|
| **Raghuram Rajan** | Governor of the Reserve Bank, and **not** an IAS officer |
| **Manmohan Singh** | The **first non-IAS Finance Secretary**. That post is, by default, still reserved for the IAS |
| **M.S. Swaminathan** | Father of the Green Revolution. His line: in an era when the world has weapons, India will have grain |
| **Arvind Subramanian** | Named with NITI Aayog in the same breath. No separate post was dictated |
| **Urjit Patel** | Governor of the Reserve Bank. **Demonetisation** fell in his tenure |

They are **not a burden on the public exchequer**. They get **no post-retirement benefits**: no pension, no perks. They are deployed for **three years**. A **performance appraisal** follows. Satisfactory work earns another term. Unsatisfactory work sends them home. A civil servant stays either way. The specialist also gives the seasoned civil servant a **new exposure**, something to absorb from a field the civil servant has not lived in. That is the **positive competition**.

**Limitations.**

1. **Against the constitutional spirit.** The Union Public Service Commission, the constitutional body for recruiting civil servants, is **not consulted**. He accepted **Article 315** for the Commission, and he separated **Article 312**: an All India Service is created by a resolution of the **Rajya Sabha**, passed by a majority of **two-thirds of the members present and voting**. The lateral interview is taken by the **Department of Personnel and Training** or by the **ministry concerned**, not by the Commission.
2. **Against social justice**, because there is **no reservation**. The last notification, issued by the Commission in **2024**, was withdrawn after allies of the government objected. He named a Bihar politician, **Paswan**, of the **Lok Janshakti Party**, with **five** MPs in the coalition. His own balance: social justice and **administrative efficiency** stand together, and efficiency is not to be surrendered in the name of social justice. The case he cited he called **Ames versus the Student Union of Ames**. The observation he wanted kept: **reservation beyond a limit can compromise the quality of a public service**. His medical picture: reservation may run up to the **MBBS**, which is a general degree, and not into the **MD**, which is a specialisation. Do not rename the case.
3. A **spoil system** in **removal and retention**, through **political patronage**.
4. **Not a panacea.** Three years is a short frame. Professional training in the services is **two years**, **24 months**. The entrant’s expertise is for **formulation**. Implementation still has to pass through administrative channels. They are not given a free hand to skip procedure.
5. **Favouritism toward one sector.** A private-sector entrant is guided by **profit**, not by a **social-service** motive.
6. A **negative effect on the morale** of efficient officers, who can be bypassed. Some of them already have a specialist exposure, and experience counts at the ministerial level.

**Way forward, as he dictated it.**

1. Absorb the more efficient and dedicated civil servants through a **360-degree appraisal** in the **empanelment** policy.
2. Train the existing civil servants on **dynamic modules**, of the kind in **Mission Karmayogi**.
3. A **parliamentary panel**: send a group of **IAS and IPS** officers into the **private sector** for a new exposure, including managerial skill. Administration, he said, is a kind of public management.
4. **Information and Communication Technology** in governance. He marked this as a **Hota** recommendation, and he called it e-governance. The chapter stays Lecture 1.
5. **Awards** for officers who perform better.

He also accepted, in answer to the class, the idea of **more specialist All India Services**, and he mentioned an **All India Judicial Service**, tying that thought to the commission on Centre–state relations of **1983**.

## 8. What the Second Commission wanted at the gate (GOV-03-08)

**At entry, a National Institution of Public Administration.** It would run degree courses in **law, management and public administration**, and build a **talent pool** of aspirants who already know the fields governance actually turns on. Completing a **bridge course** — a short diploma or certificate, of **three, four or six months** — would be the **minimum** requirement to sit the civil-services examination. He called it a good recommendation, and **not accepted**.

**Age and attempts.** For the general category the 2nd ARC wanted a **maximum of 25 years**, a **minimum of 21**, and **four attempts**. The present arrangement, as he stated it, is **32 years and six attempts**. He took both sides. A candidate from a poor or rural background may start school at **eight or nine**, and an LLB plus LLM, or an MBBS plus MD, can finish only at **23 or 24**, so four attempts and a ceiling of 25 leave almost nothing. Those, he said, are exceptions, and exceptions can be written in, as **JNU** gives a preference to graduates of rural universities at the master’s admission, and as **ex-servicemen** already have an exception in the civil services. On **administrative efficiency** he still called the recommendation good. A younger entrant is the more dynamic of the two, and by the time the older pattern reaches 32 the younger officer has already gathered seniority. In the police that seniority is what opens the better posts.

The other ceilings he added: **Hota** wanted a maximum of **24** and **three** attempts, and slogans went up against that at JNU. **Baswan** wanted a maximum of **26** for the general category.

Why he dislikes the long runway: if the attempt fails at 32, after six tries, little of the public sector is left. State commissions are thin and late. **Andhra Pradesh** once in **five** years. **Maharashtra**, **seven or eight** vacancies after **four or five** years. **Haryana**, **nine** vacancies after **three** years. The state-service maximum is generally **40**, and **40 to 42** in some states, precisely because the examination is irregular. Private employers, especially in engineering, already count a **gap of six months or a year**. Twenty-five, in his view, is still young enough for another road.

**From the State Civil Services into the All India Services.** State Civil Services are also called the **Provincial Civil Services**. The 2nd ARC wanted a **written examination followed by an interview**. At present **30 per cent** of the All India Service seats in a state come from that cadre: a provincial civil servant promoted to the IAS, a provincial police officer to the IPS. The present filter is an **interview alone**. A written paper would add transparency. He then called the recommendation **not** a good one. By the time of promotion the officer is about **49 or 50**, the exposure is one state, and after promotion the officer **stays in the same cadre**, which dilutes the all-India character. The better course, in his view, is to **remove the 30 per cent ceiling** and give the vacancies to younger direct recruits. Promotee officers generally retire as **Additional District Magistrate** or **District Magistrate**. They have not held a secretary’s post.

**Performance management.** The 2nd ARC wanted it to be **consultative** and **job-specific**. One further recommendation he added: recruit the civil servant initially for **20 years**. He said ten once, and corrected it to twenty. After 20 years, a **performance appraisal**. Satisfactory work continues. Unsatisfactory work means **compulsory retirement**.

The handout runs into a second day. The next class starts there. The Right to Information Act, in full, is the chapter he named.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** Vohra’s triangle ends in the **criminal**. Crony capitalism’s triangle ends in the **businessman**. The average IAS posting is **16 months**. Hota’s fixed term is **three years**. The CAPF Bill keeps **67 per cent** of Inspector General posts and **all** Director General posts for the IPS. Lateral entry is **three years**, **no pension**, and the **2024** notification was **withdrawn**. The 2nd ARC’s general-category gate is **25 years and four attempts**. The promotee share is **30 per cent**. The first appraisal he added is at **20 years**.</span>
