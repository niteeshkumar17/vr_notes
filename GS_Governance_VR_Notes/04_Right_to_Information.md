## 1. A human right, and then a fundamental right (GOV-04-01)

The **right to information**, or the **right to know**, has been regarded as a **human right** by the **1948** declaration he called the **United Nations Declaration on Human Rights**. The clause he used: **irrespective of frontiers**, citizens have the right to know what is going on inside their government. Frontiers here means borders.

The **United Nations Development Programme** treats the right as a **cardinal** component of any democracy, on its appeal to **eliminate corruption and enable better living**. Cardinal means indispensable. The audio said “UNT” once. The body is the Development Programme.

The **Supreme Court**, on repeated occasions, has treated the right as a **fundamental right**. The three cases he named: **Indian Express**, **Raj Narain**, and **S.P. Gupta**. The audio said “Raj Narayan.” It arises out of **Article 19(1)(a)**. It is inherent in the freedom of speech and expression. He said “inherited.” The word he wanted is inherent.

The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission** said the right brings **objectivity** into the work of a public authority, **by legitimising its actions**. His pictures of that legitimising were four scams. He placed all four in the second **United Progressive Alliance** government, and he wanted the irony kept: the **2005** Act was that government’s own law, and the law revealed that government’s scams.

| What the file showed | The number he dictated | What followed |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Commonwealth Games.** Money meant for the welfare of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes was diverted to the Games | About **₹744 crore** | A tissue roll priced at **₹600**. A bathing bucket at **₹4,000 to ₹5,000**. The misappropriation came out through the Act |
| **Adarsh Housing Society, Maharashtra.** Flats meant for army families, including the families of those who had died | He did not give a rupee figure | The flats went to political bosses and bureaucrats. The Act revealed it. **Every allocation was cancelled** |
| **Coal.** Discrepancies, misappropriation, favouritism in the allocation of coal blocks | A loss of about **₹10,000 crore** to the public exchequer | Revealed through the Act. **All the allocations were cancelled** |
| **2G spectrum** | A loss of about **₹1.75 lakh crore** | Previous allocations cancelled. **New allocations were announced** |

Without the Act, he said, these misappropriations would not have been known. Cancelling the old allocation and making a fresh one is the objectivity: the authority’s action is legitimised only when it can be seen.

## 2. What the right actually contains (GOV-04-02)

The right is the **disbursement or dissemination** of any information held by a public authority, **either voluntarily or on demand**. Voluntary is the suo motu disclosure. Demand is the written application.

It is an **integrated** right, a **composite** right. Four sub-rights: the right to **inspect**, to **collect samples**, to **take photographs**, and to **receive the information in digital form**.

**Section 2** defines information in a very wide way. He read only a few of the words: **documents, files, records, orders, reports, advice, opinion, memos**. A memo is part of the internal proceedings of a department. The list is not closed. He did not settle the subsection letter. Do not invent one.

**Public authority** is also in section 2. Five kinds.

1. Established by the **Constitution**.
2. Established by a **law of Parliament**.
3. Established by a **law of the state legislature**.
4. Established by an **executive resolution**. His examples: an **ordinance** of the President, the **Central Bureau of Investigation**, and the **Planning Commission**.
5. Any **non-governmental organisation** that is **partially, substantially, or completely** funded from the state fund, which he also called the public exchequer.

A private body can fall into the fifth kind. **Sanskriti School** is a private school, substantially financed by the state, and the **Delhi High Court** declared it a public authority. The **Indian Olympic Association** was declared a public authority by the same court. He gave no year for either.

The **judiciary** is not in that clean list. The statutory position, as he stated it, is not clear. In **2010** the Delhi High Court said the judiciary is within the Act. The Supreme Court has repeatedly said that no amendment has been proposed to bring these institutions under the information commissions. The Chief Justice also handles non-judicial appointments, and some disclosure is put on the court’s own website, but that is not the same as being under the commissions. A student’s question was why a judge’s private conduct stays out. His answer was the **independence** of the institution: that conduct is looked at when a judge is elevated, and a later disclosure can be used to tarnish the office.

## 3. Who answers, and in how many days (GOV-04-03)

Every public authority has a **Public Information Officer**. That officer is the **first point of contact**. The post is a **charge** on an officer who already exists. There is no separate examination for it. In the Public Works Department the existing ladder is assistant engineer, executive engineer, superintendent engineer, chief engineer. One of them carries the charge.

An **Assistant Public Information Officer** exists where the departmental load is high: a headquarters, a secretariat, a line-and-staff agency. His picture was a police headquarters, where fire, civil police, the armed constabulary, wireless, and the criminal-investigation wing each hold a piece of the file. The assistant has to collect across those wings. A small authority does not need one. The officer there simply opens the record.

The reply comes in the **official language of that state**. His examples: do not ask Uttar Pradesh in Malayalam, and do not expect Kerala to answer in English.

The **30 days** do not run from the day you posted the letter. The office issues an **acknowledgement**. It carries a file number, the date of receipt, and a line that the information is being gathered. The 30 days run from **the date on that acknowledgement**.

| Clock | When |
|:---|:---|
| **30 days** | The ordinary reply from the Public Information Officer |
| **35 days** | The application went to the **Assistant** Public Information Officer |
| **48 hours**, which he also called **2 days** | The matter is **life or liberty**. He slipped once and said 45 hours, and once and said 48 days. The lock he repeated is **48 hours**. Who decides that it is life or liberty is the **discretion** of the authority that has to dispatch the information. A court can also order a dispatch of its own |
| **45 days** | A **section 24** organisation, after the Central Information Commission has ordered disclosure in the public interest. He said “45 minutes” once and then corrected it to days |

The **fee** is fixed by the **Union** for a Union department and by the **state** for a state department. A person **below the poverty line pays nothing**. An appeal costs nothing. The only fee is at the entry, at the information officer, and the poverty-line exemption sits there.

**Section 8** is **exempted information**. You cannot claim it as of right. **Section 24** is **exempted organisations**. You cannot, as of right, write to them at all. That difference is the point.

Exempted information, in the order he had them write:

1. Disclosure would affect **national security**. His example was an application filed after a recent operation, asking what had been recovered. The operation’s name did not land. Do not invent one.
2. Information received from a **foreign country in confidence**: defence deals, agreements, foreign intelligence. **Rafale’s** technical configuration came from **Dassault** in confidence. He would not put it in the open, because another country can reverse-engineer it.
3. Information that may affect a **competitive position**: **trademarks**, **intellectual property**.
4. **Cabinet papers**, and **third-party** information.
5. Information on a **public-private partnership**.
6. Information that may affect the **course of an investigation**.
7. Information on a case **pending before a court**.
8. Information that would breach the **privilege** of a member of Parliament or of a legislature.
9. Information of a **private** nature about a public functionary.

Exempted organisations, as the names landed. The sheet’s short list is the **Central Reserve Police Force**, the **Army**, the **Indian Reserve Battalion**, and the **Provincial Armed Constabulary**. He added the intelligence and revenue bodies: **Research and Analysis Wing**, the **Intelligence Bureau** (the audio said “IV”), the **National Investigation Agency**, the **Central Bureau of Investigation** (the audio said “CPI”), the **Directorate of Revenue Intelligence**, and the **Financial Intelligence Unit**. One more name landed only as “PAD.” Do not turn that into a full form.

If a **public interest** is involved in a disclosure from one of those organisations, the application goes **directly to the Central Information Commission**. If the commission, on the **merits**, is satisfied and passes an order, the information is released within **45 days**. Whether something is a public interest is the commission’s discretion.

## 4. The appeal, and the penalty (GOV-04-04)

A late reply, a concealment, a misstatement, or a half reply is a **denial**. The **first appeal** is within **30 days**. It costs nothing. The first appellate officer is inside the **same department**, and generally calls both sides in person. The information officer will make excuses. The ones he named: the Official Secrets Act, and **sections 123 and 124** of the Indian Evidence Act, which come back below. On the merits, the appellate officer orders the information to be given or refuses it.

Still unsatisfied, the second appeal is to the **State Information Commission** for the state, and to the **Central Information Commission** for the Centre. Those two commissions are **independent**. They are not a departmental charge. The Act is **silent beyond the Central Information Commission**. There is no further remedy inside the Act. He got tangled, for a minute, on whether a state commission hears a central file. The lock he then stated is the one above: state commission for the state, central commission for the Centre.

The **penalty** is **₹250 a day**, up to a **maximum of ₹25,000**. It is not ₹25,000 a day. It is deducted from the **salary of the Public Information Officer**. Liability starts when the 30 days have run: on the next day the ₹250 begins, and ₹250 is added each day until the cap. His example of a penalty actually imposed: a query on the **Prime Minister’s degree** from **Delhi University**, and the penalty fell on that university’s information officer.

## 5. What the Act is supposed to do to governance (GOV-04-05)

Five effects.

**Accountability.** The Act rests on a **golden triangle**: **visibility** of a public authority’s actions and inactions, **answerability** for those actions and inactions, and **enforcement** of the penalty for non-compliance.

**Transparency.** It builds a culture of openness by **voluntary disclosure**. The maxim he dictated is **pro bono publico**, for the public good, and with it **value for the taxpayer’s money**.

**Less corruption.** The Act finds the likely sites of corruption and then supplies a remedy, which is the objectivity and the legitimacy already in the Adarsh and Commonwealth Games files.

**Decentralisation of information.** The file is no longer only inside the authority. It is dispersed to ordinary citizens. That is also how an **informed citizenry** is made: the **information asymmetry** shrinks, because the citizen has direct access to what the authority holds.

**Participatory, or consensus-oriented, governance.** Citizens can identify the loopholes, which is the right to inspect, and can contribute to the making of policy.

The Uttar Pradesh file is the one he used for the last two. A schoolgirl, **Aishwarya**, passed a garbage dump on the way to school. She wrote to the **Chief Minister’s office**. Nothing happened for **two years**. She then used the Act, to the same office, and asked two questions. Is that place a **sanctioned** dumping site? If someone falls ill because of it, **who is accountable**? The reply, from the Lucknow municipal corporation and from the Chief Minister’s office, was immediate, and it was not the answer she had been refused for two years. The plot was meant for a **public library**. The second question was not answered. **You cannot ask why.** A decision is a matter of the officer’s administrative prudence, and where the Chief Minister, the mayor, the executive engineer, the junior engineer and the supervisor are all in the chain, accountability cannot be pinned on one of them. By **2026** the dump had been removed and a public library stood on the plot. What it took, in his telling, was one vigilant citizen whose effort the Act made effective. The Act legitimised the authority’s action by forcing it into the open.

## 6. The data-protection law overshadows one clause (GOV-04-06)

**Section 8(1)(j)** is a **conditional** disclosure. Personal information is not disclosed. If a **public interest** attaches to that disclosure, it can be. The caveat is the public-interest clause. The audio said “8.1.0” and “section 810.” The sheet writes **8(1)(j)**.

**Section 44(3)** of the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** prohibits disclosure of **any** personal information. Any means any, whether or not a public interest is involved. That overshadows 8(1)(j). He also said “2020” once. The Act he was criticising is **2023**, which is also the year in the essay question.

Three implications.

- It threatens the **freedom of the press**, which sits inside Article 19(1)(a). A **data principal** is the citizen whose data is collected. A **data fiduciary** collects the data, stores it, and processes it. The government is one. The press is one. The penalty he named goes up to **₹250 crore**. An accidental breach can cost that, and he called it a direct threat to investigative journalism.
- A **paradox**, from **section 7** of the same Act. The government may take a citizen’s data and process it **without permission**. The citizen may not ask the government for the information.
- **Audit reports, procurement details, and lists of beneficiaries** can disappear from the public domain, because a beneficiary list can be called personal information. His route into that was **Aadhaar** inside **Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and mobile**, and the **direct benefit transfer**. The Supreme Court, he said, has remarked that the term **personal information needs to be defined**, so that the **right to privacy** can be balanced with the **right to information**. He did not name the case.

## 7. The 2019 amendment (GOV-04-07)

The **2019** amendment made two changes. The **salaries, allowances and other perks**, and the **tenure and conditions of service**, of the **Central Information Commission** and of the **State Information Commissions**, are now determined by the **Union government**. That includes the state commissions. The instrument is an **executive order**. Earlier, both were in the **law**.

The tenure he remembered from before the amendment was a **fixed five years**. Removal existed, but it was a longer process and a committee was involved. Now, he said, removal can also be by **executive order**. The requirement he put first, for any institution that has to work independently, is **security of tenure**. Without it, the independent functioning of both commissions is compromised.

Four criticisms.

1. A blow to **cooperative federalism**. His definition: an arrangement in which the Union and the states work together for **national** objectives and for **state-specific** ones. Education, health and sanitation were his national examples. **Ladli Behna** of **Madhya Pradesh** was the state-specific one. The Union can turn a state commission against a state government. He tied that to a **parochial** political culture: a different party at the Centre and in the state, and the commission used to the state’s disadvantage.
2. Critics call it the **Right to Information Elimination Act**. There was **no public consultation**, and no parliamentary committee. He called the amendment arbitrary.
3. Independence, already above, because the tenure is no longer secure.
4. It promotes a **silo mentality**. He defined that again as **compartmentalisation**, working in compartments. The same word in the 4 October note is this idea. Here the mechanism is the tenure. The Union decides how long they stay, and an executive order can remove them, so they will protect the position rather than do anything that might hurt the party in power. They save the chair instead of discharging the responsibility.

## 8. The older secrecy laws, and the problems (GOV-04-08)

These laws, taken together, breed a **culture of secrecy**. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission’s line, which he had already used, is the opposite culture: openness.

**Indian Evidence Act.** **Section 123**: information the head of the department has marked is not disclosed **without that head’s approval**. **Section 124**: information whose disclosure would harm the **public interest** is not disclosed. He said “section 120” once and then corrected it to 123 and 124. He taught this as the Indian Evidence Act. Do not rename the statute from outside the class.

**Civil Services Conduct Rules.** A civil servant is **barred** from a public disclosure without the approval of the competent authority. The controversial rule, and the Smita Sabharwal notice, stay the 4 October note.

**Official Secrets Act.** He will not teach it here. That chapter is internal security. One line only: information marked as a **state secret** under the Act is prohibited from **publication or any other disclosure**. The 4 October class dated the Act to **1923**. This recording said **1920**. Keep **1923**. The undefined word “secret,” and the 250-word application, stay that earlier note.

Other problems.

- **Records are not maintained.**
- **Resistance to change**, the organisational culture from the 4 October class, shows up here as **poor compliance**.
- **Misuse**, for **cheap publicity**. The requests are vague and carry no national interest: the Prime Minister’s **diet chart**, the **cost of his spectacles**, a **pen worth ₹1.25 lakh**, the **speed of the internet** in the Prime Minister’s Office. He set this beside the Supreme Court’s remark that a public-interest petition is no longer public-interest litigation. It has become **publicity-interest** litigation. Frivolous applications under this Act are the same habit.
- **Pendency.** **Satark Nagrik Sangathan**, a civil-society body that works on this Act, found **more than 4 lakh** applications pending. The audio mangled the name. The sheet’s penalty figure, which he was dictating as that body’s observation, is **1.2 per cent** of cases. The speech kept saying “only in 1.” The sheet is the number to hold.
- **Staff.** Poor orientation, meaning the staff are not trained for the Act, and a **shortage** of staff.
- **Awareness**, on the **demand** side. He separated **literacy** from **functional literacy**. A literate person can read and write, and he put the age at about **seven**. Functional literacy is being able to **comprehend** the knowledge you have acquired, including its practical use. Before this class, almost nobody in the room knew how to file an application. That gap is the functional-literacy gap.
- **No periodic revision.** He called it a one-time law, never put through a periodic assessment.
- **Threats to the life** of people who use the Act. The **Whistleblower Protection Act** has failed to secure **anonymity**. The applicant’s name need not be disclosed. Information officers disclose it anyway, for want of training, and that disclosure is the threat.
- **State information commissions** sit **defunct**, because of large **vacancies**. Information commissioners have not been appointed.

The reform list he dictated, ten points. He did not say these ten were the Second Administrative Reforms Commission. The objectivity remark earlier in the hour was. Do not paste that label onto this list.

1. An **open-data** policy: disclosure on **departmental websites** and on **district portals**.
2. Other ways to file: **call centres**, **citizen service centres**.
3. Against misuse: ask the **reasons** when the information is sensitive, and a **penalty for wrong information**. That penalty is a recommendation. The ₹250 already in the Act is for delay, and he added that the commissions can also penalise a misguided reply or a concealed fact.
4. **Information and communication technology** in the management of records.
5. A **periodic assessment** by a **nodal** institution, one created for that purpose, so the Act is revised for the needs of present governance.
6. **Best practices** passed from authorities that disseminate well to the others.
7. **Training** of information officers, with **civil-society** organisations in the room.
8. A **centralised database** for grievance redressal.
9. **Awards** for the public departments that perform better.
10. **Four regional offices** of the Central Information Commission, so the commission has a geographical reach.

**Citizen Charter** was the next heading, and he only set the questions. It is an aspirational document that improves the standards of public services. Analyse. Despite being an aspirational document, it has proved a **toothless tiger**. Critically examine. The teaching is the next class. E-governance remains parked.
