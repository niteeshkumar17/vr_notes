## 1. Hard power, and why it is harder to use (IR-07-01)

**International Relations (IR)** is a struggle for power. Two older resources make **hard power**: **military power** plus **economic power**. The sheet writes that pair as **MP + EP**. Historically that was what “power” meant.

In the post-Cold War years its use became more complex, for three reasons he put under the same bracket.

1. **Globalisation** produced **economic interdependence**. A military or economic threat now hits the sender’s own economy. The United States and China are rivals, and China is the **largest trading partner** of the United States, with American companies invested and manufacturing there. Last year, after taking office, Donald Trump opened an aggressive tariff war on China and could not sustain it. He and **Xi Jinping** met in **South Korea** and signed what was called a **truce**: both cut tariffs. Xi has since visited America again, and the attempt to normalise is being described as a **G2**, a group of two. Containment of China continues. Executing the economic threat does not.
2. **Nuclear deterrence.** **Nine** states have nuclear weapons. None of those nine can fight a direct war against another of the nine, because that war is **MAD**. India and China are the pair he used.
3. **Global challenges** — terrorism, global warming and climate change, a pandemic such as COVID — have no unilateral fix. Only a **multilateral** approach works. That compels cooperation.

Because hard power became harder to use, an American scholar, **Joseph Nye**, offered a new concept in the **1990s**, after the Soviet collapse. **Soft power is the power of attraction.** A state gets the outcome it wants by **persuasion or attraction**, without **coercive** methods. Coercion means force: military force and economic force. The practical form is an attractive **brand**. Other states then want the relationship.

## 2. The three resources (IR-07-01)

**Culture**, used positively. A rich culture that the outside world respects becomes attraction. **Yoga** is the Indian example. There is no meter that pays India a dollar per asana. **21 June** is **International Yoga Day** because yoga is Indian culture, and a more popular yoga is a more popular brand India.

**Cultural chauvinism** destroys the same resource. That is the claim that one’s own culture is superior, that the other is inferior, and that one’s values may be forced on others. The world then turns against the brand. He put the warning on the **Indian diaspora** — people of Indian origin settled abroad. Celebrate Indian festivals. Also respect the culture of the people who live there, and do not disrupt their lives. Mutual respect is the condition. Chauvinism is counterproductive.

**Political values**, the ideal or **liberal** ones: **democracy, equality, liberty, justice, human rights**. Add **secularism**, minority rights, and women’s rights. India’s Constitution is built on them. The Preamble’s democratic republic and secularism are the lines he pointed at. “India is the mother of all democracies” and “the biggest democracy” are attractive sentences, and that is why they are used.

The logic is accountability. A democratic government answers to the people. People want peace and prosperity, not war and bloodshed. In an anarchy the scarce commodity is trust — India trusted China and was attacked in **1962**. A democracy is still easier to trust than a dictator. **Kim Jong-un** answers to nobody. Dictatorships, in his line, behave irresponsibly. More real democracies would make a more peaceful world.

**Foreign policy** on the idealist or liberal pattern. He restated the previous class: **multilateralism**, friendly relations, **free trade agreements (FTAs)**, **collective security** through the **United Nations** instead of **balance of power (BOP)**, an arms race, and alliances. **Woodrow Wilson** remains the name on collective security. Respect for **human rights** and **international law**, including signing and ratifying treaties, produces a **rule-based order**.

India still carries that idealism, and it no longer carries it alone. After **1962** the policy is a **balance of idealism and realism**. India has never initiated a war. The nuclear doctrine is **no first use (NFU)**: the weapons are for self-defence if India is attacked, not for an offensive purpose. The **2023** G20 theme, **Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam** — the world is one family — is the same idealism: cooperate, seek the happiness and security of the whole world, and still strengthen national security.

The reverse damages the brand. A unilateral, aggressive policy, wars, and illegal capture of territory cost soft power. His examples: China in the **South China Sea**, violating international law; Russia’s war on Ukraine; Israel, which he said has killed **70,000** people in **Gaza**, **more than half** of them women and children. **Two days before this class**, **Benjamin Netanyahu** rose to speak in the **UN General Assembly** and representatives walked out. America’s killing of Iran’s leader, and the funeral of **Khamenei**, was his example of a damaged American brand. Soft power, in his close, belongs to a peace-loving state.

## 3. Mature democracy, not a claim of one (IR-07-02)

There is a theory called the **democratic peace thesis**. Since the end of the **Second World War**, no two democratic governments have fought a **direct** war against each other. More such governments would mean a more peaceful, stable, and prosperous world.

He immediately narrowed the word. The thesis means a **mature democracy**: democratic principles followed in reality, an elected government that actually holds power, free and fair elections held regularly. It does not mean a **pseudo-democracy**, a state that claims the name and does not follow the principles. Most states that call themselves democratic are, in his line, pseudo-democracies. A pseudo-democracy gets none of this soft power.

**Pakistan** is the neighbour he used. There is an elected Prime Minister, **Shehbaz Sharif**. Real power sits with the army chief, **General Asim Munir**. The elected Prime Minister is a puppet. Pakistan has had repeated **military coups**: an army general overthrows the elected government and becomes the dictator. He named **Ayub Khan, Yahya Khan, Zia-ul-Haq**, and, as the recent case, **Pervez Musharraf**. **Zia-ul-Haq** had an elected Prime Minister, **Zulfikar Ali Bhutto**, killed. Bhutto’s daughter **Benazir Bhutto** later became Prime Minister. His grandson is **Bilawal Bhutto**.

A student raised India–Pakistan wars, including **Kargil**, against the thesis. His answer: Pakistan is not in the category. The Kargil action was begun by **Musharraf** without informing Prime Minister **Nawaz Sharif**. When the war broke out, Sharif objected, dismissed Musharraf, and a Cabinet meeting appointed a new army chief, who had even taken office. Musharraf then couped the civilian government and became dictator. Sharif was given two choices: leave the country, or be shot. He left. Bhutto had not left, and was killed. **Operation Sindoor is hard power.** It is not an entry on the soft-power page.

The same pseudo pattern, beyond Pakistan: an elected leader eliminates opponents — killed, disappeared, or jailed — then wins an election with no real opposition, plus a few dummy candidates. **Vladimir Putin** in Russia. **Sheikh Hasina** in Bangladesh: opponents imprisoned, and in the **2024** election the opposition parties boycotted. She contested alone and won alone. A student movement in **2024** forced her to resign. A new election has since been held; he called the result a **BNP** government. The personal name was not clear on this audio. The next morning he named the Prime Minister: **Tarique Rahman**. That lock is **IR-08**. Lock **BNP** here. **North Korea** calls itself a republic, claims elections, and claims **99.9%** of voters for Kim Jong-un. No dictator, including **Hitler**, says “I am a dictator.” They say the people chose them.

Mature democracies, as he counted them: a few in **North America**, a few in **Europe**, and in the **Global South** one shining case, **India**. In India the elected government holds the power. Violate democracy, human rights, or secular values, and the soft power starts to leave.

## 4. Public diplomacy (IR-07-03)

**Public diplomacy** is engagement with **foreign publics**. If those people are attracted, they press their own elected government, and the government reciprocates. It **mobilises** the three resources. It does not replace them.

The **Ministry of External Affairs (MEA)** has a **Public Diplomacy Division** for this. **Indian Foreign Service (IFS)** officers, recruited through the civil services examination, work in that ministry. He used a posting as the picture: after the **two-year** training, three years in the embassy in **Paris**, not to negotiate with the French government (seniors already do that), but to be an event manager for the French public. Cultural festivals, yoga camps, food festivals, film festivals, open invitations on social media, Saturday evenings. Then French colleges: student exchanges, scholarships to study in India, seminars. The constitutional line for that room is **liberty, equality, and fraternity**, borrowed from the **French Revolution**.

Every major country does it. In New Delhi the embassies are not only talking to the government. On **Kasturba Gandhi Marg**, the **American Centre** is a library maintained by the American embassy, for Indian students, free against a student identity card. Weekend mailers invite them to festivals. The French and German embassies run **free** language courses. The Chinese embassy runs free language classes. The point is the same: attract the youth, and the future government hears it.

**UPSC has asked public diplomacy.** The chauvinism warning applies to the festivals as well.

## 5. What soft power cannot do (IR-07-03)

Compare the two, because the limits of soft power are the strengths of hard power.

| | Hard power | Soft power |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Tangible?** | Yes. Weapons and soldiers can be counted. So can **GDP** and foreign-exchange reserves. | No. It is **ideational**, a psychological idea. Attraction is an emotion. It cannot be counted in units. |
| **State control?** | Strict. The state knows how much it has, what it will do, and can spend it on the purpose it chooses. | No. Diffused. The state does not know how much it has, or whether it will work. It can only hope. |
| **How it acts** | **Coercive and compelling.** Other states can be forced to change policy. | **Voluntary.** Nobody can be forced to love yoga. |
| **Speed** | Immediate. | Years and decades. Respect is not a same-day result. |

His hard-power pictures: missiles, including **BrahMos**, that can be aimed at Pakistan’s territory; America using military power to force Iran off nuclear weapons; **Operation Sindoor** last year, terrorist camps at **9** places inside Pakistan destroyed **within 30 minutes**. The wider lock on that operation stays on the current-affairs note. The point here is only that hard power is immediate, and that this operation is not soft power.

The joke he used for the limit: a Prime Minister cannot take two kilograms of attraction from a cupboard and bomb a city with yoga and butter chicken. Soft power will not protect every interest.

Soft power is still worth having. It is a **public-relations** exercise, a brand, and a harmless one — yoga camps and food festivals do no damage — **unless it is bought by ignoring hard power**. Hard power remains more important. Equal weight is the minimum he asked; he then said hard power should get **more**. Over-reliance is the mistake **Nehru** made: too much idealism, and national security (**rashtra suraksha**) is lost. Yoga does not defend the state. Realism is the theory that puts weight on hard power. Idealism, or the liberal perspective, is the theory under soft power.

**Joseph Nye’s own remedy is smart power:** a mix of soft power and hard power. That mix is also the balance of idealism and realism. In an essay or a GS answer on soft power, the **concluding paragraph is always smart power**. That is the way forward.

Where soft power is enough, and where it is not, he drew with neighbours. **Sri Lanka:** the **Sinhala** community, the majority, follows **Buddhism**. India is the birthplace. **Ashoka** sent his own children to carry the message. On a recent visit the Prime Minister went to the **Mahabodhi** temple there for that reason. **Bhutan** is the same Buddhist link. **Nepal** is the Hindu link: a Hindu-majority country, **Pashupatinath** and **Janaki**, and an **aarti** at Pashupatinath by the Prime Minister. **Pakistan and China** are the hostile pair. Shared Punjabi culture will not return **Pakistan-occupied Kashmir**. Momos will not return **Aksai Chin**. A proxy war needs hard power.

**Viksit Bharat by 2047** is a hard-power project. A developed country is read off **GDP** and **per capita income**, not off yoga, culture, or cinema. The work is education, health, industrialisation, jobs, colleges, schools, hospitals, seaports, highways, railway lines, energy and self-reliance, manufacturing, exports. A **global power**, and a permanent seat on the **UN Security Council**, needs the same military and economic strengthening. “Largest population” is a valid argument and not a sufficient one.

**China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI)** was his example of smart power already in use. The hard edge: heavy investment in poor Asian and African states, and a debt trap when the loan is not repaid. **Sri Lanka’s Hambantota** port, taken on a **99-year** lease, stays on the China note. The soft edge: China says the scheme revives the old **Silk Route**, which is history and culture. India, he said, is attempting the same mix: a large soft-power effort, plus military and economic strengthening, with **2047** as the horizon.

## 6. India’s own resources, and the policies that spend them (IR-07-04)

Resources do not run on autopilot. Policy has to translate them.

**Diversity.** India is heterogeneous: religiously, culturally, and linguistically, with thousands of languages and dialects, and the communities coexist. That is **secularism** and **tolerance**, and it is a soft-power resource because it is a hard case. Secular values are easier in a **homogeneous** society. His picture of Europe, and of **France**, was one language, one culture, and mostly one religion. India’s model is different from the Western model: **sarva dharma sambhava**, equal respect and equal protection for all religions. Unity in diversity is the phrase he used. A heterogeneous society that still holds secular values is the case study.

**Buddhism** is the religion he singled out. Practitioners inside India are a minority. The birthplace is India, and the faith travelled. The sheet’s map: **South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, Central Asia**. Those histories are the resource. **Myanmar** is a Buddhist-majority country inside Southeast Asia.

**Culture beyond yoga.** He listed **Ayurveda**; a philosophical tradition — **Kautilya (Chanakya), Gandhi, Rabindranath Tagore, Swami Vivekananda, Dr B. R. Ambedkar** — whose ideas shaped people outside India; **Nelson Mandela** and **Martin Luther King** as Gandhians; cinema, including **Naatu Naatu** at the Oscars (say **Indian cinema**, not only Bollywood); cuisine and Indian restaurants abroad; the **diaspora**, engaged at events such as the one in **Texas**, and honoured at **Pravasi Bharatiya Divas**, with the chauvinism warning repeated; sport; and **higher education**, with students from **Asia and Africa** coming to Indian colleges. A student added **health tourism**. He accepted it in one line and did not develop it.

**Cricket** is a soft-power resource because India is the cricketing superpower, cricket is played in **30 to 40** countries, and the **Indian Premier League (IPL)** plus the star players pull that world towards India. Hosting a large tournament does the same work. India **aspires to host the Olympic Games in 2036**. **Paris** had the world’s attention two years before this class. Beijing had hosted the Games. He also pointed at the **next Commonwealth Games**, and did not name the city or the year. Do not invent either.

The objection “I hate cricket and Indian cinema” is an **insider** objection. Soft power is aimed at **outsiders**. Cinema is watched, on his list, in Afghanistan, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Southeast Asia, and Russia. Football does for **Brazil** and **Argentina** what cricket does for India: **Lionel Messi, Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, Pelé**. At a recent FIFA World Cup there was no Indian team, and the crowd still followed Argentina or Brazil. Messi had recently visited India. Hollywood, watched since childhood, is Western soft power consumed without noticing. Cinema reaches masses, and it works by **exaggerating reality** in order to entertain. A documentary is the place for unexaggerated life. He named the **National School of Drama** for that first lesson.

**What the ministries do with the resources**

- **Public Diplomacy Division** in the MEA, already above.
- **Indian Council for Cultural Relations (ICCR)**: a government-owned body for cultural exchange and cultural events abroad. That is **cultural diplomacy**. The MEA works with it.
- A **soft-power matrix**, which the MEA has **announced it is developing**, so that an intangible resource can be measured. He did not give a score, a year of publication, or the indicators. Do not invent them.
- **Incredible India**, a campaign of the **Ministry of Tourism**.
- **Project Mausam**, of the **Ministry of Culture**. It revives old **cultural** links with Indian Ocean countries whose historical trade followed the **monsoon** wind, because the ships ran on wind and the wind set the sea route. He is not reviving the trade. Trade would have sat with the **Ministry of Commerce** and **Piyush Goyal**. He placed the old routes at **500 years** and also at **1,000 years**. Those are his two spoken distances, not one locked date.
- **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)**: Buddhism as the link into Central Asian members. The organisation itself stays on the evolution note.
- **Nalanda**, revived as the ancient Buddhist centre of learning, with **scholarships for students of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)**.
- **Kushinagar Airport**, recently inaugurated, to serve **Buddhist tourism**. The **first flight** brought Buddhist monks from **Sri Lanka**. He gave no inauguration date.
- **G20, 2023.** Theme **Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam**, which he sourced to India’s history and the **Vedas**: the world is one family. Venue **Bharat Mandapam**. The same hall later hosted a **BRICS** summit, and its walls are decorated with **yoga asanas**. During that presidency India pushed the **Vishwaguru** narrative. A guru, in the culture he described, **mentors**. The word is not a claim to dominate the world. The mentoring he named is towards peace, prosperity, and stability.
- **Vaccine Maitri** during the pandemic: free vaccines. Already on **IR-06**.
- Gifts to foreign leaders, including at the recent BRICS summit: Indian **cultural artefacts**, a painting, a textile. Not a box of chocolates and a bottle of wine. The rule he wanted: **whenever culture or history appears inside a foreign-policy act, the act is soft power.**

He closed the topic on the same sentence as the theory. The programmes are good. They are not a substitute for hard power.

## 7. The UN budget, and the half of the statistic (IR-07-05)

A permanent seat needs more than population and more than soft power. The UN is large: a secretariat in New York, specialised agencies, funds and programmes — he named **UNESCO, WHO, UNICEF, UNDP, UNEP** — and peacekeeping. Members **voluntarily** contribute. **193** members.

He gave these shares of the UN budget:

| Contributor | Share he stated |
|:---|:---|
| **India** | **About 1%**. He then said **1.016%**. |
| **United States** | **22%** |
| **China** | **20%** |
| **Germany** | **8%** |
| **Japan** | He said **around 7–8%**, and also **6–7%**. Lock the band, not one figure. |
| **Brazil** | **Around 4–5%** |

**The United States, China, Germany, and Japan together contribute more than half.** On money alone, Germany, Japan, or Brazil has the stronger claim to a permanent seat. India’s answer is troops: the **largest contribution of troops** to peacekeeping. The financial share still weakens the case. A higher contribution would raise India’s weight, because the organisation depends on the large payers. The government knows the gap. The reason he gave for not closing it is capacity: **there is not the money.**

The statistic he called half-true: India is the **sixth largest** economy, a **$4 trillion** GDP. He first said “4 million,” then locked **$4 trillion**. Divide by **140 crore** people and the **per capita income** is among the lowest. He did not give the per capita figure. **Article 21** is the right to life and personal liberty. In **Maneka Gandhi**, the Supreme Court read life as a **dignified** life: adequate nutrition, shelter, and clothing. Feeding 140 crore, with crores still below the poverty line, is the burden. **Japan** and **Germany** have populations he put **below Rajasthan’s**, and a GDP as high as India’s or higher, so they have surplus to contribute. India has the same order of GDP and the developmental bill. Soft power can help. It is not sufficient.

## 8. Why a neighbour cannot be dropped (IR-07-06)

The syllabus line is India’s relations with its neighbours. He will take the countries one by one, with **Dr Sushant Varma**, across later sittings. Afghanistan, he said, Varma has already taught; this handout still carries an Afghanistan section for the next class. Before any capital, four questions: why the neighbourhood matters; what India’s goals there are; which official policy is being followed; what challenges that policy faces. This sitting answered the first, stated the goal, and named the policy. Implementation and the challenges are next.

No country becomes a **credible global power** while ignoring its neighbours. Neighbours get priority because they cannot be removed. It is an irreversible geographical fact. **Atal Bihari Vajpayee:** you can choose and change your friends and your enemies, but you cannot change your neighbours. Friends move. **Fifty years ago** India’s relations with **America and Israel** were terrible; they are friendly now. The United States and **Japan** were rivals in the Second World War and are friends now. A neighbour shares a border and therefore always touches the national interest.

The Afghanistan picture: discomfort with the **Taliban**, a record of sponsoring terrorism, and the memory of **1999**, when **IC-814**, an Indian Airlines aircraft, was hijacked to **Kandahar** under Taliban rule. Asking Afghanistan to relocate to Africa is not a policy. Like them or fight them, they remain neighbours. The only choice is to learn to deal with them.

Two extreme ways of dealing, and only the first serves the national interest.

| Cooperate | Fight |
|:---|:---|
| Resolve disputes peacefully. Friendly relations. **Multilateralism.** | Conflicts, border disputes, nothing resolved. **Unilateral.** |
| **Regional cooperation** in **South Asia**, through the **South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)** and the **Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC)**. | No cooperation. Wars, including the absurd calendar he sketched: one neighbour a year. |
| **FTAs** with neighbours. | No free trade. |
| **Peace, stability, and prosperity** in South Asia. A peaceful region is the prosperous one. | Resources depleted. Defence budgets rise. Education, health, jobs, industry, and research are what get cut. |

He said “technological” while expanding BIMSTEC. The name is **Technical**. **China is East Asia, not South Asia** — that lock stays on **IR-06**.

**Pakistan** does not fit the cooperative column. It sponsors terrorism, a peaceful settlement is very difficult, and a befitting reply is required. Pakistan gets a **separate** policy. He will teach it. The cooperative approach is for the **other** neighbours. Do not copy the Pakistan relationship onto all of them.

The handout’s first page carries the Prime Minister’s line, which he quoted: **a nation’s destiny is linked to its neighbourhood.** A troubled neighbourhood means forget peace, development, and growth. The same government has placed the **highest priority** on friendship and cooperation with neighbours. **Narayanan**, **National Security Adviser** while **Dr Manmohan Singh** was Prime Minister, said South Asia affects India **geopolitically, geostrategically, and geoeconomically**.

His evidence that wars with neighbours are a trap, not a strength:

- **China** has fought **zero** direct wars in the **last 40 years**. He set proxy wars aside. Fighting would deplete the resources that have gone into growth. War with **Taiwan** is available and is being avoided, because America would come in. He called Taiwan a **proxy of the United States**.
- America is trying to end the war with **Iran** — not because Iran is the stronger state, but because the resources are wanted for the contest with **China**. Trump has more than once announced a peace deal as nearly signed. Iran has not signed.
- America is likewise trying to wind down the war in **Ukraine**, which he called a proxy of the United States, armed by America. China, in his line, would prefer that war to continue, because it consumes American resources, and America’s real rival is China.
- America’s own neighbours are **Canada and Mexico**. It has not fought wars with them. That peaceful neighbourhood is part of how American growth happened.
- Before the two world wars the powerful states were the European colonisers. They destroyed each other — Britain, France, Germany, Portugal, the Dutch — and have not recovered that status. **American hegemony replaced British hegemony.** One reason is geography: America lay thousands of miles away, across the **Atlantic**. The war zone was Europe. **Not a single bullet** fell on the American mainland. The one attack he counted was **Pearl Harbor**, a naval base on an island in the Pacific, by Japan. While Europe fought, America built defence industry and **sold the weapons**. A peaceful neighbourhood (Canada and Mexico) meant there was no threat at home.

## 9. The cornerstone, and a neighbour’s crisis (IR-07-07)

The cornerstone of Indian foreign policy, and its objective in the neighbourhood, is a neighbourhood that is **strategically secure, politically stable, economically cooperative, and harmonious**.

**Strategically secure** means the neighbourhood strengthens the security of South Asia. Territories are not used to harbour and sponsor terrorism. **Politically stable** and **economically cooperative** answer the pattern he listed: neighbours repeatedly fall into **economic crisis** and **political crisis**.

Do not celebrate those failures. The false assumption is that their problem stays on their side of the border. A shared border means the instability **spills**. There are no iron borders.

- Economic collapse, poverty, hunger: people migrate, and India inherits a **refugee** burden. **1971: 15 million** from East Pakistan. That number stays on **IR-06**.
- Unstable neighbours become a **breeding ground for terrorism**. **Rajiv Gandhi** was assassinated because of **Sri Lanka** and the **Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE)**, not because of a problem born inside India. **IC-814** went to Kandahar while the Taliban ruled.
- **Nepal**, last year: **Gen Z** protests, violent, forced the Prime Minister to resign. He did not name that Prime Minister. An open border means a long crisis would push people into India.
- **Bangladesh, 2024:** a student movement forced the government to resign. Political chaos. Two years before this class, student protesters entered the Prime Minister’s residence.
- **Sri Lanka, 2022:** an economic crisis from **China’s debt trap** and from the pandemic, which became a political crisis. The government had to resign. Protesters entered the President’s residence, ransacked it, and were in the swimming pool. The videos circulated. A civil war, the LTTE, sits in the older Sri Lanka file.
- **Afghanistan:** the Taliban returned.

**Donald Trump**, in his previous term, built an iron wall on the **US–Mexico** border against drug smuggling and illegal migration. The problems did not stop. Smugglers dug under the wall. Migrants jump it. A soldier cannot stand on every inch. The **donkey route** is the same wall: Indians go to Mexico and cross illegally. Isolation from a neighbour’s problems does not work. The preference is stable, cooperative, harmonious neighbours.

## 10. Gujral, non-reciprocity, and Neighbourhood First (IR-07-08)

One instrument from the **1990s** is the **Gujral Doctrine**, launched by Prime Minister **I. K. Gujral**, to build cordial relations. Its principle is **non-reciprocity**. The hope was **trust** and **goodwill**, and through them the secure, stable, cooperative, harmonious neighbourhood.

Non-reciprocity: India is the biggest and the most powerful state in **South Asia**, exponentially more so than the small neighbours. The large state should also be generous. Help, and **do not expect a favour in return**.

Why generosity is self-interest: apply **balance of power** inside South Asia, as defined in Lecture 1. A balance does **not** exist there. The imbalance is huge, so the small states feel insecure. Their allegation is that India behaves like a **bullying big brother**. Confirm the allegation, threaten force, and the insecurity deepens. They will then try to balance India.

**Internal balancing** — matching India’s own capability — is unavailable to them. **Maldives** has a population of **5 lakh**. He set that against the examination: more than **5 lakh** people have filled the form. Maldives cannot build India’s capability. **External balancing** is what remains: an alliance with a state that can counterbalance India. That state is **China**. It is already happening. A growing Chinese role in the neighbourhood is the biggest problem. The **String of Pearls** stays on **IR-05**. The sheet’s two branches under BOP are these two methods; the second is marked **allies**.

The counter is to **address the insecurity**, not to feed it. That is non-reciprocity: be generous, create trust and goodwill. The **present government continues** the principle.

Neighbours come in two rings.

- **Immediate:** they share a border with India.
- **Extended:** they do not share a border; they are neighbours of the neighbours. He named **Southeast Asia, Central Asia, the Indian Ocean, and West Asia**.

India now gives the two rings **equal** importance, and has policies for the extended ring. **Southeast Asia:** **Look East**, now **Act East**. **Indian Ocean** island states: **MAHASAGAR**, which he expanded as Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth **Across the Region**. The locked expansion on the China note is **Across Regions**. The island names that are clear on the audio: **Mauritius, Madagascar, Seychelles, Comoros**. One word before Mauritius is not clear. Do not add a fifth country. Both policies are **later lectures**. This sitting stays with the **immediate** ring.

For immediate neighbours the official policy is **Neighbourhood First**, launched in **2014**. He said **2014** three times: first priority to neighbours, cordial relations, the secure and stable and cooperative and harmonious neighbourhood. **The sheet writes a year that reads 2022. Lock the spoken year, 2014.**

**Pakistan is not inside Neighbourhood First.** Pakistan has a separate policy, the **Doval Doctrine**, opened the next morning as **IR-08**.

He stopped there. The next morning opened the policy itself, the challenges, and Afghanistan from **page 5** of this handout. That sitting is **IR-08**.

The **soft-power matrix** is announced, not scored. **Kushinagar**: first flight from Sri Lanka, Buddhist monks. **Nalanda** scholarships go to **ASEAN**. **Incredible India** is Tourism.

India leads in **peacekeeping troops**. Sixth-largest economy, **$4 trillion**, **140 crore**, per capita among the lowest. **Article 21** via **Maneka Gandhi** is a dignified life.
