## 1. Space situational awareness (ST-10-01)

He also accepts the exam name **space domain awareness**. Same idea.

It is awareness of **all space objects**, and he was explicit that this means **both**:

- man-made objects, and
- natural celestial bodies.

A hit from either is equally damaging. The count runs into **millions**. Awareness has to be **real time**, around the clock, and it has to know two things: **where** the object is, and **how** it is moving — **speed** and **direction**. From that you predict a probable collision and fly a **collision-avoidance manoeuvre** in time. The manoeuvre itself, and ISRO’s **122** in fourteen years, stay Lecture 11.

The job is already hard when the motion is random. It is harder once some objects may be **thrown on purpose**. That is why awareness is also a wartime instrument, not only a debris instrument.

**How it is done.** The principle is **echolocation**, which is the **radar** principle. He used bats as the picture: an ultrasonic pulse goes out, the echo gives distance, and the animal turns until both ears hear the same intensity. That aim is an isosceles triangle, and then it strikes. For space, the same job needs a **group of radar antennae** on the **ground**, on **ships**, and on **satellites** whose work is to keep sending the waves.

**India’s group** is **NETRA** — **Network for Space Object Tracking and Analysis**. He said “network for space objects” and then landed on that expansion. Use the second one.

**Private SSA** is now open in India. The company he named is on the sheet as **Digantra Space**. The audio also came out as “Vikantra”. The firm meant is **Digantara**. Lock the sheet spelling beside the name, and do not build a second company out of the slur.

**Bodyguard satellites** were already on the Lecture 11 list. Here they are the craft that fly beside a strategic asset and keep scanning, so a deliberate attempt to hit that asset can be pinned to a country. The different ways a space war can be fought he postponed to a video. Do not invent them.

## 2. The September 2026 admission, and the holes in the 1967 treaty (ST-10-02)

Lecture 11 already had the United States’ weapons-in-space admission as public, and already had “the Moon is ours.” This sitting is the argument he wants written.

He dated the admission to **September 2026** and called it a **watershed** in space regulation. The wish to damage another country’s satellite is as old as the technology, the **1950s and 1960s**. Until this admission, charges traded between the United States, Russia, China and France were **denied**. Denial meant that putting weapons up was still treated as **morally undesirable**, whether or not anyone had already done it. The admission pulls that benchmark down. He expects later, open admissions from China or Russia, and a harder **arms race**.

For a country that spends its space money on **applied** missions, the shift is the painful part. Once defence in space is a compulsion, the share of the budget that used to go to civilian uses moves toward war preparedness. He named **India** as that kind of state.

**Why the Outer Space Treaty did not stop it.** International law, in his line, is studied more in the breach than in the observance. There is no enforceability. Lecture 10 already called the 1967 treaty **non-binding**. The three clauses he used today:

1. Outer space is the **common property of mankind**.
2. No nation may **appropriate** a space resource. You may use it, as you may use a swing in a park. You may not take the swing home. A sovereign claim is forbidden. **“The Moon is ours”** (Donald Trump, on X; **POTUS** = President of the United States) is the claim he set against this clause. He had already dictated the mains stem in Lecture 11: lunar ambition is not the same as joining a race.
3. No nation may place a **nuclear weapon** or any **weapon of mass destruction** on a **celestial body**.

**Lawfare** is the second way of misbehaving. The first is open violation. The second is to stay inside the letters and empty the purpose. He called the American news a clear case of lawfare. Read with it, from the day before, the report that **SpaceX** lifted **secret satellites** on a **Falcon** rocket. He left open the reading that the admission was a test of the reaction, and the satellites the act that followed.

The holes he wanted named:

| Hole | How it gets used |
|:---|:---|
| Use versus appropriation | Living off a resource can be dressed up as “use.” He treated this hole as real but less serious, because the stock is so large |
| **Conventional weapons** | The treaty prohibits nuclear weapons and weapons of mass destruction. It is **silent** on conventional weapons. Silence is being read as permission |
| **Where** the ban applies | The ban he used is on **celestial bodies** — Moon, Mars, an asteroid. It does not, on that reading, ban a weapon **in orbit**. “You can keep it in space. You cannot keep it on the Moon.” |
| **State** versus **private** person | The ban on appropriation is being read as a ban on **states** only. A private company, on that reading, may claim the resource. He called the reading nonsensical: a citizen’s title on Earth comes from the state, so the person cannot step outside the state and acquire a freedom the state is denied |

**United States and Luxembourg** have, he said, already passed laws that let **their private companies** appropriate celestial property. Material mined from a surface would belong to the company. That is one reason those states push a **private-led** programme. He added that **NASA’s budget has been declining for many years**. No figure for that decline.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** Lecture 11’s sheet used **OST** for **tourism**. Here **OST** is the **1967 treaty** again, as in Lecture 10. ## 3. Space tourism (ST-10-03)

He expects the topic under three heads only: **definition, types, significance**. Definition is tourism with the word space in it: **visiting space for recreation**.

Lecture 11 had already put a seat at **20 to 60 million dollars** for a few minutes, as a way to fund missions. This sitting is the typology and the balance sheet. The price he dictated for the suborbital queue is **20, 30 or 40 million dollars** a seat. Do not merge the two ranges into a new one.

| Type | What the passenger gets | When |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Suborbital** | Up, a look, and gravity pulls the craft back. **No orbit.** A few **minutes** — he said trips as short as **3 minutes**, and others of **6, 7, 8, 9 minutes**. A cheaper variant stays **close to the Kármán line** and turns back. **Virgin Galactic** goes to **70–80 km** | Happening |
| **Orbital** | The craft stays in **orbit around the Earth**. Lasts **days** | Happening |
| **Extraterrestrial**, also **transorbital** | To the **Moon** (orbit or landing) and, later, a **Martian** version. He joked that a honeymoon could become literal | **Future.** “Not anytime soon” |

The **100 km** Kármán line, and NASA’s roughly **80 km**, stay Lecture 10. He still filed the 70–80 km Virgin flight under suborbital tourism.

**For**

1. **Revenue**, especially for an agency that is short of money. Take it from people who have the money, and spend it on useful satellites.
2. **High-skilled jobs.**
3. **Parallel technologies.** The **Apollo** programme pushed **semiconductor chips**. Space work produced **Velcro**. A technology in one field leaks into others.
4. **Heritage.** Visitors of different nations see the Earth as **one commonly shared heritage**. His illustration was **Artemis 2**: astronaut **Victor Glover**, filming from lunar orbit, saying that from there the Earth is not black, white and brown but one humanity. You leave the Earth in order to see that it is shared.

**Against**

1. **Too costly** to be commonly affordable. Space is said to be everyone’s property, and most people cannot go and look at it. He also flipped the point: if it were cheap, the traffic would be unmanageable.
2. A **huge carbon footprint**, on an already fragile Earth system.
3. More **space debris**, from launch-vehicle components left behind.
4. **Health and safety.** Tourists are not trained for long, as professional crews are. The risk is what the bond they sign is for.
5. **Space law** for what happens up there **has not evolved**. His picture was two nationalities in one cabin and no settled rule for a dispute.

**The line he wants at the end:** the need is **sustainable space tourism**, which is a larger job than the tourism itself. The competition in space, and the law for it, is yet to mature. The sheet’s “need of the hour” is that sentence.

**Who flies people.** **SpaceX**, **Blue Origin** (he called this the **most frequent** provider), **Virgin Galactic**, and others coming up.

**Who has gone.** The first space tourist was an American, **Dennis Tito**, in **2001**. The only **Indian** space tourist he named is a **Telangana** resident, **two years** before this class. The audio is “Kopitan Tutakura”; the sheet is “Gopi Chand Tho Takra”. The person is **Gopichand Thotakura**. Do not invent the flight date beyond “two years ago.”

He then separated **Indian** from **Indian-origin**. A couple of Indian-origin people have also gone. One he placed **last year**, about **80**, an adventurer and real-estate investor. The name in the audio is **“Mr R.B. Singh Bahal”** and it did not land cleanly. Do not replace it with a guessed name. He used the story to say that private firms already sell patches of the Moon and Mars, that those deeds have **no force under earthly law**, and that people buy them for the boast. He mentioned a gift to Shah Rukh Khan and a patch linked to Sushant Singh Rajput. That is colour, not a statute.

## 4. Why the programme started when India was poor (ST-10-04)

He called the Indian programme the stage they would stay on after this. India began **early relative to its own poverty**, not early relative to the major space powers. The country was barely independent, and the decision was not unanimous.

The critics asked three things. Where is the money, in an impoverished state, for a cost-heavy sector? Where is the manufacturing and infrastructure a space economy needs? Why this, ahead of maternal and infant mortality, illiteracy, financial inclusion, unbalanced regional development, disasters, drinking water? Their fear was rational in one respect: early space work in the world was often a **symbol** used to out-compete someone else.

The political answer he wanted: space **expedites** the very jobs the critics listed. Agriculture, roads, disasters, financial inclusion, education, forests, resources, smart cities. This year’s **GS-I** question, the role of satellites in **climate-resilient smart agriculture**, is the example he pointed at. Lecture 10 already treats satellites and that kind of agriculture. Do not reopen it.

**Dual use** is the second reason, and he asked for it to be heard carefully. The same investment serves **peaceful** uses and **military** strength. A hostile neighbourhood made military growth a compulsion.

| Family | What he locked |
|:---|:---|
| Communication | **GSAT-6, GSAT-7, GSAT-7A**, for the **Army**, the **Navy**, and the **Air Force**. He was explicit that **GSAT-7** is the **Navy** satellite and is also called **Rukmini**, and that the **A** in **7A** is the **Air Force**. The Army mapping is the order of that sentence, not a second sentence of its own |
| Communication, again | **CMS-03**, a recent launch, for the **Navy’s maritime** communication |
| Earth observation | **Cartosat**, and **RISAT** (**radar imaging satellite**), for surveillance and imaging. He tied the lesson to **surgical strikes** and to what he called **Operation Sindhu**. The 2025 operation already locked in the IR notes is **Sindoor**. Do not create a second operation out of the slur |
| Navigation | **NavIC**, for precise **missile guidance** in war. He said it is **not functional now**, because India has **fewer than the minimum** number of satellites up. A **NavIC-3** launch he placed in the **middle of October**, about the **15th to the 20th** |
| The fleet | **Last year** the Cabinet approved **over 52** military satellites, **₹27,000 crore**. The military fleet moves from a **single-digit** count to many. **China** has nearly **300 to 400** |

Even without a battle in space, the **launch vehicle** is dual-use because it is a **rocket**, and so is a **missile**. One carries a spacecraft, the other a warhead. **A.P.J. Abdul Kalam** is the missile man of India and was **project director of ISRO’s first launch vehicles**. The Soviet **Sputnik** went up on an improvised missile. He mentioned, as something he had been reading, that Iran may be turning launch vehicles into missiles in the present crisis with the United States. That is an aside, not a system to memorise.

**Sarabhai Doctrine**, already Lecture 10: every rupee in space is for **nation-building and applied missions**, not for **prestige or power**. The sentence he dictated, as Sarabhai’s: there is no fantasy of competing with economically advanced nations in the exploration of the Moon or the planets or in manned flights; if India is to play a meaningful role in national development, it must be **second to none** in applying advanced technology to the real problems of man and society. He then answered his own question — are we still guided by it? — with **not exactly**, and left the proof to the second phase below.

## 6. Where a rocket should leave the ground (ST-10-06)

Three conditions, and he flagged them as Prelims material. Every point on Earth has the **same angular velocity**: one turn, **360 degrees**, in about **24 hours**. The **equator** is the longer circle, so a point there has the higher **surface** speed. A rocket leaving a moving Earth borrows that speed, as a person jumping from a moving bus borrows the bus. There is **no refuelling** on the way. Payload is the feature that matters, and any free push saves fuel.

1. **As close to the equator as is practical.**
2. **Eastward**, the direction of Earth’s rotation. Jumping against the bus is worse than useless.
3. **Eastern coast.** The path just after lift-off should not cross a populated region. A failure then falls in the **sea**.

**Thumba** looks like a breach. It is a coastal village in **Thiruvananthapuram** district, **Kerala**, on the **western** coast. It was not chosen for launch vehicles.

The **1960s** were, in his phrase, the decade of geophysics. The **magnetic equator** does not lie on the geographic equator. Along that magnetic equator, in the **ionosphere**, runs a stream of charged air he called the **electrojet** (he reached it by way of jet streams). The ionosphere bends the signals on which navigation depends, so it had to be studied. The tool is a **sounding rocket**, which goes **up** and comes back with instruments. You launch that rocket **from** the magnetic equator, so the vertical path meets the electrojet. **Thumba sits on the magnetic equator.** That is a positional advantage for **sounding rockets**, not for satellite launchers.

The station is the **Thumba Equatorial Rocket Launching Station (TERLS)**. If a question says “equator,” the trap is **which** equator: **magnetic**. Sounding rockets are **still** flown from Thumba. The magnetic-equator reason is **no longer** why. The dates of the first Nike-Apache (**21 November 1963**) and the first Indian Rohini RH-75 (**20 November 1967**) stay Lecture 10.

He said TERLS **was renamed** the **Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC)**. Treat that as his shorthand. VSSC is the centre that designs and develops the launch vehicles; it is the place the vehicles come from. After two recent **PSLV** failures, both of them strategic, **Ajit Doval** went there. Conspiracy talk followed. He said the problems are believed to have been fixed, and that **no official document** on the cause is out.

| Vehicle | Status in this class |
|:---|:---|
| **Satellite Launch Vehicle (SLV)** and **Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle (ASLV)** | **Not active.** He gave the full forms and said not to linger on them |
| **Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV)**, **Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV)**, **Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV)** | **Active** |
| **Next Generation Launch Vehicle (NGLV)** | **Under development.** He expects a question. It is India’s most capable vehicle-to-be, under **Project Surya**. **Cabinet approval last year** |

**Starship** (SpaceX) he called the world’s most capable vehicle, and he said its **first orbital** flight had just succeeded — “yesterday” as he taught. An orbital flight, in his definition here, is one that **places satellites in orbit**. It lifts nearly **1,50,000 kg** to the **lower orbit**. The comparison he made is **PSLV**, nearly **1,700 kg**. He added that no Indian vehicle is even a tenth of Starship. NASA’s human missions, he said, are being planned on this rocket. Do not replace 1,700 kg with another Indian vehicle’s payload.

He also floated the news that ISRO is **gradually giving up making launch vehicles**. What VSSC then becomes he left as an open discussion, and he told the class to watch his video on the evolution before the next sitting. The sales that make the news concrete are in §8.

## 7. Sriharikota, the dogleg, and a second site (ST-10-06)

**Sriharikota**, which he abbreviated **SHAR**, is the point chosen for the **launch station**. It is administratively in **Andhra Pradesh**, a **detached** piece on the **eastern** coast. The station is the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre (SDSC)**, India’s **primary** launch centre. “Primary” was his hint that a second centre is coming. Dhawan, he added, was an authority on **liquid propulsion**. The centre’s birthday was being marked around this class. No date locked beyond that.

Why this island:

- **Eastern coast**, so the early path is over the sea.
- **Detached.** Colonies were not allowed to grow. Launchers fail, as SpaceX’s fires show, and a dedicated islet limits the loss. A launch can still be a functional success if the satellite is already in orbit.
- **Climatically stable.** Discount the cyclones, and the site sits in the **rain shadow of the south-west monsoon**. A launch site should not be too windy, because wind disturbs the trajectory.

A **launch pad** is a concrete platform with towers, joined by a **rail link** — a link, not a rail network — to a **vehicle-assembly** building about **3 to 4 km** away, sometimes a little more. The assembled vehicle rides that line, is hooked to the towers, and lifts. He showed a drone view of **Kennedy Space Center**, Florida, as the same scheme.

**Two launch pads are active.** A **third** is being prepared. **Cabinet approval** for it also came **last year**. It will be larger, and it is for **NGLV**. It can also take **GSLV Mk III**, which he now calls **LVM3** (he said “GSLV-MART3” and “Launch Vehicle Mark-3”). LVM3 currently goes from the **second** pad. The third pad is **not** a PSLV pad.

**Dogleg.** An equatorial orbit from Sriharikota is a smooth eastward path. A **polar** orbit, flown straight, goes **over Sri Lanka**, and every launch would force disaster drills there. The rocket is therefore turned **around** Sri Lanka. That is a **dogleg manoeuvre**. It is a longer path and it **burns more fuel**. It is used for **polar** launches.

A site **further south** would enter a polar orbit without that bend. India is building one at **Kulasekarapattinam**, in **Thoothukudi** district, **Tamil Nadu**. The audio is “Kula Sagara Patinum” and “Tutikorin.” He said it should be **ready by the end of this year**, and that it will be **dedicated to polar satellites**. Polar launches are more frequent than non-polar ones, so once it is up it could be **busier than Sriharikota**. Because ISRO is stepping back from building the vehicles, this site is to be **handed to private companies**, after a period of handholding. That hand-over has already been in the news.

The sheet writes **62–68** beside this site. In the audio, **62 to 68 per cent** is how much **cheaper Falcon 9** is than India’s PSLV and GSLV charge per kilogram, and **Falcon Heavy** is **82 per cent** cheaper. Do not turn 62–68 into a property of Kulasekarapattinam.

## 8. Three phases, and the overload (ST-10-07)

The mains stem he dictated, to be written with his video: **trace the evolution of the Indian Space Programme. Comment on its present state, the changes, the policies, and the significance.** He teaches **three** stages. Some books use three and a half or four. He stays at three.

The programme began around **1962–63** as the **Indian National Committee for Space Research (INCOSPAR)**: research, and sounding rockets for the atmosphere. That committee became the **Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)** on **15 August 1969**. He called the choice of Independence Day symbolic. Headquarters **Bengaluru**. The work is split across centres. He named, without expanding them, **ISTRAC, MCF, LPSC, ISAC**, what he called **ZAC** (the centre meant is **SAC**), what he called **NRSE** (the centre meant is **NRSC**), and **NSIL**, besides VSSC and SDSC. Do not invent the expansions in an answer until a later class gives them.

| Phase | What changed |
|:---|:---|
| **First, till about 2000** | Sarabhai Doctrine. Taxpayer money only. **Design, develop, fund and operate** satellites and launch vehicles. Satellites at the start were **communication** and **Earth observation** only. Launchers were PSLV, GSLV and the rest. India was a **knowledge consumer**: known science, new applications, no new knowledge. The tag for that is **spacefaring** or **space-capable**. He said **over 18** nations hold it today. The sheet’s **430** is a mis-write. Do not learn 430 |
| **Inside the first phase: 1992** | **Antrix**, the commercial arm, from *antariksh*. Some goods and services were sold, so some revenue appeared. Not enough to take the programme off the budget. The audio put this “sub-centre” in a word that sounded like Israel. That is ISRO slurred. Antrix is not an Israeli body |
| **Second, from about 2000, and more precisely from Chandrayaan-1 in 2008** | To be called a **power**, India has to be a **knowledge producer**. Extra-terrestrial work: **Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan, Shukrayaan, Aditya**, the space station. **NavIC** satellites started going up in **2013**. This phase is **not** a clean Sarabhai Doctrine |

**Institutional overload** is the result of asking one government organisation to do four things at once: the **routine** (the same PSLV, the same communication and Earth-observation satellites, and the salaries), a **commercial** success while remaining a government body, the **exploratory** missions that also carry national pride, and all of it on a **limited budget** and a **limited workforce**. Overload shows up as **overruns of time and money**.

- The **cryogenic engine** took **20 years**.
- The **reusable launch vehicle** has been in development for the **last 10 years** with **not a single complete test**.
- **NavIC’s first generation:** **seven** satellites, launched from **2013 to 2018**. Design life **9 to 10 years**, so by the time the last was up, the first had lived about half its life. He said the constellation was nearly dead before civilians could use it. Nobody in the room uses NavIC on a phone. They use **GPS**. **Gaganyaan’s** delays are the same pattern, not a shortage of intelligence.

**The share of the economy.** Despite firsts he listed — Mars on the **first attempt**, and a landing close to the **lunar south pole** — he called the present an **existential** pressure. The global space economy is **much more than 400 billion dollars**; some experts put it at **more than 500 billion dollars**. It is growing fast. Lecture 11’s “under 20 per cent saturated” is why he thinks the growth is still ahead. The sheet also writes **more than 900 billion** and **1 trillion dollars**. The spoken decade-end sentence did not land a clean figure. Keep the spoken pair and the sheet pair **apart**. Do not average them.

India’s share is **2 per cent**. **Space Vision 2047** wants it **beyond 10 per cent**. He called that a **five-fold** jump, and ambitious. A larger share needs better technology, which needs more **research and development**. Research and development, in his line, is a luxury of a **cash-surplus** economy: it is **risky**, **costly**, and **slow**, and the money may not come back. The societies that get the technology are the ones that can afford to fail.

The programme was **government-owned and government-operated** for almost the whole of its life. The **Department of Space** sits under the **Prime Minister’s Office**, not under Defence and not under the science-and-technology ministry. So does the **Department of Atomic Energy**. That placement is the evidence of **strategic** value. A welfare budget then leaves space with what he called half a peanut, and most of that peanut goes to routine work, not to research. He showed a comparison slide of what the United States and China spend. He did not dictate a figure. Do not supply one.

The commercial edge India did have was a **cheap launch** on PSLV, from low labour cost. **SpaceX** undercuts that by **scale** and by **reuse**. Once the vehicle flies again, the price falls. He expects a day when launching on SpaceX is cheaper than launching on India’s own PSLV. The percentages are the ones in §7: Falcon 9 at **62–68 per cent** cheaper, Falcon Heavy at **82 per cent**.

## 9. Opening the sector, without calling it privatisation (ST-10-08)

The third phase is **liberalisation**. He was firm that this is **not privatisation of ISRO**, and that critiques which use that word are ill-informed. The prohibitions on private companies are being taken away. On his view of natural justice there was never a good reason for the ban. It belonged to an earlier phase.

The **decision** is **2020**. The document is the **Space Policy, 2023**. The sheet writes the arrow **2020 → Space Policy 2023**. Private companies may now offer **end-to-end** services. Earlier they were **vendors**: components, fabrication, paint. Now a company can be the service itself.

| Service | Firms he named |
|:---|:---|
| Launch | **Skyroot Aerospace**. **Vikram-1**, the same private vehicle Lecture 11 used for Mission Embrace, had its **first successful** orbital flight about a **month** before this class. Orders followed. **Agnikul Cosmos** |
| Satellites | **GalaxEye** (audio “Galaxy”) and **Pixxel** (audio “Pixel”). A GalaxEye satellite, **OptoSAR**, also called **Drishti**, was in **this year’s mains**. He read that as proof the private decision is already inside the examination |
| Situational awareness | **Digantara**, already in §1 |

**Bellatrix** (audio “Bellartix”) he spoke in the same breath as Skyroot and Agnikul. The sheet places it with the **satellite** firms, beside GalaxEye and Pixxel. Do not force it into one column.

**More than 400** start-ups are already registered, and **nearly 1,000** are lined up for a licence. The “more than 400” count is already on the Lecture 11 debris page. The new fact is the queue of about a thousand.

Opening the gate is not enough. The policy has to **enable**.

- **Foreign direct investment up to 100 per cent**, on the **automatic route**. No sectoral cap inside that sentence. Do not add one.
- **GST exemptions** and **import-duty exemptions** on imported items.
- A **technology innovation fund** of the Department of Space, **nearly ₹1,000 crore**. He first said the earliest beneficiary was “Dual Space”, then landed on **Dhruva Space** (audio “Druwal”). Use Dhruva, and treat the first name as a slip. Funding, he said, is now happening often. He showed clippings he did not dictate.
- **IN-SPACe** — **Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Center**. Constituted in **2019**. **Autonomous**, not a department of ISRO. It does two jobs that usually stay apart: it **promotes** and it **authorises**. It publishes the guidelines for launch vehicles, for situational awareness, for making satellites. A private project, **and an ISRO project**, goes through those guidelines. He played the Skyroot chief executive thanking IN-SPACe for authorising Vikram-1 quickly.
- **NewSpace India Limited (NSIL)**, from around the same time, is the opposite kind of body: a **centre inside ISRO**, and a **commercial arm**. A commercial dealing is **authorised by IN-SPACe** and **carried out by NSIL**. Antrix remains the **1992** arm. Do not merge the two companies.
- **Transfer of technology.** ISRO is selling most of the technology of its launch vehicles. An expression of interest he put on screen had an **August** deadline in **this year**, already past on the day he taught. A **parliamentary panel on science and technology**, reporting **this March**, red-flagged the price: technology sold cheap is **revenue foregone**. His answer, if that becomes a mains question: the aim is a **richer country**, not a richer ISRO. Cheap transfer is the price of a faster sector. **SSLV was sold to HAL last year.** PSLV and GSLV are being sold gradually. Kulasekarapattinam goes to private firms on the same logic.
- **People.** The shortage of professionals is, he said, the biggest present constraint. The one institute that trains them as space graduates is the **Indian Institute of Space Science and Technology**. He said “IISP”. The name already used in these notes is **IIST**. **Regional academic centres** have been opened inside existing colleges, as research centres: **NIT Kurukshetra, NIT Jaipur, NIT Patna**, a Guwahati university (audio “Gawad”), and **IIT (BHU)**. Beside those, **incubation** centres, which he called **STICs**, against an older set he called **RICs**. He did not expand either letter string. Incubation is the warmth that turns an idea into a start-up: industry, money, and the government, inside the college. **Agnikul Cosmos** came out of **IIT Madras**. **Digantara**, “if I am not wrong,” came out of **Lovely Professional University**. Keep the hedge on Digantara’s campus.

**What is still missing** is a **comprehensive space law** for commercial activity. The programme runs on **executive** decisions, which can change overnight. An investor wants a rule that Parliament must amend. He said the United States and almost every country with a private space industry already has such a law, including laws that let private firms appropriate. India has been drafting. Counting 400 or 1,000 start-ups is only the beginning. The test is the **order book**, and the government has to be the **biggest customer** while the law is still absent.

**The resignation memo is not a brain drain headline.** Reports, including one he attributed to NDTV, said **over 100** ISRO scientists had quit or retired early and that the Centre was tightening the exit. He called that shallow. Resignations are ordinary. The story became news when a **memo** appeared. It covers **Group A scientific and technical** staff linked to **Gaganyaan and other important missions**. For those people, **voluntary retirement and resignation may not be accepted as a matter of routine**. It is not a closed door. Earlier a centre director could clear an exit; now, for these projects, it is not automatic. The reason he gave is delay: Gaganyaan is late, and **Bharatiya Antariksha Station** is in a hurry. The debate he set, and then parked on his video, is **brain drain versus knowledge spillover** (he also said “brain circulation”). The sheet has the first pair. Do not write the spillover case until that video is in the notes.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** **NETRA** is the tracking network, not a satellite. **IN-SPACe** authorises and is **outside** ISRO. **NSIL** sells and is **inside** ISRO. **Antrix is 1992.** **ISRO’s birthday is 15 August 1969**, not 1962. **1962–63 is INCOSPAR.** **Thumba is the magnetic equator, for sounding rockets.** **Sriharikota is the east-coast launch centre.** **The third pad is for NGLV and LVM3, not for PSLV.** **Kulasekarapattinam is polar, and it is going to private firms.** **Liberalisation is not privatisation.** **100 per cent FDI is the automatic route, with no finer cap in this class.** **NavIC’s seven satellites took 2013 to 2018.** **The 2 per cent share and the “beyond 10 per cent” target are Vision 2047.** **Over 18 space-capable nations, not 430.**</span>
