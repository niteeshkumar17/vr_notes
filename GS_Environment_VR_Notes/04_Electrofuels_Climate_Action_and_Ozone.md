## 1. Electrofuels (ENV-04-01)

**Electrofuels**, or **e-fuels**, are lower-carbon fuels made from **green hydrogen** and **recycled carbon dioxide**. The example he dictated is **e-methanol**, used in the **shipping** sector. Chemically it is still methanol. He was writing **CH₃OH**. The audio landed as “CHGOH.” The “e” marks the **process**, not a different molecule.

Green hydrogen is the renewable route already taught: **electrolysis of water**. Recycled carbon dioxide is captured carbon dioxide that is **used**, not buried. A thermal power plant’s carbon dioxide is captured. If it is injected into sub-surface sedimentary rock, that is **geological sequestration**, which is **carbon capture and storage**. If the same captured gas is fed into industry, it is **recycled** carbon dioxide. A series of reactions then yields methanol. It is cleaner than a fossil fuel because the carbon dioxide in it would otherwise have entered the air.

That methanol can be turned into **ethanol**, and that ethanol is then **electro-ethanol**, for ethanol blending. It can also be turned into an **e-aviation fuel**.

Inside transport, cars can be electrified. **Shipping and aviation cannot.** Those two are the **hard-to-abate**, emission-intensive sub-sectors. Electrofuels are for them.

Three advantages.

1. They are **almost carbon neutral**, because production uses recycled carbon dioxide. Combustion still emits carbon dioxide. The name is “almost,” not “zero.”
2. They are **drop-in fuels**. A drop-in fuel goes into the tank of the **existing** vehicle with **no modification** of the engine or of any other part. The assembly line does not change, so the manufacturing cost does not rise. Ethanol in a car was his picture of a drop-in fuel.
3. They can **contain** the emissions of shipping and aviation.

He then closed the capture vocabulary from Lecture 2. Capture one tonne. Sequester half a tonne in rock, and use half a tonne for electrofuels. The whole operation is **carbon capture, utilisation and storage**. Utilisation, in this class, is the electrofuel.

The **Belém 4X** fuels are now all accounted for except biogas: **biofuel, biogas, hydrogen, and electrofuel**.

## 3. Three rounds of national contributions (ENV-04-03)

Every country has filed **nationally determined contributions (NDCs)**. The first were in **2015**, for the window that runs from **2020 to 2030**. When those were met, India revised them in **2022**. The revision is **NDC 2.0**, and he said it was **voluntary**. The Framework Convention did not force it. In **March 2026** the government gave **NDC 3.0**, for **2031 to 2035**. He told them to read 1.0 and 2.0 from the handout, and he dictated 3.0. He also walked the earlier numbers aloud.

**Emissions intensity of GDP** is also called the **carbon intensity of the economy**. It is emissions in a year divided by GDP in that year: how much is emitted to produce one dollar. For the ratio to fall, the numerator must fall or the denominator must rise, and in practice **GDP must grow faster than emissions**. India has been meeting the intensity targets **while emissions are still rising**, because GDP has been growing faster. When that happens, the economy is being **delinked** from emissions. He once said “relinking.” The term he wanted is **delinking**.

| Round | Non-fossil capacity | Intensity of GDP | Extra carbon sink |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| First, 2015 | **40 per cent** by 2030. Met, then revised | **33 to 35 per cent** below the **2005** level, by 2030. Met, then revised | **2.5 to 3 billion tonnes** of CO₂ through additional forest and tree cover. The **2030** date on that sink stays the 16 August note |
| **2.0**, 2022 | **50 per cent** by 2030. A **September 2025** notification said non-fossil capacity had crossed half. He called that **five years early**. The board now shows **54 per cent** | **45 per cent** below 2005, by 2030. He said this too has been achieved | The same sink, until 3.0 |

## 4. Panchamrit, and the schemes that are supposed to get there (ENV-04-04)

At **COP 26, 2021**, India announced five commitments. He called them **Panchamrit**. Some repeat the contributions. Some do not.

1. **500 gigawatts** of **non-fossil** capacity by **2030**. Non-fossil today is **300**. That is **200** still to add in about four years, and addition is not that fast. He called this **the only target India will not meet**. The audio said “non-energy.” He meant non-fossil.
2. **50 per cent of energy requirements from renewable energy by 2030.** He said this is **already done**. Keep his words. Do not quietly rewrite them as the generation figure. Generation is still mostly coal.
3. Reduce **total projected carbon emissions by 1 billion tonnes** from now to **2030**.
4. Reduce the **carbon intensity of the economy by 45 per cent by 2030**. Same ratio as emissions intensity of GDP. He said this has been achieved.
5. **Net zero by 2070.**

The developed countries, and he named the United States, the United Kingdom, and the European Union, said **2050**. China said **2060**. India said **2070**. He once slipped and said 2017. The year is **2070**. The common responsibility is net zero. The differentiated thing is the year. That principle stays the 2 October class.

He would not dictate every scheme. These are the ones he did.

**National Hydrogen Mission.** What matters is that India is building **green** hydrogen, the cleanest colour. Three likely outcomes he read off the handout: green-hydrogen capacity of at least **5 million metric tonnes a year**; an electrolyser target the audio recorded as **6,200 gigawatts**; and abatement of **50 million metric tonnes** of greenhouse gas a year. Set beside a **552-gigawatt** grid, **6,200 gigawatts** of electrolysers did not land cleanly. It stays as spoken. Check the handout before you memorise that one number.

**Ethanol** is already **E20**, and most of it is still **first-generation**. The generations stay the 2 October note.

**FAME India**: Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Electric Vehicles. The vehicles on the road now are **battery** electric vehicles. A **hydrogen fuel-cell** vehicle is the later possibility. The difference between charging from the grid and making the current inside the cell stays the 2 October note. Vehicular emissions are both a climate problem and an air-pollution problem.

**Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana**: subsidised **LPG**, clean cooking fuel, for the poorest households.

**Pradhan Mantri Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana.** The point is the **roof**. Households harness their own solar power. Surplus goes into the grid and the household can earn from it. It is not free. There is an installation cost, a subsidy, a maintenance cost, and some years before it breaks even. **More than 51 lakh households** are on it. **15 gigawatts** of rooftop solar has been commissioned. Apartments in large cities often have no roof to use. Independent houses in smaller towns do.

**Pradhan Mantri Surya Sarovar Yojana.** **Floating solar**, **5,000 megawatts**, which is **5 gigawatts**, on **lakes, ponds, and other inland water bodies**, by **2030**. Sarovar is not the sea. The life in those waters is **aquatic**. Ocean life is **marine**. A panel changes the **microclimate**, because the sunlight reaching the water changes. He said that life can adapt to a microclimate, and that it cannot adapt to **water pollution**, which is the larger problem of these water bodies. The priority, as he set it, is the pollution.

**Nuclear Energy Mission.** **100 gigawatts** of installed nuclear capacity by **2047**. Today it is **9**. He called that almost a tenfold rise in about twenty years.

## 5. Critical minerals and rare earths (ENV-04-05)

The **National Critical Minerals Mission** covers both **critical minerals** and **rare earth elements**.

His critical-mineral examples: **copper, cobalt, lithium, gallium, germanium**.

Rare earths he placed in **group 3** of the periodic table. His examples: **lanthanum, scandium, cerium, neodymium, and yttrium**. Yttrium is spelt with a Y. The audio first said “Pitium,” then corrected it.

They are called rare **not because the planet has little of them**. They can be abundant. They occur in **very low concentration**, so taking them out is technologically hard and economically hard. **Monazite sands** on the beaches of **Karnataka and Kerala** are rich in **neodymium**, and neodymium makes **permanent magnets**. The sand is everywhere. The neodymium inside one grain is not a coal seam. His contrast was an **open-cast coal mine in central India**, where the mineral is the rock you are standing in.

These minerals are what today’s deep technology runs on: semiconductor chips, and therefore artificial intelligence, renewable energy, electric vehicles, phones, defence, space, and medicine. He called them **the oil of the future**, more important than the oil of the last century.

India imports them and wants **self-reliance** in both groups. **China controls more than 60 per cent** of the supply chain of both. That is a geostrategic vulnerability. China did it by **buying mines abroad**, not only by what lies inside China. **Cobalt** is his example. Much of it is in a few African countries, and the **Democratic Republic of Congo** has some of the richest reserves. Those mines are owned by Chinese companies, private or public. Congolese people do the mining. He called the labour a human-rights violation, the profit Chinese, and the pattern **neo-colonial**: a **scramble for Africa**. The same minerals, he said, are why the United States talks about **Greenland**, and why a ceasefire proposal once asked Ukraine for control of the minerals on its territory.

One objective he opened, and he said not to copy the whole handout list: **processing and recycling**. Electronic waste, and solar panels at the end of their life, should be dismantled and the minerals taken back, not thrown into a landfill. The informal scrap trade already recycles a great deal of electronic waste. The mission wants that capacity built formally, and at scale.

## 6. Carbon markets, then the border tax (ENV-04-06)

**Carbon markets began under the Kyoto Protocol**, so that developed countries could meet **their** legally binding targets. Trading is in **carbon credits**, also called **units**. **One credit is one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent** reduced, removed, or sequestered. Any greenhouse gas can be traded, after it is converted by its warming potential. One tonne of methane is **21** tonnes of carbon dioxide. That 21 stays the 2 October class. A forest that removes one tonne in a year earns **one** credit. He later insisted that a **plantation is not a forest**. A forest is an ecosystem. An assemblage of trees is not one. Developed countries, he said, cut their natural temperate forests long ago, because that timber suited paper and pulp, and what they now call forest is mostly later planting. Saving a natural tropical forest has a social cost. Do not treat “plant some trees, sell a credit” as the same act.

**Emissions trading** under Kyoto was allowed **between any two developed countries**. His board:

| Country | Target | Achieved | Position |
|:---|---:|---:|:---|
| X | 10 | 8 | short by **2** |
| Y | 8 | 10 | surplus of **2** |

Y sells the surplus 2 to X. X pays. The **Framework Convention** keeps the book. After the sale the book says X reduced 10 and Y reduced 8. In the air, X reduced 8 and Y reduced 10. The planet still sees **18**. If Y had no buyer, Y would have stopped at its target of 8, X would still have managed only 8, and the planet would have seen **16**. The price has a **floor** set by the Convention, which is supposed to cover the cost of the reduction. Above that floor, demand and supply set the price.

He called this useful **in the short term**, because it pays a country to beat its target. The long-term line is that every country should carry its own responsibility. A rich country can otherwise buy its way out. Companies have also made a business of it: they pay small farmers to grow trees and sell the credit, and the farmer may not know a market exists. A steel plant that leaves thermal power for renewable power, and cuts its emissions from 10 tonnes to 2, has reduced **8 tonnes** and earns **8 credits**. The credit is the reduction, not the forest as such.

**Paris** has carbon markets too, and **developed and developing countries both** take part, because the goals are no longer only the developed world’s. **Article 6** provides **market and non-market** approaches.

- **Article 6.2**: **bilateral** mechanisms for carbon trading.
- **Article 6.4**: a **centralised** carbon market, administered by the **Framework Convention**.
- **Article 6.8**: **non-market** approaches, such as **technology transfer and knowledge sharing**. His example was India sharing solar technology with an African country and buying nothing.

India’s own market is the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme**. It is the market-based way to regulate and trade credits inside the **Indian carbon market**. The August note already uses that scheme as a trade reply. This class is where he named it as the domestic market.

**Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** of the **European Union**, and the **carbon border tariff** of the **United Kingdom**. The audio kept saying “water” and “C-RAM.” Both are a **carbon tax** on emission-intensive imports from countries whose climate policy they call relaxed. His list: **iron, steel, aluminium, fertilisers**, and later **cement, glass, and ceramics**.

They say the aim is to stop **carbon leakage**. Leakage is what happens when a strict climate policy makes an emission-intensive industry **move** to a country with a weaker policy. The emissions do not stop. They change address. Their net-zero year is **2050**. India’s is **2070**. Through their lens, 2070 looks weak. Through India’s, 2070 is the differentiated year, and India is meeting its own contributions. He said both descriptions can be held at once, and that the disagreement is real.

The economic version: cleaner energy raises the cost of steel and cement in Europe and Britain, so the plant may shift to India and stay on coal. The border tax protects that home industry. He treated **carbon leakage** as the politically presentable name, and the **safeguard of their own industry** as the purpose. India has raised it in trade talks with both. With the **United Kingdom**, the understanding he described is that if an Indian firm has already paid for credits under the Indian scheme, that cost is **factored into** the British levy. He said the rates and the arithmetic are more detail than the examination needs.

**COP 31** he placed in **late October and November**, with the decisions coming in **November**. Anything technical, he will teach. The rest is the newspaper. Do not fill the meeting from outside this class.

## 7. Vienna, Montreal, Kigali (ENV-04-07)

The **Vienna Convention** was adopted in **1985** to protect the **stratospheric** ozone layer.

The **Montreal Protocol** came into force in **1989**. It is **legally binding**. It **banned** the global production and consumption of **ozone-depleting substances**. He called it the **only environmental treaty with universal ratification**.

A **protocol** is a **legally binding supplementary agreement of a convention**. The convention is the parent. Kyoto sits under the Framework Convention. Montreal sits under Vienna. The **Paris Agreement is not legally binding**. If it were, he said, it would have been called a protocol. Kyoto was. Montreal is.

The depleting substances were replaced by **hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)**: **19 gases**, used in **refrigeration and air conditioning**. HFCs do **not** deplete ozone. **Fluorine** does not. **Chlorine and bromine** do. HFCs are still **fluorinated gases**, and **all fluorinated gases cause global warming**. So the 1989 switch protected ozone and began a climate problem. The Framework Convention did not yet exist in 1989. It is 1992.

Ozone is recovering. If the present pace holds, he said it will have **fully recovered between 2060 and 2080**.

In **2016**, the year after Paris, the **Kigali Amendment** was adopted in **Kigali**, the capital of **Rwanda**, at the **28th Meeting of the Parties** to the Montreal Protocol. That is a meeting of the parties, not a conference of the parties of the climate treaty. It **entered into force on 1 January 2019**. It is legally binding. India has **ratified** it.

The objective is a **phase-down** of HFCs, not a phase-out. Underline phase-down. The cut he settled on is **85 per cent**, by **2047**. The audio first mumbled “80 to 85 by the late 2040s,” and he then corrected the figures to **85 per cent** and **2047**. The replacement is the **hydrofluoroolefin (HFO)**. An HFO neither depletes ozone nor warms the climate.

The amendment follows common but differentiated responsibilities. The responsibility is the phase-down. The **timeline** differs. Three groups, as he read the table:

| Group | Who | Start | Finish |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| First | The richest countries | **2018** | The finish year did **not** land cleanly. The audio has “30 cents.” Do not invent it |
| Second | **China**, and the richer African users, his example **South Africa**. The poorest African countries are not using these gases | **2024** | **2045** |
| Third | **India**, and other hot countries of **South Asia and West Asia** | **2028** | **2047**, an **85 per cent** cumulative phase-down, stepwise |

A complete phase-out was not the 2016 deal. He said the American industry then held the **patents** on HFOs, so a forced switch would have hurt refrigeration industries such as India’s. The first cut was therefore 85 per cent, and time was bought. The United States ratified, in his telling, because that patent was a commercial interest.

## 8. Why the hole is polar, and only for a season (ENV-04-08)

**Stratospheric ozone** is the **good** ozone. It absorbs harmful ultraviolet light. In people that light means eye cataract, skin disease, and skin cancer. It also kills **phytoplankton**, the main producers of the oceans. Without them he sees no ocean life, and without ocean life no life on the planet.

Concentration is measured in **Dobson units**. It moves with season and latitude. The average he gave is **300**. The layer’s thickness in the stratosphere is **3 to 5 millimetres**. Depletion is a fall in that concentration, so the layer thins.

The substances were stable down in the **troposphere**. They did not react there. Air currents carried them up. The stratosphere supplied the conditions, and the reactions then ran fast. Ozone is the less stable arrangement, so it breaks if those conditions are present. His example of a chlorofluorocarbon was **Freon-12**, **dichloro-difluoromethane**. Ultraviolet light frees a **chlorine radical**, and that radical reacts with ozone. He wrote the equations so the class could see the radical, and he said the **examination will not ask the equations**.

Two conditions, **both at once**.

1. Extremely low temperature, **below −78 °C**, available in **polar stratospheric clouds**.
2. **Sunlight.** The equations have ultraviolet in them, and ultraviolet is sunlight.

So the reactions are at **polar latitudes**, in **late winter or early spring**.

Those clouds are **not weather clouds**. Weather stops at the troposphere. They are not condensed water vapour. They look like clouds and are accumulations of **nitrogen oxides**, seen only at the poles, which is why the name is polar. Peak winter at the pole is dark, so there is no sunlight and no reaction. Peak summer, the clouds are not cold enough, so again no reaction. The window is the turn of the seasons.

The **ozone hole** people learn at school is over the **South Pole**, because the loss there has been more severe. The same kind of loss happens over the **North Pole**. It stays at the poles because other latitudes do not get this cold.

## 9. Two smogs, and what the index actually counts (ENV-04-09)

**Primary** air pollutants are formed **directly**, from human sources or natural ones. **Secondary** air pollutants are formed by **chemical reactions between primary ones**.

Natural sources he named: **wildfires** and **volcanism**. Human sources: **industry**, and industry here **includes the energy sector**; **transport**; **agriculture**, and in India that means **stubble burning**; **construction and demolition**, a heavy particulate load; **burning of solid waste**; and **indoor** pollution from **traditional cooking fuel**.

Where fossil fuel or organic matter is burnt, the primary examples are **carbon monoxide** from incomplete combustion, **sulphur dioxide** from the sulphur in coal, and **nitrogen dioxide**. **Tropospheric ozone** is the secondary example.

**Smog** is two things.

**Photochemical smog** is **brown air**, or a **brown haze**. It is secondary. **Nitrogen oxides** react with **volatile organic compounds** in **sunlight**. Without sunlight the reactions do not run. The oxides are the three already defined in Lecture 2: nitric oxide, nitrous oxide, nitrogen dioxide. Their sources are the same combustion list. The organic compounds are primary pollutants, **hydrocarbons that vaporise quickly**, used in **petroleum products, paints, polishes, and dry-cleaning agents**. He named **benzene, formaldehyde, and chloroform**. A dry-cleaned garment or a fresh polish smells of them. In large amounts they irritate the eyes, and chronic exposure can cause cancer. Cities have both ingredients. The mixture contains many secondary pollutants. Two he named: **tropospheric ozone**, and **peroxyacetyl nitrate**.

**Sulfurous smog** is **smoke plus fog**, also called **grey air**, because the air looks dull. The smoke carries primary pollutants: **sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, and particulate matter of 2.5 and of 10**. Secondary pollutants can be in it too. It is the **winter** smog of **northern India**, when the ground is cold and condensation sits low as fog. Photochemical smog belongs to the season that has sunlight. The winter problem he named is the grey one.

The **Air Quality Index** was given by the **Central Pollution Control Board** in **2015**. It is a **weighted average of eight pollutants**. He had them write the **human** sources beside the handout. Natural sources for the particulates are the volcanism and wildfires already named.

| Pollutant | Human sources he dictated |
|:---|:---|
| **PM 2.5 and PM 10** | The same list: burning fossil fuels, which means industry and vehicles; construction; stubble; traditional cooking fuel; **unpaved roads** |
| **Nitrogen dioxide** | Burning fossil fuels; stubble |
| **Sulphur dioxide** | Burning fossil fuels; stubble; **metal extraction and processing**, his example **copper smelting**. Coal and many ores carry sulphur. The gas pollutes the air. The acid contaminates water and soil |
| **Carbon monoxide** | Incomplete combustion: industry, vehicles, stubble |
| **Ozone** | Nitrogen oxides and the volatile organic compounds, in sunlight. This is the **one secondary** pollutant in the index |
| **Ammonia** | Rotting **agricultural slurry**, meaning livestock waste; **waste-treatment plants**; **chemical fertiliser factories**; nitrogen fertiliser on the field. The Haber–Bosch process makes ammonia, and urea on a field releases it. The smell of decay is ammonia and hydrogen sulphide. Ammonia itself is pungent |
| **Lead** | **Leaded petrol**, and he said aviation still uses it; **lead-acid battery** manufacture; lead extraction and processing |

The statement “the index is a weighted average of **primary** pollutants only” is **false**, because ozone is in it. **Carbon dioxide is not one of the eight.** It is a greenhouse gas, not an air pollutant. He said prelims has asked both the list and that carbon-dioxide question.

The Board’s index runs from **0 to 500**. **Up to 100** the air is **satisfactory**. **Above 100** it is not. A private index, and he named **IQAir**, can print a number above 500. The government figure stops at 500. In a northern winter he put the value at **more than 400**. A shouted 5,600 was not the figure.

The **World Health Organization** annual averages he dictated: **PM 2.5, 5 micrograms per cubic metre**, and **PM 10, 15**. The sheet writes 10 beside PM 10. The speech said **15**. He called 5 and 15 too ideal for India. The Board’s own limits: **annual** average **40** for PM 2.5 and **60** for PM 10; **24-hour** mean **60** and **100**. In a peak northern winter the 24-hour reading can hit **999**, which he said is as high as that meter will go, and which he called poison rather than air.

## 10. Cleaning the air, and why the residue still burns (ENV-04-10)

The **National Clean Air Programme** is the government’s programme for the air of **more than 100 cities**. The objectives sit on the handout. He did not dictate a percentage. He dictated the steps he thinks the clean-up needs. Control, mitigation, and abatement he treated as the same job.

1. **Electric mobility**, with the stress on **clean public transport**.
2. **Construction and industry** actually complying with the air-pollution guidelines, enforced by the **State Pollution Control Boards** and the **Central Pollution Control Board**.
3. **Solid-waste management** across cities.
4. **Ex-situ and in-situ** management of crop residue.
5. **Tree planting** at a large scale across urban India.

Stubble burning runs from **October** and peaks in **November**. October is still warm enough for the pollution to rise, and if the wind is above about **12 to 13 kilometres an hour** it also moves sideways. In November the temperature in the north falls hard. The air no longer lifts the pollution, the wind stalls, and what has been emitted stays.

**In-situ** means **on the field**. **Ex-situ** means **away from the field**. He swapped the two words once. The definitions that followed are the lock. Away from the field, the residue can be a feedstock for **second-generation ethanol**, or for **biogas**. He said neither is happening now. The ethanol generations stay the 2 October note. On the field, the **Indian Council of Agricultural Research** offered the **PUSA biodecomposer**, as capsules, sprayed on the residue. It was supposed to rot the residue in **7 to 10 days** and leave manure. That window matters. After the rice harvest the farmer has about **7, 10, or at most 15 days** before wheat must be sown. The decomposer did not finish in the stated 7 to 10 days, so farmers did not use it. Machines exist, and they can be rented. Nothing is as fast or as cheap as fire. **NITI Aayog** once recommended a **minimum support price on the residue itself**, on the lines of paddy procurement. That was not done. He cited the government’s financial limit.

Burning destroys the **soil biota**, cuts **humus** and **moisture**, and the next season then wants more irrigation water and more fertiliser. His point was that water and fertiliser are subsidised, so the ecological bill is not the farmer’s bill. The older cause is the **crop**. **North-western India** is **semi-arid**. After the **Green Revolution**, in the **1960s and 1970s**, it moved to **rice**, which made sense when the country needed food. Fifty years later, with food security in hand, he said that region should move to crops that fit a semi-arid climate. A support price, once given, is politically hard to take back. He told them to read that day’s article and to join it to the agriculture notes. The article’s title was not dictated.

## Locks

- An electrofuel is **green hydrogen plus recycled carbon dioxide**. E-methanol is still methanol. **Almost** carbon neutral. **Drop-in** means the engine stays. Shipping and aviation are the hard pair. Using the captured gas is what turns storage into **utilisation**.
- **552 GW** installed. Fossil **252 (46 per cent)**, non-fossil **300 (54 per cent)**, nuclear **9**, solar **165**. **Third** in renewable capacity. **70 per cent of generation is still coal.**
- Intensity is **emissions over GDP**. India has met it so far because **GDP outruns emissions**, not because emissions are falling. **Delinking** is that comparison.
- NDC 3.0, March 2026, for 2031–35: non-fossil **60 per cent**, intensity **47 per cent** below 2005, sink **3.5 to 4 billion tonnes**, all by **2035**. The 50 per cent capacity mark was notified in **September 2025**.
- Panchamrit: **500 GW** non-fossil by 2030 is the target he said **will be missed**. Net zero **2070**. Rooftop: **more than 51 lakh** households, **15 GW**. Floating solar: **5 GW** by 2030, inland water. Nuclear: **100 GW by 2047**.
- Rare earths are rare because they are **dilute**. Monazite in **Karnataka and Kerala**, neodymium, permanent magnets. China holds **more than 60 per cent** of the supply chain. Cobalt’s example is the **Congo**.
- One credit is **one tonne of CO₂ equivalent**. Kyoto trading was **developed country to developed country**. X short by 2, Y ahead by 2, planet still at 18. Article **6.2** bilateral, **6.4** centralised, **6.8** non-market. The Indian market is the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme**.
- The European mechanism and the British tariff are a carbon tax on emission-intensive imports. **Leakage** means the factory moves. India calls the tax **protectionist** and against differentiated responsibilities.
- Vienna **1985**. Montreal in force **1989**, the only universal environmental treaty. Kigali **2016**, in force **1 January 2019**, **phase-down**, **85 per cent by 2047**. HFOs. India starts **2028**. Fluorine does not eat ozone. Chlorine and bromine do. HFCs warm the climate.
- Hole: **below −78 °C** and **sunlight**, together, so **late winter or early spring**, **poles only**. Average **300 Dobson units**. Thickness **3 to 5 mm**. Good ozone is stratospheric. Bad ozone is tropospheric, and it is both a greenhouse gas and a pollutant.
- Photochemical smog needs **sunlight**, and it is brown. Sulfurous smog is **smoke plus fog**, and it is the northern winter. The index has **eight** pollutants, includes **ozone**, and excludes **carbon dioxide**. Satisfactory is **up to 100**. World Health Organization annual: **5 and 15**. The Board: annual **40 and 60**, daily **60 and 100**.
- In-situ is **on the field**. Ex-situ is **away**. PUSA was **7 to 10 days**, and it missed that window. November traps the smoke because the air no longer lifts it and the wind stalls.
