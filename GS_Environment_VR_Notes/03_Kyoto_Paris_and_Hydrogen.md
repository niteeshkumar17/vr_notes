## 1. Kyoto, then Doha (ENV-03-01)

**COP 3, 1997, the Kyoto Protocol.** It fixed **legally binding** targets for the **developed** countries only. In the 1990s the developing countries were not yet large emitters, and the first assessment report had just said the change was real and was the work of the historical emitters.

The collective target was a **cumulative emission reduction of 5 per cent**, base year **1990**, over the five-year implementation window **2008 to 2012**. Each developed country had its own target. The United States, the European Union, Australia, and Russia were the names he used. The gap between 1997 and 2008 was **preparatory time** for the energy transition: technology, research, reskilling, and finance. They could start earlier. By 2012 they were supposed to have arrived.

**COP 18, 2012, Doha.** The **Doha Amendment** was an **eight-year** extension, **2013 to 2020**, with **revised** targets. The new collective cut was **18 per cent**, same base year **1990**. By 2012 none of the developed countries had met Kyoto. He calls Kyoto a **partial success** in the developed countries’ telling and a **partial failure** in the developing countries’ telling. Doha he calls a **complete failure**.

An agreement of this kind needs ratification by a **two-thirds** majority. Doha got that majority only in **October 2020** and entered into force in **December 2020**, the pandemic year, when climate action was nobody’s agenda. It was a formality. The United States **never ratified** Kyoto and has never committed itself. The European Union did something under Kyoto, then grew cautious: it would not clean up a mess the United States, the **single largest historical emitter**, refused to own. Till as late as **2020**, developed-country action had been inadequate. Three decades had been lost since the 1990 report.

## 2. Paris, and the 2018 special report (ENV-03-02)

**COP 21, 2015, Paris.** The post-2020 deal. By then **China** had overtaken the United States and was, and still is, the **largest** emitter. The **United States** is second. **India** is third. Two developing countries sat among the top five, so the future action could not be the developed world’s alone. That is the break with Kyoto.

The aim is to limit the rise in global average temperature to **2°C**, but **preferably 1.5°C**, by the **end of the century**, compared with **pre-industrial** times. Paris accepts that some rise is unavoidable. It refuses an unchecked rise. Both mitigation and adaptation follow from that.

He put the pre-industrial average at about **13.5°C**, and he also said the Holocene range is about **13 to 14.5°C**. A rise of 2° takes his 13.5 to **15.5**. A rise of 1.5 takes it to **15**. One sentence in the audio attached 13.5 to the year 2024. The comparison he was building is pre-industrial against the end of the century. Do not learn 13.5 as today’s temperature.

The commitments are **voluntary** and **self-determined**: **nationally determined contributions**. Countries filed them with the Framework Convention. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ran them through its models. If everyone met the first batch, the rise by 2100 was about **3 to 5°C**. The contributions were inadequate. The Panel stays out of the negotiation. In **2018** it issued a **special report**, not an assessment report, on **1.5°C**, to guide the next course. Two things have to be done:

1. A **cumulative emission reduction of 45 per cent** by **2030**, base year **2010**. The short-term target, all countries together.
2. **Net-zero emissions by 2050.** The mid-century target.

**Net zero** does not mean emissions stop. Growth needs them. It means **anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions are completely neutralised** by **carbon sequestration**: **sinks such as forests**, or **carbon capture, utilisation and storage**. His picture: industry emits five units and a forest takes five back; agriculture emits eight and geological storage takes eight back; the planet is at zero. **Carbon neutrality** is the same phrase. The capture method itself stays Lecture 2.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** Kyoto’s 5 per cent is **developed countries**, base **1990**, window **2008–2012**. Doha’s 18 per cent is the same base and a later window. Paris’s 45 per cent is **everyone**, base **2010**, by **2030**. Net zero by **2050** is the Panel’s mid-century line, not India’s year.</span>

## 3. Equity, the budget, and the money (ENV-03-03)

**Climate equity** is distributive justice in sharing the planet’s **carbon budget**, especially between developed and developing countries. **Parity of per capita emissions** is another indicator: the country’s emissions divided by its population.

**Carbon budget** is the total greenhouse gas humanity may still emit so that the rise stays inside the Paris targets. He walked the class off 1.5°C. The planet has already warmed about **1.1°C**, and 1.5°C will be breached in the next several years. The working ceiling he then used is **2°C**. Carbon dioxide’s residence time is **500 years to thousands of years**, so the budget includes the past, the present, and the future. The gas put up at the start of the industrial revolution is still there.

It is a **limited natural resource** and a **global common**. A resource, because emissions are the means of growth. Limited, because an unlimited release would push the rise past 2°C. A global common, because every country uses it and none can claim sovereignty over it. High seas were his comparison: territorial waters out to **12 nautical miles**, then the exclusive economic zone, then the open ocean, which carries ships, cables, and marine life, and which no state may fence. Atmospheric circulation spreads emissions the same way. They do not stay in the air above the country that made them.

A fair share, if every person is equal, follows **population**. He put the world at **8.3 billion**. Five per cent of the people should have five per cent of the budget. The record is the opposite.

| Who | What he said they have used |
|:---|:---|
| United States | **4 per cent** of the world’s people, **25 per cent** of the budget |
| North America | about **35 per cent** of the budget |
| European Union | about **30 per cent** of the budget |
| The developed world | **more than half** the budget, with **less than half** the people |
| The developing world | **more than half** the people, and **less than half** the budget |

The developed world’s growth, in his line, was paid for with the developing world’s unused budget.

On the handout table he did not dictate the tonnes. He dictated the comparison. India is the **third-largest** emitter, and its **per capita** emissions are about **one-fifth of the United States**, **one-third of China**, and **almost half the world average**. The United States and China sit **above** that average. The gap is the inequity: emission-heavy lifestyles in the developed world.

Through the **2000s**, China and then India were growing fast, and the conferences became a tug of war. Developing countries, with India and China in front, had three shared complaints.

1. The budget had already been used up, disproportionately, by the developed world.
2. Disasters in the tropics — cyclones, cloudbursts, droughts — were bringing **losses and damages**, and the science kept tracing them to developed-country emissions. Nepal’s own use of the budget he called almost nothing. He did not give a clean percentage.
3. Cutting emissions needs money, and the developing world’s budget is already spoken for: poverty, jobs, infrastructure, a decent life.

They agreed, under that pressure, to **voluntary** action, with no legally binding targets on them, **provided** the developed world paid. That payment is **climate finance**: funding by developed countries to **developing countries and least developed countries** for mitigation and adaptation. It was formalised at **COP 15, 2009, Copenhagen**. Before 2020 any voluntary sum would do. For **2020 to 2025** the developed countries together were to mobilise **100 billion US dollars every year**. We are past that window. In no year was the full 100 billion paid. The highest was **somewhere around 90 billion**. Worse, much of what was counted was **debt**, not **grant**. Debt has to be repaid. The developing countries call that a fudging of the compensation.

**Common but differentiated responsibilities** is a principle of environmental management from the **Rio Summit, 1992**. All countries are responsible for climate action. The responsibility follows the **damage** that country has done to the climate system. Common, not equal. On that test the developed world carries more. **This principle, and climate finance, are how climate justice is strengthened.** The August note’s longer phrase, with “respective capabilities,” stays that note. He did not add those two words here.

## 4. Two Indian ideas: the sun, then the grid (ENV-03-04)

He is teaching the initiatives in which **India led**. The dictation for the rest of the handout he left to the handout.

**International Solar Alliance**, at **COP 21, Paris, 2015**. Founding members **India and France**. France is there because it hosted the conference. The idea is India’s. **Sun-rich** countries collaborate so that by **2030** there is **1,000 gigawatts**, which is **1 terawatt**, of installed solar capacity. The world already has **more than 1 terawatt**, and **more than half** of that is with **China and the United States**, who do not need the alliance. He did not say they were barred. He said the point was the rest: South Asia, Southeast Asia, West Asia, Africa, and the sun-rich parts of Europe.

**One Sun, One World, One Grid**, also the **Green Grid Initiative**, at **COP 26, Glasgow, 2021**. Founding members **India and the United Kingdom**, the host. Again India’s idea. The aim is a **transcontinental interconnected power grid**.

Any energy source is reliable only if it is **affordable** and **available all day, every day of the year**. Fossil fuel still wins the second test. A coal plant, once built, burns stock in any season. Renewable power follows geography: no sun at night, a failed monsoon that leaves a reservoir short, wind that is not blowing. Solar is cheap now because it is **subsidised**. The sun is not over India all day. It is over the planet all day. When it is night in East Asia it is still day in South Asia, then West Asia, then Africa. An interconnected grid lets the day-side sell to the night-side.

India’s grid is already tied, regionally, to **Nepal and Bhutan**, and it takes their hydroelectricity. Transcontinental means that chain continues across Asia and into Africa. A second use is a **real-time mismatch**. His illustration, not a published surplus: **165 gigawatts** installed, a winter that needs only **150**, and **15** left over. Storing all of it needs battery capacity at that scale, and the cost of the solar plant plus the battery spoils the cheapness. Selling the 15 to a country that is short, his example **Sri Lanka**, needs the wire. India already has agreements toward **Singapore** and toward **West Asia**. The grid is only with **trusted** partners. He ruled out **Pakistan**. Africa, extremely sun-rich, is the large prize. **Mission LiFE** is on the handout and was postponed.

## 5. Biofuels, and why E20 arrived early (ENV-03-05)

**Biofuels** are fuels from **biomass**. The biomass, better called the **feedstock**, sorts them by generation.

| Generation | Feedstock |
|:---|:---|
| **1G** | **Edible** products rich in **sugar and starch**: sugarcane, rice, corn, millet, **cassava**. Cassava is tapioca. Sabudana is tapioca after processing |
| **2G** | **Agricultural and forest residue** |
| **3G** | **Microorganisms**, such as **algae** |
| **4G** | **Genetically modified** plants with a **high carbon** content |

The example is **ethanol**, chemically **ethyl alcohol, C₂H₅OH**, made by ordinary **sugar fermentation**. India **became E20 last year**: the mixture is **20 per cent ethanol and 80 per cent petrol**. Most of that ethanol, in India and in the world, is still **first generation**. Earlier it was sugarcane and rice. In the **last two years** the feedstock has shifted toward **broken rice and corn**.

The **Food and Agriculture Organization** raised the **food-versus-fuel** debate for 1G. About **10 per cent** of the world’s people face hunger, land is limited, and using it for fuel is, in his words, ethically wrong. He said the debate has **less force in India**: subsidised foodgrains cover the calories, and it is **not** the edible rice in the Food Corporation’s godowns that is being turned into ethanol. India’s own argument was **water**. Sugarcane and rice are highly water-intensive, and the gain in the air was being bought by emptying aquifers. Punjab wanted rice sown with the monsoon, so an Act delayed sowing. Harvest then collided with wheat, inside a window of about **7 to 10 days**, and the cheapest clearing was to **burn** the field. **NITI Aayog** has told the government that leaning on sugarcane and rice is unwise. Corn and millets are less thirsty. India is **not self-sufficient in corn**, so the new demand is met by **importing corn**.

Three advantages, and the first two were **economic**, not environmental. The **National Biofuel Policy of 2018** existed because there was a **surplus of sugarcane**: a second income for the farmer, and less crude to import. The environmental one is combustion. Ethanol carries a lot of **oxygen**, so the blend burns more completely and **carbon monoxide** falls. Carbon monoxide is a hazardous air pollutant, and in the air it can become carbon dioxide. He mentioned octane number and left it.

The controversy is the clock. The first target was **E20 by 2030**, and the manufacturers were building vehicles for that year. The government then moved the target to **2025**, five years sooner, without the automobile lobby in the room. Making the alcohol is easy. Making every part from the tank to the tailpipe tolerate E20 takes time. The cars were not ready: the fuel is **hygroscopic**, it **corrodes**, and maintenance, service, and mileage became the argument. The government denies much of what social media added. He said vehicles now coming off the line are compatible, so the mismatch should ease.

**Global Biofuel Alliance.** Founding members **India, the United States, and Brazil**. India announced it during its **G20 presidency in 2023**.

## 6. Glasgow, Sharm el-Sheikh, and what “21 times” means (ENV-03-06)

**COP 26** adopted the **Glasgow Climate Pact**. Most developed countries, at first **including the United States**, pledged **net zero by 2050**, the Panel’s year. **China said 2060. India said 2070.** The spread of years is common but differentiated responsibilities: a smaller emitter takes a later year. The worry he added is that the United States has made an **official exit from the Paris Agreement**. If it does not reach 2050, he does not expect anyone else to either.

**Methane pledge.** Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas. Its **global warming potential is 21 times** that of carbon dioxide. The potential is the **heat energy absorbed by 1 tonne** of a greenhouse gas **compared with 1 tonne of carbon dioxide**, over a **given** length of time. The length has to be the same for every gas. He used **100 years** as the working example. Carbon dioxide is always the reference, and its potential is always **1**. If carbon dioxide absorbs X and methane absorbs 21 times X, methane’s potential is **21**. **Nitrous oxide is 310.** Of the three, nitrous oxide is the most powerful, then methane, then carbon dioxide. You do not need X, Y, and Z. Agriculture is a large methane source, which is why the big methane emitters made the pledge. He did not dictate a percentage cut. The sources of the gas stay Lecture 2.

**COP 27, 2022, Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt.** A **loss and damage fund**. Climate finance already covers developing countries and least developed countries, and India is eligible. India cannot be lined up with **Nepal** or with a small-island state such as **Fiji**: India is the third-largest emitter and has itself damaged the climate system. Those two have caused almost none of it and pay heavily. The fund is climate finance **only for the most vulnerable**: **least developed countries and small island developing states**.

**Loss and damage**, the definition he told them to underline, is the **negative consequences of unavoidable risks of climate change**. His examples: rising sea level, a prolonged heatwave, desertification, acidification of the sea.

**Ocean acidification** is a **decline in the pH** of seawater, because the oceans have absorbed about **25 per cent** of anthropogenic carbon dioxide. The gas dissolves and makes a **dilute carbonic acid**. The average pH was **8.2** before the industrial era and is now **8.1**. The scale is **logarithmic**. A fall of 0.1 is not a 0.1 change. He called it about **30 per cent**. The water is still above 7. It is still called acidification because it has become less alkaline, and life survives less well. Fisheries are food and livelihood for island states and the least developed countries, so the loss is theirs. The **420 ppm** of carbon dioxide stays Lecture 2.

**Desertification** is the **extreme** of land degradation: the soil **completely** loses its fertility. Land degradation itself, a decline in fertility and productivity, was the previous lecture. This class adds the end of that road, and the harm to **terrestrial** biodiversity, agriculture, and the food and livelihood of the poorest countries.

## 7. Dubai, Baku, and Belém (ENV-03-07)

**COP 28, 2023, Dubai.** The **first global stocktake**: a review of everyone’s progress together, because 2100 is too far to wait. It is done **every five years**. The first was **2023**. The second is **2028**. Of the **G20**, the only country he said was on the right path to its **own** national contributions was **India**. What those contributions contain is a later class.

**Phase-down** means **reduction**. **Phase-out** means **elimination**. The pressure on India was to phase out **coal**. Coal is the economic lifeline: about **70 to 80 per cent** of India’s electricity is still coal-based. He said both “about 80” and “70 to 80.” Keep the range. India’s answer was that phase-down is under way, at a pace India sets, and phase-out is ruled out.

**Energy transition** is the move from fossil fuels — **coal, oil, gas** — to non-fossil fuels — **renewables and nuclear**. The same move is **decarbonisation of the economy**. India and China are the coal-heavy end. The United States and the European Union are heavier in **oil and gas**, because their own coal was used up early. Coal is the **most polluting** of the three. India does not deny that. It refuses to have coal singled out while oil and gas are spared. India **imports** oil and gas, and it imports coal too, because Indian coal is poor in quality. It still holds about **10 per cent of the world’s coal stocks**. He does not see coal leaving for the next **30, 40, or 50 years**. He read the European pressure as American pressure: the United States wants India buying American oil and gas, and he treated the sanctions on Russian oil as that commercial interest.

**COP 29, 2024, Baku, Azerbaijan.** The 2020–2025 window is over. Climate finance after 2025 has been renamed the **New Collective Quantified Goal**. The **International Energy Agency** and the United Nations, as he cited them, say developing countries and least developed countries need **5 to 6 trillion US dollars** from **2025 to 2030**, which he also put at about **1 trillion US dollars a year**. The developed world’s new promise is **300 billion US dollars every year by 2035**. India called it **too little and too late**. Whoever draws the money has to show it is for mitigation or adaptation. His example was Nepal justifying an early-warning system for a **glacial-lake outburst flood**.

**COP 30, last year, Belém, Brazil.** The audio landed as “Bilum” and “Bellum.” Forests are the symbol of that meeting, and the Belém package has forest points he did not open. What he taught is the **Belém 4X pledge**. The jump from fossil to non-fossil cannot be overnight. The fuels in the gap are **sustainable fuels**, because they keep the economy standing while the transition happens. Four of them: **biofuels, biogas, hydrogen, and electrofuels**.

**Biogas** is the gaseous mixture from the **anaerobic** decomposition of organic waste. That chemistry is already Lecture 2. **Electrofuels are the next class.**

## 8. Hydrogen is clean only at the last step (ENV-03-08)

Hydrogen as a fuel is **elemental H₂**. It is abundant, but on Earth it sits in compounds: **water** or **natural gas** (methane). The chain is **production, then storage and transport, then use**.

Use is **clean**: no emissions. Burning hydrogen does not make carbon dioxide. Storage and transport can have small, manageable emissions, depending on the truck. **Production** is where the processes split, so the gas is **colour-coded**. The gas itself is **colourless**. The colour is the method.

| Colour | How it is made | Emissions |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Brown, grey, or black** | **Fossil fuels.** Natural gas by **steam methane reforming**. Coal by **coal gasification**, a different equation he did not write | **Yes.** This is the cheap, simple route, and it is what countries mainly use |
| **Blue** | The **same** fossil route | The carbon dioxide is **partly or fully sequestered**. Cleaner than brown, and **costlier**, which is why sequestration is often skipped and the hydrogen stays grey |
| **Green** | **Electrolysis** powered by **renewable** electricity. A diesel generator does not count | **None.** The cleanest |

He put the reforming equations on the board so the emission was visible, and he said the examination does not need them.

**CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂**, at high temperature and pressure, and then **CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂**.

Electrolysis, only when the electricity is renewable: **2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂**.

**Green hydrogen** has three uses he listed.

1. **Decarbonise hard-to-abate industry**: steel, cement, fertilisers, oil refineries. Abatement means cutting emissions. These sectors are hard because they are extremely energy-intensive, so they have stayed on fossil fuels.
2. **Burn it in an internal-combustion engine** to make power. Unlike a fossil fuel, that combustion does not make carbon dioxide.
3. **Decarbonise transport** through **hydrogen fuel-cell electric vehicles**.

Industry has barely adopted it. Hydrogen is stored at **extremely high pressure**, and at that pressure it is **extremely explosive**. The cylinder material used for compressed natural gas is not enough. A **lithium-ion** battery vehicle, the kind he tied to Tesla, has to be **charged from the grid**, and India’s grid is still mostly thermal. It is still better than petrol or diesel for city air, because the burning is not happening inside the engine. A **fuel-cell** vehicle is different: **electrochemical reactions inside the battery make the current**. It does not take electricity from the grid and it does not need a charging station. The hydrogen-powered **train in Haryana**, launched recently, is this kind of vehicle.
