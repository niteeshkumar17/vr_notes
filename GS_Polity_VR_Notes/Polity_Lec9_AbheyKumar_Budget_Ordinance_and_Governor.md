## 1. The budget is an annual financial statement (POL-10-01)

**Budget** is not the constitutional word. The word is the **annual financial statement**, **Article 112**. The President **shall cause** it to be laid before both Houses. The President does not personally lay it. The same form is used for a State: the Governor shall cause the statement to be laid.

By convention the Union **Finance Minister** presents it. A Prime Minister has done it when a Finance Minister was not available. He did not land a clean list of those Prime Ministers. States keep their own practice. Karnataka’s Chief Minister presents the budget because that office holds the finance portfolio. He said Delhi’s Chief Minister will do the same next year. The Delhi name on the audio did not land cleanly. Do not invent it.

The date is also a convention. **1 February**, and only **from 2017**. Before 2017 it was the **last working day of February**. It was brought forward to gain about one month for discussion. It is 1 February, not the first working day. Even if that day is a Sunday. **1 February 2026 was a Sunday**, and the budget was still presented. The Finance Secretary sits in the gallery with the figures. The speech itself does nothing. It is a political message. Authority of law still needs a bill.

After the speech, the budget comes back as **two bills**. The **Appropriation Bill** is the expenditure. The **Finance Bill** is the revenue. Together they are the budget, and both are **Money Bills**. The Lok Sabha (LS) takes the Appropriation Bill first.

## 2. Demands for grants, and the three cut motions (POL-10-02)

A **demand for grant** is a ministry or a department asking Parliament for money. Parliament holds the Consolidated Fund, so Parliament decides. **This year there were 102 demands.** Last year also 102, unless a ministry is created or removed. Each demand is discussed and then **voted in the Lok Sabha**. The Rajya Sabha (RS) may discuss a Money Bill. It cannot reject one.

During that discussion a member may move a **cut motion**. It is a proposal to cut the demand. It is the tool of **financial control**, and it exists **only in the Lok Sabha**. His running example, not a real scheme: a department wants **₹50,000 crore** for a national answer-writing programme, one centre in each of 500 districts.

| Cut | What is questioned | How the motion reads | If it passes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Disapproval of policy** | The policy, not the amount | The demand **shall be reduced to rupee one** | Defeat of the government. The Prime Minister and the Council resign |
| **Economy** | The amount, not the policy | The amount **shall be reduced by** a stated sum (his sketch: ₹10,000 crore) | **Not** a defeat. They need not resign. It raises a doubt, so a **no-confidence motion** is then most likely |
| **Token** | Neither. A grievance | The amount **shall be reduced by ₹100** | A grievance is registered. Not a defeat |

₹1 is an insult, not a budget. Zero would have been a clean refusal. ₹100 off ₹50,000 crore changes nothing. The token cut is there so a member can record a grievance on a matter under the Council of Ministers. His sketch: twelve years in power, and still no centre in that member’s district.

**None of these has passed in about 80 years**, and he does not expect one to pass. A Council with a majority will not let its own demand be cut. The point is the debate. Control in this system is discussion, not the power to reject a majority. The sheet’s mark beside the economy cut is easy to misread as a formula. Lock the speech: an economy cut is not a defeat, and a no-confidence motion is the likely next step, not an automatic resignation.

## 3. The two bills, the guillotine, and the committees (POL-10-03)

Once the demands are voted, the **Appropriation Bill** is introduced. It is those demands written as a bill: demand 1, ministry, amount, and so on, **102 lines**. **No amendment to the grants is allowed**, because they have already been voted. The **Finance Bill** is taken up next. It is the revenue, largely the taxation proposals. **Amendments are allowed**, because this text has not been voted before. His illustration, not a rate he enacted: a member may move that a goods and services tax of 18% be made 15%.

The Finance Bill then goes to the Rajya Sabha on the Money Bill rules of Lecture 8. The most the second House can do is recommend, inside **14 days**. Assent, notification, and the budget process is over. The financial year is **1 April to 31 March**, so the new authority must exist by 1 April. Working backwards, a hostile Rajya Sabha still needs its 14 days, so the Lok Sabha has to finish nearer **15 March**.

**102 demands cannot all be discussed.** This year the House discussed **two**. Last year, **three**. The two were the **Ministry of Railways**, which is always taken because of the money, and the **Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare**. The sheet’s “not only railway” means exactly that second demand. Do not invent a railway exception.

Dates are fixed, so the Speaker closes the rest. **Guillotine** is an informal word. It is **not in the rules**. It is an instrument to bring **closure to all the pending discussion**. The pending demands are **clubbed and voted in one go**. It can close other business. Its popular use is the budget, because the dates are fixed. **Guillotine is weak financial control.** Control here is discussion, and the discussion has not happened. The reason is **lack of time**.

The break in the Budget session is not a holiday. It is for the **Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)**. There are **24**, each of **31** members. That size, and the **21 Lok Sabha plus 10 Rajya Sabha** inside each committee, stay in Lecture 6. This class adds a different split: of the 24, **16 work under the Speaker** and **8 under the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha**. Ministers are not on them. They divide the 102 demands, about four or five each, and they have about **four weeks**. Committee meetings are not in public view. The report, and the size of the money, decide which few demands also reach the floor. They generally choose about four or five, and the House manages two or three.

This year’s calendar, as he walked it. He said a date may be a day off. The sheet’s “19/5” beside the Appropriation Bill is not a May date to learn. He said **about 19 March**.

| Day | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| **28 January** | Budget session opens. **President’s address** |
| **1 February** | Finance Minister’s speech |
| About **13 February to 9 March** | Break. The 24 committees examine the demands |
| About **19 March** | Appropriation Bill passed. Already against the clock |

Before 2017 the session itself began only around 28 February. That is one reason it was brought forward.

## 4. Vote on account, Article 116 (POL-10-04)

If 1 April arrives and the budget is not law, the government cannot withdraw from the Consolidated Fund. The Constitution makers left a tool. **Article 116**, the **vote on account**.

**Only the Lok Sabha.** The Rajya Sabha has no role. The House authorises the executive to **continue to withdraw** money from the **Consolidated Fund of India** until the budgetary process is completed.

**Before 2017** this was needed **every year**, for **April and May**. The sum was **one-sixth** of the previous year’s budget: two months out of twelve, the same schemes and the same salaries, at the same rate.

**From 2017** the session starts at the end of January, so a vote on account is **not needed every year**. It has been used **in the election years**, and he counted that use as **twice**: **2019** and **2024**. On **1 February 2024** the Finance Minister still had to provide for the months of the election. Voting was in **April, May, and June**. The next government came in **July**. The full budget belongs to the government that comes after the election. What is presented on 1 February is temporary.

The popular name is an **interim budget**. There is no such word in the Constitution, any more than there is a word “budget”. An interim budget **is** a vote on account. No new schemes. The model code of conduct is already in the way.

## 5. Ordinance: Articles 123 and 213 (POL-10-05)

An **ordinance** is a law made by the executive, and it is temporary. The President’s power is **Article 123**. The Governor’s, for the State, is **Article 213**. The first two digits flip. The principles are the same. He taught the President.

**Satisfaction of the President** means the Union Council of Ministers. The Council must be satisfied that circumstances exist which need an **additional authority of law**: the existing law is not enough. And Parliament must not be in a position to legislate. If **either House is not in session**, Parliament cannot pass a law, and an ordinance can be made. If Parliament can still legislate, an ordinance cannot.

On **7 October 2026**, in that classroom, he said the President **could not** promulgate an ordinance. The session had **not been prorogued**. A sitting may not have been happening that afternoon. The session was still on. He read that as the government keeping the power to bring a bill suddenly. He did not name the session.

**Promulgate** means to bring into force. The power is **co-extensive** with the **legislative power of Parliament**. It extends to the same limit, nothing more and nothing less. The test is always: could Parliament have done this?

| Could Parliament? | Could the President, by ordinance? |
|:---|:---|
| Repeal an Act of Parliament | Yes |
| Make a law with effect from a back date | Yes, as a general rule. **Criminal law cannot be backdated.** Taxation laws mostly are |
| Impose a new tax | Yes |
| Amend the Constitution | **No.** That is **constituent power**, not legislative power |

Constituent power is the power the Constituent Assembly used: to write the Constitution, and so to change it, delete from it, or add to it. Parliament has assumed that power. The President cannot assume a power that is already an assumed power. An ordinance has the **same force as an Act**. The only difference is that it is **temporary**, because the recess is temporary. He said the Houses come back within a maximum of **six months**. That gap is Article 85, already in Lecture 7.

When the real law-maker is back, the ordinance is laid before Parliament.

- If they **pass** it, it becomes an Act.
- If they **disapprove** it, it ends **that day**.
- If they do **nothing for six weeks**, it **lapses**. Lapse means it expires. It is not “killed” by a vote.

## 6. Safeguards, and the three cases (POL-10-06)

Executive power is the easiest to misuse, so the safeguards are already in the design.

1. **Temporary.** Even a misuse lasts only until six weeks after the session.
2. **The Constitution cannot be amended** by an ordinance.
3. **Parliament can disapprove.** The ordinance has to be presented. If the Council hides it, members can move a motion to disapprove it.
4. **The rules of the Lok Sabha.** When the session resumes, the **concerned minister shall make a statement** explaining the circumstances.
5. **Judicial review**, last.

**A.K. Roy versus Union of India, 1982**, is the case for judicial review of an ordinance. The ground is **mala fide**: bad faith, bad intention. What is examined is the **existence of the circumstances**, and so the **satisfaction of the President**.

An ordinance becomes inoperative in four ways: the six weeks run out, Parliament disapproves it, the President **withdraws** it, or the court strikes it down. The only other path is that it becomes an Act. **Decisions taken and completed before it became inoperative do not become invalid for that reason alone.** They can still be invalid for some other reason. While the ordinance was in force, it was law.

It is **not a parallel power**. It cannot run beside Parliament. When the Houses can legislate, an ordinance cannot be made. It is also **not the legislative function** of the President. It is only a **legislative power**. Making law is the function of the legislature. The executive is filling a gap, which is the balance, and the legislature then checks it. The same idea as mercy in Lecture 3: a power may be judicial in nature, and the President still functions as the executive.

**Krishna Kumar Singh versus State of Bihar, 2017.** To **table** the ordinance before the legislature is a **constitutional necessity**. If it is not tabled, that **can be a ground** to declare it invalid. The court did not say it becomes invalid by itself. The case is a State case. The idea is the same for Parliament. His recent illustration of a doubtful use: an ordinance, which he placed **in May**, raising the Supreme Court from **34 judges to 38**. He did not see the urgency. An ordinance, in his view, is for a real emergency. The August current-affairs note already has a **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026**, on the same numbers. He did not identify the two. Do not treat them as one instrument.

**Re-promulgation** is the second problem. A session can be kept shorter than six weeks, so the ordinance is still alive when the House rises, and the next day it is promulgated again. **Dr D.C. Wadhwa versus State of Bihar.** The year **1987** is on the sheet. He did not speak the year. From **1967 to 1981**, fourteen years, the **Governor of Bihar** promulgated **256 ordinances**. Some were re-promulgated so often that they survived the whole period. **Not even once** were those ordinances presented to the Bihar legislature.

The holding, in the words he dictated: **re-promulgation of an ordinance without any attempt to seek legislative approval is unconstitutional.** The extraordinary power of promulgating an ordinance **cannot be used to bypass the legislative process**. Re-promulgation itself is not bad if the government tries to get the House’s approval. Using it to skip the House is what is wrong. People had started calling that an ordinance raj. The phrase he wants instead is the **rule of law**.

With the ordinance, he closed Chapters 1, 2, and 3 of Part V. Chapter 3 is only the ordinance. Chapter 4 is the Supreme Court, and he will not take the courts here. The judiciary is unified, so the Supreme Court, the High Courts, and the district courts are one later block. **Part VI**, the States, starts now. Only the differences from the Union are taught. The mirror is the rest: President and Governor, Prime Minister and Chief Minister, Lok Sabha and the assembly.

## 7. The Governor’s office, and B.P. Singhal (POL-10-07)

**Article 153:** there shall be a Governor for each State. The original words left an impression of one Governor, one State. The **7th Constitutional Amendment, 1956**, the year of the reorganisation of States, made it clear that **one person may be Governor of more than one State**. The Union territory idea of 1956 stays in Lecture 2. This class uses the year only for the Governor.

The **President** appoints the Governor, which means the **Union Council of Ministers**. **Article 156:** the Governor holds office for **five years**, **subject to the pleasure of the President**. That pleasure is the Union Council’s pleasure.

The office is dual. The Governor is the **head of the State executive**, and also the **representative of the Union in the State**. In the federal system, this is the office that links the Centre and the State. Appointment does not create a boss. The Governor is an **independent constitutional office**. **Not an employee** of the Union. **Not an agent** of the Union. Representative, yes. If the President stood above the Governor, the President would be the head of the State executive, and the Governor would not be a head at all.

Some Governors have behaved as agents. Last year, in Tamil Nadu, he named **Governor Ravi**, who stopped bill after bill. The allegation was that the Governor was taking directions from the Union. **Five judges** sat on it. The same friction had already appeared in **Punjab**, and in **Kerala**, whenever the State government differed from the Union government. The advisory reference and the minimum of five judges are already in Lecture 5. The holdings on assent are in the next section.

**B.P. Singhal versus Union of India, 2010**, is the case on this pleasure. The audio said “B.P. single”. The sheet writes Singhal.

- A Governor **can be removed without being given a reason**, and **without a show-cause notice**.
- Removal **cannot be without a reason**. The reason need not be told to the Governor. It must exist.
- The power is **extraordinary**, so it belongs to **extraordinary circumstances**.
- **Judicial review** lies for **mala fide**.
- If the court asks and the executive **does not disclose the reasons**, that silence is itself mala fide.
- Reasons that are **arbitrary, unfair, unreasonable, or whimsical** fail. Whimsical means driven by mood or emotion, not by reason.
- **A change of government at the Centre is not a valid ground.**

The pattern before the judgment was a wholesale removal when the Union changed. He placed that at the **2004** change, Bharatiya Janata Party out and Congress in. Those Governors went to court. The **2010** judgment is why, at the **2014** change, the new government could not remove Governors outright. His account of what happened instead: a phone call, and a request to resign by 5 p.m. Do not turn that phone call into a reported transcript of a named Governor. He used Governor Ravi as the illustration of someone who would resign if the Centre changed.

Ambedkar’s last speech in the Constituent Assembly, as he recalled it: the Constitution is only as good as the people who run it. An average constitution becomes great in the hands of great people. A great constitution becomes a disaster in the hands of the wrong people.

The line he wanted written, not the classroom story: **the Central government does not control the Governor officially. Its political leadership tends to select pliable persons for the post of Governor, and thereby develops alternative mechanisms of control. In the ultimate analysis, the Governor owes the appointment to the Central government.**

## 8. Assent after the 2025 reference (POL-10-08)

The case is a reference. The short title he wrote: **Re: Assent, Withholding and Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India, 2025.** “Re” marks a reference. The minimum of five judges stays in Lecture 5.

**Article 200** is the Governor’s assent. **Article 111** is the President’s, already taught. He offered a memory aid, not a rule that every Union article has a twin: the State article often sits **89** articles later, and that aid works up to Article 111. Mercy is 72 and **161**. Quorum is 100 and **189**. Assent is 111 and **200**. The Governor has one power the President does not, the power to **reserve** a bill, so **Article 201** is an extra article and the gap after that becomes **90**. Ordinance is 123 and **213**. State services ask these numbers. He does not expect the Union Public Service Commission to.

**Before the reference**, the reading of Article 200 was four written powers plus one unwritten one.

1. Assent.
2. Withhold assent, called an **absolute veto**.
3. Return the bill, called a **suspensive veto**.
4. **Reserve** it for the President’s consideration.
5. An unwritten **pocket veto**, on the same idea as the President.

A Punjab case, about three years before the reference, had a different reading. He said that case need not be known now, because the reference is final.

The Governor **may withhold**, and **must give reasons**. The court has treated this as a **discretionary** power. The President, on a Union bill, withholds on the advice of the Council. With reasons, the State legislature can repair the bill, so the withhold is **practically the same as a suspensive return**. **Withholding assent simpliciter**, a bare rejection with no reason, is the absolute veto, and **it is not allowed**. The underlying idea: an **unelected head cannot be a roadblock** to the legislative process of a democratically elected legislature.

The Governor **may reserve any bill**, and **any includes a Money Bill**. That too is discretionary.

**There is no pocket veto.** An unreasonable delay can be taken to court. The court may **direct the Governor to decide**. It may **not direct what to decide**. Judicial review here is **only on the delay**, not on the **merits** of the assent.

**Article 201** is a different situation from Article 111. The bill is a **State bill**. The “President” here is the **Central government**, in practice the **Ministry of Home Affairs**, not the individual who receives a Union bill.

- The President may assent.
- The President may withhold **with reasons**. **Simpliciter** is barred. There is **no absolute veto**. With reasons, it is practically suspensive.
- The bill comes back to the **State legislature**, which has **six months** to decide. He said this is the **only time limit** in the whole scheme. There is **no time limit** on the Governor or on the President.
- If the legislature passes the bill a second time, it is presented **to the President again**, not to the Governor.
- The second time, the President is **not obliged to assent**. The powers remain. A third or a fourth return is possible if it stays reasonable. An infinite loop of delay brings the court in.

**West Bengal’s anti-goonda bill** has been returned. He said this was not yesterday’s news. It was already some weeks old on 7 October. Do not invent the date, or a fuller title than the one he used.

## 9. Discretion: Article 163, Article 356, Article 371 (POL-10-09)

The constitutional position of the President, from the earlier class: a **constitutional head**, not a mere figurehead. Normally on the advice of the Council. Discretion is **implicit**, **situational**, and **not written**. The office is **largely** ceremonial, not totally. By design it is **more influence than power**. In a House with no majority, the President chooses who gets the **first chance** to prove a majority. The President does not choose the Prime Minister. The majority does.

**Article 163(1)** puts a Council of Ministers, headed by the Chief Minister, to aid and advise the Governor, **except** where the Constitution requires the Governor to act in discretion. The word **discretion is written** for the Governor. It is not written for the President. That contrast is already in Lecture 3. This class adds the design.

| | President | Governor |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Head | Constitutional head | Constitutional head |
| Discretion | Implicit, situational, not written | **Explicit**, intentional, written in Article 163 |
| Office | Largely ceremonial | **Equally ceremonial and functional** |
| Design | More influence than power | **Both power and influence** |
| Without the Council | Cannot function | **Can** |

With respect to the Council of Ministers, **the office of the Governor is far more secure, and far more powerful, than the office of the President.** The President cannot exercise an executive function without the Council’s advice, or against it. The Governor can. A caretaker Council is a Union practice. It is not necessary in a State. Under **President’s rule** the Council is dismissed, and the Governor runs the State with the Chief Secretary. The when and the why of that rule are the next class.

Every discretionary situation of the President is also available to the Governor, **except the pocket veto**, which the Governor does not have. He did not repeat the President’s list. The **additional** powers, which the President does not have, are these.

1. **Article 163(1)** declares that the Governor enjoys discretion. It does not list the powers.
2. **Article 163(2).** If there is a question whether a matter is discretionary, **that question is itself the Governor’s discretion**, and only **within the Constitution**. There is no question about the Governor deciding the State budget, or about making a Chief Minister against a clear majority.
3. **Article 200.** Reserve **any** bill, **including a Money Bill**.
4. **Article 356.** The Governor may, on their own, **invite the President to take over** the administration of the State. That is President’s rule. The when and the why are tomorrow.
5. **Convention.** A **fortnightly report** to the Union. Administration, new laws, law and order, or whatever the Governor chooses to write. The invitation under Article 356 can go in that report.
6. **Article 371**, and the lettered articles after it (**371A, 371B, 371C**, and the rest of that line). The Governors of **some** States have a **special responsibility** written into the Constitution. He named **Gujarat, Maharashtra, Nagaland, Manipur, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, and Karnataka**. For these matters the Governor acts on the **direction of the President**, meaning the Union, and **subject to those directions** may still decide what is not written down. The State Council’s advice does not run here. The one example he gave for someone who is not from the State: the **Governor of Gujarat**, the development of **Kutch and Saurashtra**. If you are from one of the seven, read that provision. Do not complete the other six from outside this class.

The State **Council of Ministers** is not different from the Union. The same **15%** limit, already in Lecture 4. Cabinet minister and minister of state, the same. Reforms of the Governor’s office he will give **in writing**. They were not dictated. Do not invent a commission’s list.

**Parked for the next sitting:** the **State legislature**, and then the **Union territories**. **Article 356** in full, the when and the why, is part of that next sitting.

- **Article 163** writes the Governor’s discretion. The office is far more powerful, against its own Council, than the President’s. **Article 371**: seven States, and only Gujarat’s Kutch and Saurashtra as the worked example.
