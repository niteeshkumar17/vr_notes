## 1. Who may contest — Article 84 (POL-08-01)

One article covers both Houses. The conditions are the same except age.

| | What he locked |
|:---|:---|
| Citizen | Of India |
| Age | LS **not less than 25**. RS **not less than 30** |
| Oath | Must **subscribe** to the oath in the **Third Schedule**, as a **candidate**, before the election |
| Leftover window | Any other qualification **Parliament adds by law** |

The law in that window, for this class, is the **Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951**.

**Oaths are stacked.** A candidate takes one. A member takes another (Speaker Pro Tem administers that one — Lecture 6). A minister takes **two** more: oath of **office**, and oath of **secrecy**. His illustration: **Narendra Modi** has taken **four**; **Rahul Gandhi**, not a minister, has taken **two**. A losing candidate has still taken the candidate’s oath. The oath’s core, as he put it, is true faith and allegiance to the Constitution.

### Where you must be a voter (RPA 1951, not a second age rule)

| House | Registered as a voter |
|:---|:---|
| **LS** | In **any** LS constituency in India. Vadodara does not stop a contest from Varanasi. His example is Modi: voter in **Gujarat**, seat in **Uttar Pradesh** |
| **RS** | Also in **any** LS constituency. RS has **no** constituency; the State is one unit |
| **Assembly** | In **any** Assembly constituency **of that State**. The same Gujarat voter cannot contest a Uttar Pradesh Assembly seat |
| **Council** | Same State rule |

**National election → registered anywhere in the country. State election → registered anywhere in that State.**

Originally the RPA said an RS candidate had to be a voter **in that State**. Changed in **2003**. ***Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India* (2006)** upheld the change. Two locks from this sitting, which Lecture 6 did not have (the open-ballot half of the same case stays on **POL-07-02**):

1. The domicile rule was in the **Act**, not the Constitution. Parliament made it and Parliament could change it.
2. The duty is to **represent** the State, not to **be from** it. It is an indirect election. The Members of the Legislative Assembly decide whether the person can represent them.

## 2. Disqualification — Articles 102 and 103 (POL-08-02)

**Qualification** = you must **possess** these factors, or you are not allowed to contest. **Disqualification** = you must **not** possess these factors. If you pick them up later, you go. So disqualification binds the **candidate and the sitting member**. Article 102(1) says it in one line: disqualified **for being chosen as**, and **for being**, a member.

He told them to read 101, 102, 103 in order. **Article 100** is quorum (Lecture 5). The grounds sit in **102**.

### Clause 1 — five grounds

| | Ground | His reading |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **(a)** | **Office of profit** under the Government of India or a State | Saved if Parliament by law says that office shall not disqualify. Section 4 below |
| **(b)** | **Unsound mind** | Only if a **competent court** has declared it. Not a political label |
| **(c)** | **Undischarged insolvent** | A **discharged** insolvent is eligible. Insolvent = assets have fallen below liabilities. The **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code** is the name of that law; he did not open it |
| **(d)** | Citizenship | Not a citizen of India, or has taken a foreign citizenship, or has acknowledged allegiance elsewhere |
| **(e)** | Any other disqualification in a **law made by Parliament** | That law is **RPA 1951**. Section 5 below |

**Explanation — the minister trap.** For the purpose of **this clause only**, a minister of the Union or of a State is **not deemed** to hold an office of profit. He is an office of profit. The explanation exists only because parliamentary government draws ministers **from** the legislature (Lecture 4). Without the explanation, clause (a) would throw them out. For every **other** clause and article, a minister **is** an office of profit. “A minister is not an office of profit” is the wrong sentence.

### Clause 2

Disqualified under the **Tenth Schedule**. The Constitution does not use the words “anti-defection law.” That is popular usage. Section 6 below.

### Article 103 — who decides clause 1

If a question arises whether a member has attracted **Article 102(1)** — **not** clause 2 — it goes to the **President**, and the decision is **final** in the sense he has used before: it will be acted on. Before deciding, the President **shall** obtain the opinion of the **Election Commission of India (ECI)** and **shall act according to that opinion**. Binding. This is the first decision in this series that the President takes on advice **other than** the Council of Ministers. The Council cannot advise here, because its own members may be the people under question.

**Article 101**, as far as he opened it today: **no dual membership**. One person cannot sit in two Houses, Centre or State (LS, RS, Assembly, Council). That is why the legislature-party circles in section 6 do not overlap. He did not open resignation or the sixty-day absence rule.

## 3. Office of profit (POL-08-03)

Neither the Constitution nor the statute defines it. Courts have built the meaning, and he said the tests are still evolving. This is not a closed formula.

**His definition.** An office or position **other than a political post**, filled by **election or nomination**, for which the person draws a **profit from the government** (Union or State).

Three words, in that order:

| Word | Lock |
|:---|:---|
| **Office** | Separate from the person. **Droupadi Murmu** is the person. **President of India** is the office. If you cannot tell the post from the person, there is no office, and so no office of profit |
| **Profit** | **Net gain**, not a company’s revenue minus cost. Money is not required. **Pecuniary** gain counts: a rent-free house, a car, free petrol, a driver. Influence, power and position can be a gain |
| **Under the government** | Private profit is not the problem. He used a business fortune (Vijay Mallya) as the contrast. The harm is coming **under executive control** |

Root, already in Lecture 2: **separation of powers**. Members exist to check the executive. They cannot sit under it.

**Tests.** If the answer to **any one** of these is yes, he treats it as an office of profit under the government:

1. Does the **government appoint**?
2. Can the **government remove**?
3. Does the **government pay** the remuneration?
4. Is the **source of the funds** under government control, even if someone else hands the money over?
5. Can the government **control how the office does its work**?

***Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India* (2006).** She was an RS member (Samajwadi Party) and chairperson of the **Uttar Pradesh Film Development Corporation (UPFDC)**, appointed by the Uttar Pradesh government. She had taken **no salary and no facility**. The Court **upheld the disqualification**. The question is whether the **office** can give a profit, not whether **she** took it. The “₹2 lakh a month” in the transcript is his sketch of a salary the office could pay. He said he did not know the real figure. Do not learn 2 lakh.

Same year as Dr **A.P.J. Abdul Kalam** returning the office-of-profit exemption bill (Lecture 2, the National Advisory Council). He connected the year. He did not say Kalam personally disqualified her. Parliament can still, by law, declare an office **not** to disqualify — that is Article 102(1)(a)’s own window, and it is what that 2006 bill tried to use.

## 4. RPA 1951 — sections 8, 8A, 9, 9A, 10, 10A (POL-08-04)

Same grounds for the Centre and the States. He said learn the **map**, not a section-number question. The periods are the trap.

| Section | What | For how long |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **8** | Crime. **General rule:** convicted and sentenced to imprisonment of **2 years or more**. Some listed crimes disqualify even **below** 2 years. He did not open that list | The jail term **plus 6 years**. A 5-year sentence is 11 years on this arithmetic. If the term is cut (mercy, good behaviour) the “plus 6” runs on the **reduced** term |
| **8A** | **Corrupt practice** in an election. His example: a lift, water, juice — not otherwise a crime, but an attempt to influence a voter. The election chapter will define the list | **Not fixed.** Not **more than 6 years**, set by the **President on the ECI’s advice**, after a court has found the practice |
| **9** | **Dismissed** from government service for **corruption** or **disloyalty** to the State | **5 years** |
| **9A** | Holds a **government contract** (a road, bottles, security — any contract) | The **whole contract**. After it ends, the bar ends. He called this office of profit in a private shape |
| **10** | A post in a **government company or corporation** (manager, secretary, managing director) | While the government holds **25% or more**. **Below 25%** is not this section |
| **10A** | **Fails to lodge** the account of election expenditure. Winners and losers both must lodge it | **3 years**, and the seat goes if they had won |

**Do not merge two percentages.** RPA section 10 draws the line at **25%**. Under the **Companies Act**, a **controlling stake is 26%**, because some decisions need **three-fourths** of the shareholders, and 26% can block that. Different statutes.

**2024 LS expenditure caps**, as he stated them: **₹95 lakh** in the biggest constituencies, **₹75 lakh** in the smaller ones. The cap depends on the size of the seat and is revised. These are the last general-election figures he gave, not a permanent number in the Act.

**While an appeal is alive.** He said a High Court, and then the Supreme Court, can **suspend the conviction**, and the person contests again in the meanwhile. That is why convictions do not empty the House at once. He marked it as an election-reform problem, not as a ground he was adding.

Election petitions and disqualification disputes of this kind: **High Court**, then the **Supreme Court**. Lower courts do not have that jurisdiction. If a **Chief Minister** is the person convicted, the Council goes with the Chief Minister. The others can be ministers again. The convicted person cannot come back while the disqualification lasts.

## 5. Tenth Schedule (POL-08-05)

Added by the **52nd Constitutional Amendment, 1985**. Not in the original text. About forty years on, still disputed. He named the **Raghav Chadha** episode as the recent argument, and **Aaya Ram Gaya Ram**: **Gaya Lal**, a Haryana legislator, changed parties **three times in one day**.

**Defection** = **crossing the floor**. In Britain the ruling side sits on the Speaker’s right and the opposition on the left, so the phrase is physical. In India, changing party is only one example. An independent has no party to change.

**Why the law exists.** Point 1, the one he wanted first: the voter chose a **party policy**, or an **independent’s own policy**. A later switch is disloyal unless the person goes back and takes a **fresh mandate** in a by-election. Point 2: **stability** — horse-trading, resort politics, money. The text is aimed at **unethical** defection, which means an **ethical** defection is possible. The exceptions below are that ethical slice. They are still defections. They do not disqualify.

**Who is covered.** Every **member** of a legislature, Centre and State, elected or nominated. Prime Minister, Leader of the Opposition, Speaker, nominated RS member — yes, because each is a member. The **Chairman of the RS is not a member**, so the law does **not** apply to him. He still **decides** RS cases.

| Term | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| **Original political party** | The party on whose **ticket and symbol** the candidate stood, as the **ECI certificate** records it |
| **Legislature party** | That party’s members **inside one House**. The unit of the law. What happens in the original party, outside the House, is not this law |
| **Leader** | One member of that legislature party. His examples for this House: **Narendra Modi** leads the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) legislature party in the LS; **J.P. Nadda** leads it in the RS (the audio came through as “Nanda”); **Rahul Gandhi** leads the Congress legislature party in the LS |
| **Whip** | Not a word in the Constitution. Two uses: the **member** who issues the direction, and the **direction** itself. The Constitution’s phrase is **direction of the party**. A large group may have a chief whip and deputies. The job is discipline on the floor: attendance, voting, how long they speak |

His figure for the BJP legislature party in **this** LS: **240**. That is this House, not a permanent strength.

### Grounds — different for three kinds of member

| Member | Disqualified if |
|:---|:---|
| **In a legislature party** | **Voluntarily gives up** the party. Resignation is the clear case (Raghav Chadha leaving the Aam Aadmi Party). Courts have also treated **anti-party activity** as giving up membership. That phrase is subjective; the presiding officer applies it |
| Same | **Votes or abstains against the direction**, and the party has **not condoned** it **within 15 days**. If the whip was to abstain, a vote in favour is the breach. Parties often condone, because a lost member is a lost vote |
| **Independent** | **Joins any party.** Voting as they like is allowed. There is no whip. Joining an ideology is the breach |
| **Nominated, already in a party** when named | Treated as a member of that legislature party. The party’s count goes up by one. The same two grounds apply |
| **Nominated, not in a party** | May **join within 6 months**. Joining **after** 6 months is a ground. His example: **P.T. Usha**, a sportsperson nominated to the RS, not from a party. He said she had already sat about **three years** and had not joined |

The 6 months is a **line**, not a magic length. He said three months or a year would have been the same kind of line. The point is a reasonable time to choose an ideology, against the risk of a late deal. Day 1 after six months is on the wrong side of the line they drew.

### Exceptions — ethical, so no disqualification

**1. Merger.** The Tenth Schedule’s text, as he read it: the **original political party merges** with another, **and** the legislature party **agrees** by **not less than two-thirds** of its members.

The dispute is how to read those two steps.

| Reading | What it requires |
|:---|:---|
| **Conjunctive** | Original party first, legislature party second. One without the other fails |
| **Disjunctive** | The legislature party’s two-thirds, **alone**, is enough. The original party need not have merged |

**No Supreme Court judgment** has closed this. **Presiding officers have consistently used the disjunctive reading.** His example: the Aam Aadmi Party’s RS legislature party had **10** members; **7** (more than two-thirds) decided to merge with the **BJP legislature party in the RS**; the original party had **not** merged. On the presiding-officer practice, that merger stands. He called the suitcase version of the same fact the reason the reading is easy to misuse. Do not turn 7 of 10 into a Supreme Court holding.

**2. Presiding officers.** Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman, Deputy Chairman. If they **resign the party on being elected**, and **do not join another party**, they are not disqualified. Neutrality sits above party loyalty. Resigning and then joining another party is **not** this exception. His sketch: a Speaker who resigns and then joins the Congress has given up the neutrality.

**3. Split — removed.** Originally, **not less than one-third** of the legislature party could break away and form a new party. Too easy, especially in a group of five or six. Removed by the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003**. That is the **same** amendment that capped the Council of Ministers at **15%** of the LS (Lecture 4). **No split is protected now.**

### Who decides

| Question | Decides |
|:---|:---|
| An LS member | **Speaker** |
| An RS member | **Chairman** (the law does not apply **to** him) |
| The Speaker himself | A **member elected for that question only** |

**Paragraph 7** of the original Schedule said the decision was final and could not be questioned in any court. ***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992)** struck that down. **Judicial review** is basic structure. Hollohan was a member of the **Nagaland** Assembly; Zachillhu was its Speaker. The Speaker had disqualified members and said the Court had no power. The Court said the presiding officer acts as a **tribunal**: not a judge, but using a judicial power (a **quasi-judicial** body). Tribunals sit under the courts. The **High Court** reviews, and the Supreme Court after that. The sheet spells the case; the audio came through as “Kehoto Holohan versus Zakilu.” Lock the sheet’s spelling.

### Reforms he actually discussed

These are arguments, not the law. The handout’s longer list was not taught. A time limit on the Speaker’s decision, he said, is a small repair, not the change that matters.

1. Give Tenth Schedule disqualification to the **President**, on the **ECI’s** opinion, as in Article 103. Two reasons: the Speaker’s **neutrality is questionable** (an allegation, not his finding that they are biased), and a politician is being asked to **interpret** the Schedule. Speakers have said they do not want the power.
2. Extend the same loyalty logic to **pre-poll alliances**. A party walking out of an alliance is not defection today.
3. Use the whip only where the law’s purpose is at stake: **stability of the government**, and a **manifesto** being turned into a bill or a proposal. His stability examples: **no-confidence**, **confidence**, a **Money Bill**, and the **Motion of Thanks**. On everything else, let the member vote. He previewed that defeat of a Money Bill means the Prime Minister resigns, and said the budget class will open that. Do not build it here.

## 6. Sessions — Article 85, and one special sitting under Article 352 (POL-08-06)

**Article 85.** The President **summons** (on the Council’s advice) so that **more than six months** do not pass between sessions. **Two** sessions a year would meet the Constitution.

**By convention** there are **three regular sessions**, plus special sessions. The words “regular” and “special” are **not** in the Constitution. Regular means it happens every year.

| Session | When he placed it | What is fixed |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Budget** | Late January to about mid-April. He said the exact day changes and is not the point. Longest | The **most important**, for two reasons below |
| **Monsoon** | Generally July–August. This year’s sitting ran hardly **19 days**, about three weeks | **No fixed agenda.** Whatever the country is arguing. This year he named a **paper leak**. Last year he said “operations” and did not name one |
| **Winter** | November–December. Coming soon. Shortest; sometimes hardly **10 working days** | No fixed agenda. He wants it read as a **year-end review** (more money, or spending that has to be cut) |

The Budget session is the only one whose agenda cannot move. If the budget is not passed, the government cannot spend.

**Why the Budget session is the most important — two reasons, not a list.**

1. **President’s address** to **both Houses together**, on the first day of the first session of the year. The President speaks. The **Council** writes it: last year’s achievements, this year’s proposals. The Houses then discuss it in their own chambers and pass a **Motion of Thanks**. In the LS he treats that as **as good as a confidence motion**: the ruling side has shown it has the numbers, before any other business. It is not Rule 198’s no-confidence motion (Lecture 6).
2. The **budget** is introduced and passed. He called the budget a **policy statement**. Once it passes, expenditure can be incurred, and the next year’s work is set.

When he spoke, the Monsoon session had been **adjourned sine die** by the Speaker and had **not yet been prorogued**.

**Special session.** Still summoned by the President on the Council’s advice. It may sit **outside** the three, or **inside** one of them. It is called for a **specified business**, and **only** that business is taken. No Question Hour, no Zero Hour.

This year’s example, already on Lecture 6 as a failed bill: the **last three days** of the Budget session were kept for **women’s reservation and delimitation**, the **131st Constitutional Amendment Bill**, which **failed**. That stretch was a special session **inside** the Budget session.

**Special session of the LS under Article 352** — discontinuing a **National Emergency**. This fills the third special power of the LS from Lecture 6.

- At least **one-tenth** of the LS members give **notice in writing** that they intend to move a motion to **discontinue** the emergency.
- If the LS is **already sitting**, the notice goes to the **Speaker**. If it is **not**, the notice goes to the **President**.
- Whoever receives it must summon the sitting **within 14 days**.
- The **only** business is that motion.
- It passes by a **simple majority**. Putting an emergency on is hard. Taking it off is easy.
- If it passes, the **President shall revoke**. Nothing ends by itself. The President has to **issue the order**. The LS can force that order.

Do not mix this one-tenth with the **quorum** of one-tenth in Article 100. Different articles.
