## 1. The assembly is necessary. The council is a choice (POL-11-01)

The Constitution **envisages a bicameral** State legislature. Bicameralism is **not compulsory**. When there are two Houses they are the **Legislative Assembly** (Vidhan Sabha) and the **Legislative Council** (Vidhan Parishad). When there is one House, it is the assembly. The assembly is necessary. The council is a choice.

The assembly is the mirror of the Lok Sabha (LS). The council is **not** the mirror of the Rajya Sabha (RS). The class is about the council.

**Six States** have a council, and their names are in **Article 168**: **Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana**. The sheet writes “MP” in that list. He said **Maharashtra**, not Madhya Pradesh. The audio said “Delangana”. That is Telangana.

**Article 169** creates or abolishes a council. The process starts in **that State’s assembly**. The resolution needs **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, and also a majority of the total membership**. Parliament may then, **by law**, create or abolish. The resolution is **necessary and not sufficient**. Without it, Parliament can do nothing. With it, Parliament may still do nothing. **Rajasthan** has passed such a resolution. **Assam** has passed one. Parliament has not made the law. Adding or deleting a name changes the Constitution, and that change is an **ordinary law**, not a constitutional-amendment bill. Article 2 or 3 is the other example he named, already in the territorial lectures. Parliament’s own majority, where the Constitution is silent, stays the simple-majority line in Lecture 5. He did not re-teach it.

The assembly’s own size is a guideline: **not more than 500, and not less than 60**. Parliament decides the exact number **by law**, and that law **can go below 60**. A later, smaller State cannot be forced up to the number an earlier State already holds. **Goa has 40. Mizoram has 40. Sikkim has 32. Puducherry has 30.** The sheet’s 90 beside Goa and Mizoram is not the number he spoke. Do not learn 90.

A council **shall not exceed one-third of the assembly, and shall not be less than 40**. That too is a guideline. The law that creates the council fixes the exact number.

## 2. Five ways into the council (POL-11-02)

Divide the House into thirds, split one third into sixths, and split one sixth into twelfths. Five groups. The Constitution says **as nearly as may be**, because a House does not always divide cleanly.

| Share | Who sends them | The lock |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **1/3** | Elected **by the MLAs** of that State | **Not from among themselves.** They vote. They cannot contest. Dual membership is already forbidden |
| **1/3** | Elected by the **local bodies** | Municipalities and district boards are the examples. The law of that State names the bodies. A water-supply body or an electricity body can be one. It is not “only the municipality and the panchayat” |
| **1/12** | Elected by the **graduates** | A **graduates’ constituency**. The voter has held a graduation certificate for **at least three years** |
| **1/12** | Elected by the **teachers** | At least **secondary-school** grade. Primary teachers do not vote. It is not a geographical constituency. The sheet’s “Monday” is this grade, not a day |
| **1/6** | **Nominated by the Governor** | That means the State Council of Ministers. The fields he named: **arts, science, literature, social service, and cooperatives** |

The RS nomination list does not carry **cooperatives**. These fields are guidelines. A cooperative is an organisation whose **members are also the owners**. His example was Amul: the person who brings the milk is paid for the milk and also shares the profit. He mentioned a new taxi cooperative in passing. The name did not land cleanly. Do not invent a brand.

Where there is an election, the method is the **proportional representation system by the single transferable vote**, the same method as the RS, plus the nominations. He did not re-teach the count.

## 3. The council cannot stop the assembly (POL-11-03)

Where the State has only an assembly, that House decides. Where a council also exists, there is **no joint sitting and no deadlock**. The assembly is the boss. A constitutional-amendment bill does not arise in a State. A Money Bill, and a financial bill that is already known to be one, cannot be introduced in the council, on the same logic as the RS. What can start in the council is an **ordinary bill**. The Type 2 puzzle, a bill that becomes financial only during debate, he set aside as another thing.

**A bill that starts in the council** and is then passed comes to the assembly.

- If the assembly **passes** it, it goes to the Governor and can become an Act.
- If the assembly **rejects** it, the bill **ends**. An RS rejection can still lead to a joint sitting. A council rejection, when the council was the originating House, does not.

**A bill that starts in the assembly** and then reaches the council. A **Money Bill** is exactly the RS rule: recommendations inside **14 days**. For an **ordinary bill or a financial bill**, the council has four choices: pass, reject, recommend amendments, or take no action.

- If it **passes**, the bill goes on to the Governor.
- If it **rejects**, the bill returns to the assembly. The assembly may drop it. If the assembly **passes it a second time**, it returns to the council. A second rejection means the bill is **deemed passed by both Houses**. If the council then does nothing, wait **one month**, and it is deemed passed. Discussion is allowed. The decision is already the assembly’s.
- If it **recommends amendments**, the bill returns to the assembly. A second passage by the assembly brings it back, and the council again has **one month**.
- If it takes **no action**, wait up to **three months**. The same bill then goes back to the assembly. A second passage brings it to the council, and **one month** of non-consideration deems it passed.

By its own legislative power, the council can **neither reject nor amend** a bill the assembly has passed. It can only try to convince the assembly. Its role is **revisory**: debate, review, and a wider set of people than the assembly alone. Small States do not need one. Goa’s assembly is already 40. Uttar Pradesh has about **400** MLAs, and he still treated that as not enough of the society. Ministers can come from the council, as they can from the RS. The conclusion he wanted set against Lecture 6: the RS is the **second** House, not a secondary one. The council **is** the secondary House. It is inferior to the assembly.

The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission** said the council should be made **compulsory** in every State, and that making it compulsory is not enough. Its role should be **redefined on the lines of the RS**: if the second tier is represented in the first, the third tier should be represented in the second. Make the council **the House of local government**. Panchayats and municipalities received constitutional status in **1993**, by the **73rd Amendment**, which he also placed in **1992**. He said the commission submitted **15 reports**, including one on ethics in administration, and that the ethics paper from 2013 is administrative ethics. He did not name the chairman.

He called local government the government that touches daily life the most, and the weakest. The classic gap is the **three Fs: functions, functionaries, and funds**. He did not see a local-government service. The bodies depend on deputation and on grants from the State. He called the experiment about **30 years** old, and still an experiment. This is not the local-government chapter.

## 4. Eight Union territories, eight reasons (POL-11-04)

**Part VIII.** In **1950** there were no Union territories. There were only States, in four groups: **Part A, Part B, Part C, and Part D**, which were **Parts VI, VII, VIII, and IX**. He did not teach which States sat in which part. In **1956** the fourfold scheme was abolished. The twofold scheme, States and Union territories, is what is still used, and he called that **70 years**. Parts VI and VII were merged into the States, and **Part VII was deleted**. It is **still empty**. Parts VIII and IX were merged into the Union territories, and Part IX was deleted, then later rewritten. **Part IX today is the panchayats.**

A Union territory, as an idea, belongs to a **federal** country. In a unitary country the whole country is already under one government, so the label does nothing. Every federal country, on his logic, will at least have its **capital** as such a territory, because that is where the central government sits. He called the demand that Delhi become a State a failure of that logic. His examples of capitals: **Islamabad**, and **Washington in the District of Columbia**. He told them to check ten federal countries, and the neighbours first, and not to stop at the United States, Canada, and Australia. He did not dictate the ten.

What is special about India is that there is **not just one**. There are **eight**, and **each is a Union territory for a different reason**. No two are administered in the same way.

| Union territory | Why, as he locked it |
|:---|:---|
| **Delhi** | It is the **capital** |
| **Puducherry** | An **Indo-French agreement**. As long as the people want it, and he said they will always want it, their **separate identity** is preserved. It cannot be merged into another State, so it has an **assembly**. It is also **small and scattered**, not one block: **Puducherry** on the coast inside the Tamil Nadu map, **Karaikal** to the south, **Yanam** on the Andhra coast, and **Mahe** on the Kerala side. The headquarters is Puducherry. “It was a French colony” is a fact. It is not the answer |
| **Chandigarh** | **Political.** When Punjab and Haryana were separated, both wanted the developed city. The Centre kept it, as a **temporary** solution. Temporary solutions here have a habit of becoming permanent. He contrasted **Hyderabad**: Andhra Pradesh and Telangana both wanted it, Andhra did not hand it to the Centre, and that side would build a **new capital**. He did not name the new city |
| **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** | **Cultural distinctiveness**, the Portuguese layer, and again **small and scattered**. Diu he called a small island. Daman sits further in. Dadra and Nagar Haveli shares a border with Gujarat and Maharashtra. Folded into those States, the smaller identity would disappear. The gulf name on the audio did not land cleanly. Do not invent one |
| **Jammu and Kashmir** | **Internal security** |
| **Ladakh** | **Cultural distinctiveness** |
| **Lakshadweep** | He placed it on **the same reason** as the Ladakh line he had just given |
| **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** | The sheet marks these **strategic**. He defined strategic, and did not finish a separate “because” sentence. **Strategic means defence**: the long-term survival of the nation. **Tactical** is short-term. A gun is tactical. A missile is strategic. Trade, education, and tourism he called short-term beside that |

## 5. How a Union territory is actually run (POL-11-05)

A State is a **partner**. A Union territory is the Union’s **own territory**, a child rather than a partner. The executive head is the **President**, the same head as the Union. The President appoints an **administrator** to work on that behalf. The administrator is **suitably designated**. There are two designations. Both are administrators. A **Lieutenant Governor** is one rank below a Governor, and above a plain administrator. The audio kept saying “left-hand governor”.

He first said the three Union territories with an assembly have a Lieutenant Governor, and then he listed **five**: **Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands**. The **other three** have an administrator, and he did not name those three. The Lieutenant Governors of Delhi, of Puducherry, and of Jammu and Kashmir are **not the same as one another**.

**Article 239** is this executive design. The President, meaning the Union government, may make **regulations** for the **good governance** of a Union territory. A regulation has the **same force as an Act of Parliament**. Parliament is the parent, and Parliament does not sit for a small territory, so the executive regulates. Regulations are **not** the ordinary tool for **Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir**, because those three can legislate for themselves. **Chandigarh** is also outside the ordinary regulation power. He tied that to a **negotiated arrangement with Punjab**, which he called a tripartite agreement. The name of the arrangement did not land. Do not invent it.

For **Puducherry**, the ordinary rule is still no regulation, but a regulation **can** be made if the assembly is **suspended or dissolved**. He did not extend that exception to Jammu and Kashmir. He read it as part of the bargain with the French: more local power, and a Union power that returns only when the assembly is not there.

An **advisory committee** supplies the local view, because Delhi cannot know what a distant territory needs. The advice is on **State List** matters: a **policy**, a **legislative proposal**, and the **budget**. Nothing is written about the membership. In general, **eminent persons** of that territory, and the **member of Parliament** from it is always one. Every Union territory has a Lok Sabha seat. **Three** also have Rajya Sabha seats. **Lakshadweep has one** member of Parliament. The committee exists for the **five** territories that do **not** have their own assembly. The three that do, do not need it.

The legislature was an afterthought. The original thought was that Parliament would make the law. Puducherry was factually part of India from **1954**, and legally from **1962**. The **14th Constitutional Amendment** inserted **Article 239A**: Parliament may **by law** provide a **legislative assembly and a council of ministers**. Three territories have that assembly: **Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir**.

- For the **other five**, Parliament makes the law on the **State List**.
- For the **three**, **both** the assembly and Parliament can make law on those subjects. He called that a concurrent situation in practice. The parent’s power does **not** disappear when the child is allowed to decide.
- The assemblies are not equal. **Delhi** may legislate on the State List **except public order, police, and land**, because it is the seat of the Union government. He gave that as the example. **Puducherry is not under the same three exceptions.**

**Article 241.** Parliament may by law do one of two things: give the Union territory its **own High Court**, or place it inside the jurisdiction of a **nearby** High Court. A **subordinate court** exists in every Union territory. The High Court is the administrative boss of those subordinate courts. The Supreme Court may **overrule a judgment**. It has **no administrative authority** over a High Court. A High Court decides its own process. Two territories have their own High Court: **Delhi**, and **Jammu and Kashmir**, which is also the High Court for **Ladakh** and is called the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir **and Ladakh**. The court’s **name** has often not followed the city’s new name. **Madras** High Court sits in Chennai, and Puducherry is under it. **Bombay** High Court sits in Mumbai. **Calcutta** High Court sits in Kolkata. Chandigarh is under the **High Court of Punjab and Haryana**, which sits in Chandigarh. It is not a High Court of Chandigarh.

## 6. National emergency, Article 352 (POL-11-06)

**Part XVIII** makes sense only after the Union, the State, and the Union territory are in view, because this is where that design breaks. Three situations sit in the chapter. **Article 352**, national emergency. Four articles later, **Article 356**, President’s rule. Four more, **Article 360**, financial emergency. President’s rule is not called an emergency in ordinary speech. It is in the same chapter. Article 360 was not taught.

If the President is satisfied, and that satisfaction is the **Union Council’s**, that circumstances **threaten the security of India or of any part of it**, the President may **proclaim** a national emergency. **National describes the kind of situation, not the map.** It can be confined to one place. The grounds are only three: **war, external aggression, or armed rebellion**. Nothing else.

The advice must be the **written advice of the Cabinet**. That this is the only article which uses the word Cabinet stays Lecture 4. This class adds the story of why it is written.

The proclamation is valid for **one month**. Within that month it needs **Parliament’s approval**. The resolution is the tough majority already in Lecture 5: **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, and a majority of the total membership**. After approval it is valid for **six months from the date of approval**, not from the proclamation. If approval comes on the thirtieth day, the first stretch is about **seven months**. Each later extension is **six months**, and there is **no outer limit**, but no single approval runs past six months.

Inside a six-month period it can be **discontinued**. He treated the special session of the Lok Sabha, and the case where the Lok Sabha is already dissolved so the Rajya Sabha approves, as already known. The **one-tenth** notice and the **14 days** stay Lecture 7.

What is on the page now is **not** the original article. The changes he walked are the **44th Amendment**. He also said the **38th** was made to bring on the emergency, and that the **42nd**, in **1976**, during the emergency, is the amendment that made the government too strong. The 44th is the one he called the saviour.

| What changed | Original | After the 44th |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Third ground | **Internal disturbance**, which he called subjective | **Armed rebellion**: an **organised uprising to overthrow a lawfully elected government** |
| Advice | Alleged to have been the Prime Minister’s, and **verbal**, in 1975. He stressed that this is an **allegation**. There is no recording | **Written advice of the Cabinet** |
| First validity | **Two months**, for all three situations in the chapter | **One month**, and **only** for national emergency. President’s rule and financial emergency are **still two months** |
| Majority for approval | Silent, so **simple** | The **tough** majority, and **only** for national emergency. The other two are still simple |
| How long after approval | **Until revoked** | **Six months**, then a fresh approval. It cannot sit there forgotten |
| Power to discontinue | Not in the original design | The **special session**, added by the same amendment |

National emergency has been proclaimed **three times: 1962, 1971, and 1975**. The first two were **external aggression**. He sees no controversy there. **1975** was **internal disturbance**, and misuse has been alleged. The **1962** proclamation was revoked in **1968**. The **1971** proclamation was **not withdrawn**. When 1975 was about to be proclaimed, a civil servant pointed out that an emergency was already in force. The ground was different, so a **new** proclamation was made. **Both were removed in 1977**, when the Janata Party, the leaders who had been arrested, came to power. The six-month clock is there so that a proclamation cannot be infinite, and so that it is not simply forgotten.

## 7. What a national emergency does (POL-11-07)

Four effects. **None on the judiciary.** The judiciary is the safeguard that remains.

**Executive.** The State executive **continues**. It is brought under the **effective control** of the Union, which may now give **directions even on the State List**. In ordinary time the Union already directs the same machinery, the district administration, but only on **Union** subjects, because implementation is not split into two services. During a national emergency the direction reaches the State List as well. The executive becomes **unitary**. His line for ordinary time is that India is federal **with unitary tendencies**. During this emergency there is no tendency left. It **is** unitary, and only for the emergency.

**Legislature.** The State legislature **continues**, and Parliament **also** legislates on the State List. He called that a **concurrent jurisdiction** over the State List. The Seventh Schedule, in practice, collapses from three lists to two: Union, and a State List that now behaves as concurrent. A law the Union makes on that list applies to **every State**, including a State where nothing in the emergency was happening. When the emergency is revoked, that law **lapses after six months**. Within those six months the State may **adopt** it and make it its own Act. Once the Union’s concurrent hold ends, the State is free.

If an election cannot be held because a term is ending, **Parliament may by law extend** the term of the **Lok Sabha or of an assembly**. Not more than **one year at a time**, and not more than **six months after the emergency is revoked**. The House elected in **1971** was due again in **1976**, in the middle of the emergency. The term was extended to **1977**, and a second extension ran toward **1978**. The second was **not used**. Elections were held. He slipped and said “1971” for the year the government stopped it. The extensions he actually named are 1977 and the unused one toward 1978.

**Financial relations.** The President **may** suspend the distribution of financial resources between the Centre and the States. It is a power, not a duty. **It has never been used**, including in the emergencies that were wars. The ordinary **41 per cent** share stays the economy notes. He only used it as the distribution that this power can interrupt.

**Fundamental rights.** The rights themselves are the next block of classes. Two articles, and only the effect.

- **Article 358.** If the emergency is on the ground of **war or external aggression**, and **not** armed rebellion, **Article 19 stands suspended**. It is **automatic**. He named freedom of speech and expression, and freedom of movement, as the kind of freedom that cannot be used to walk into a war or to hold a rally in one. Because the right itself is gone, there is **no remedy later** for what was done while it was gone.
- **Article 359.** On **any** of the three grounds, the President **may** suspend the **right to move a court** for the violation of a fundamental right. The **right remains**. The **remedy** is paused. When the emergency ends, the remedy returns, and the person may then go to court. The original word was **any** fundamental right. The **44th Amendment** added **except Articles 20 and 21**. Article 21 he called the right to life, already a popular name. Article 20 he called a protection in cases of conviction, written in because of illegal arrest and detention. Do not fill those two articles from outside this class.

## 8. President’s rule, Article 356 (POL-11-08)

If the President is satisfied that circumstances exist **in the State** such that the administration **cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution**, the President may take over. Satisfaction may rest on the **Governor’s report, or otherwise**. Otherwise can be a media report. It is not closed.

Two takeovers. The President takes the **administration**. The President also declares that **Parliament shall legislate on the State List for that State**, not for every State. That is the difference from Article 352.

The proclamation is valid for **two months**, then needs Parliament’s approval by a **simple** majority. Once approved, the proclamation **is valid for six months**. Those two months are **inside** the six, not added on. If approval comes on the last day, the total is still six months, not eight. After that, one more six-month approval is ordinary. **One year** is the easy stretch.

After one year, **both** of these must be true, and they must be true at **every** later extension:

1. A **national emergency** is in force in the State, or in any part of it.
2. The **Election Commission** certifies that an election is **not possible**.

Each such extension is six months, and they may run for **two more years**. The Constitution’s ceiling on President’s rule is **three years**.

**Executive.** The State executive is **dismissed**. It does not continue under directions, which is what Article 352 does. The Union executive exercises the power, through the **Governor and the Chief Secretary**. In this hour the Governor **is** an agent, under the Union’s directions. The ordinary rule, that the Governor is not an agent, stays Lecture 9. This is the exception that class had parked.

**Legislature.** Two options: **suspend** the assembly, or **dissolve** it. Suspension leaves the members in place and takes away the legislative work, because Parliament is doing it. Dissolution ends the membership. **Manipur** is the recent picture: President’s rule from **February 2025 to February 2026**, and the assembly was **suspended**, not dissolved. He treated that imposition as necessary. A law Parliament makes for that State’s State List **remains** after the proclamation is revoked. The State **may repeal** it. The default is the opposite of a national emergency: there the Union’s State-List law **lapses in six months** unless the State adopts it; here the law **stays** unless the State repeals it.

Nothing happens to **financial relations**. Nothing happens to **fundamental rights**. Only a national emergency touches those rights.

The misuse he described belonged to the **1960s, the 1970s, and the 1980s**: a State government of a different party was dismissed, and the reply, when that party later held the Union, was to dismiss the other side. It is rarer now. The reason he put first is **coalition politics**. Regional parties sit inside the Union government, so the Union cannot casually injure them. His illustration was the **Shiv Sena**: a Union that needs such a party cannot dismiss that party’s State government for the difference alone. The second reason is the judgment.

**S.R. Bommai versus Union of India, 1994.** The audio said “Bambai”. The sheet writes Bommai. He was the Chief Minister of **Karnataka**, and his government had been dismissed. The points he wanted kept:

- Some of them are **desirable norms**, not a hard order. The **majority of the Council is tested on the floor of the House**, not decided by the Governor’s impression. No clear majority is a ground for President’s rule. At the Union, that gap is a caretaker government. A State need not have one. The recent picture is **Tamil Nadu**: after the election nobody had a majority, the Governor did not invite **Mr Vijay** for several days, and the fear was that the delay was being used to prepare President’s rule. He was invited. He proved a majority. The Governor does not decide the numbers.
- A **warning** first, and **time to respond**, which the court put at **one week**.
- If it is misused, **judicial review** lies for **mala fide**. What is examined is the **satisfaction**, and the **circumstances**.
- Until Parliament has approved, **no irreversible action**. **Dissolution is irreversible. Suspension is not.** Until approval, the President may **only suspend**. Dissolution waits for Parliament.
- Article 356 is justified for a breakdown of the **constitutional** machinery. It is **not** justified for a breakdown of the **administrative** machinery. A State that **refuses a direction of the Union** is the constitutional example: ordinarily a direction on a Union subject, and, during a national emergency, on a State subject as well. **Bad roads, a failure of good governance, or a corruption charge against a Chief Minister** are administrative. They are not a ground.
- It is an **extraordinary** power. Use it **sparingly**, or it disturbs the federal balance. His picture was a service weapon: it is necessary, and it is not for every day.

In the Constituent Assembly, members said the Union would misuse it, and he said they were proved right. **Ambedkar’s** answer, as he recalled it: the power is large and it is **necessary**, and **it is my hope that Article 356 will remain a dead letter**. A dead letter is a power that exists and is rarely used. He thinks that hope is now visible, because of coalitions and because of Bommai. Earlier it was among the most misused provisions.

**Parked:** **Article 360**, the half page. The next sitting is **fundamental rights, Part III**: what a right is, what a fundamental right is, and where India’s came from.

## Locks

- Six councils, **Article 168**: Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, **Maharashtra**, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana. Not Madhya Pradesh. **Article 169**: the tough majority, then a law. Necessary, not sufficient. Rajasthan and Assam are waiting.
- Assembly guideline **500 and 60**. By law: **Goa 40, Mizoram 40, Sikkim 32, Puducherry 30**. Not 90. Council: **not above one-third, not below 40**.
- Five shares, **as nearly as may be**. Graduates: **three years**. Teachers: **secondary**, not primary. Nomination adds **cooperatives**. Method is the single transferable vote.
- **No joint sitting.** Money Bill: **14 days**. No action on an assembly bill: **three months**, then a second passage, then **one month**. The council cannot reject or amend by its own power. It is the **secondary** House. The RS is not.
- **1956**, twofold. **Part VII is empty. Part IX is the panchayats.** Eight Union territories, eight reasons. Puducherry is four pieces and a treaty. Chandigarh was temporary.
- **Five** Lieutenant Governors, not three. **Article 239A, 1962, the 14th Amendment.** Delhi except **public order, police, and land**. **Article 241.** Delhi, and Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, have their own High Court. Puducherry is under **Madras**.
- Article 352: **one month**, then **six months from the approval**. Grounds: war, external aggression, **armed rebellion**. Three proclamations: **1962, 1971, 1975**. The 1971 one lasted, forgotten, until **1977**.
- Article 358 suspends **Article 19** only for war and external aggression. Article 359 pauses the **remedy**, on any ground, **except 20 and 21** after the 44th. A Union law on the State List **lapses in six months**.
- Article 356: **two months inside six**. Simple majority. **Three years**, and after the first year a national emergency **and** an Election Commission certificate. The Union’s law **remains**. **Bommai, 1994**: floor, one week, no dissolution before approval, constitutional breakdown rather than bad roads. Ambedkar’s **dead letter**.
