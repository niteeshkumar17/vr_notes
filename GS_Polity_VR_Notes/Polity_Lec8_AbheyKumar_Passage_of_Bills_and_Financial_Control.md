## 1. Four bills, and why some need the President’s recommendation (POL-09-01)

By **subject**, four kinds. The **ordinary bill** has no definition of its own. It is the **default**: whatever is left after the other three. By **who introduces**, two kinds. A **government bill** is introduced by a minister. A **private member’s bill** is introduced by any member who is not a minister, whichever party they belong to.

The Constitution does **not** stop any member from bringing any bill. A **Money Bill can be a private member’s bill.** The condition is the **President’s recommendation**, which in practice is the Council of Ministers. They may refuse an opposition bill. That makes introduction difficult. It does not make it prohibited. He said private members’ Money Bills are already pending. Sources that say a Money Bill cannot be a private member’s bill are wrong.

Recommendation is required when the proposal should move only with the agreement of the side in power, because the last election gave that side the authority to spend the people’s money. A member may still propose. His sketch, not a real project: a highway of 500 kilometres, four lanes, **₹2,000 crore**. That is expenditure, so it falls under a Money Bill, and the recommendation will not come unless the Council agrees. The same logic sits on some ordinary bills. Renaming Kerala as **Keralam** needed the President’s recommendation under **Article 2 or Article 3**. There is no list of twenty bills to memorise. Ask whether the ruling side must agree before the proposal is even introduced.

A **Money Bill** deals with **only** money matters. The keyword is **only**. If anything else is in the bill, it is a financial bill. Money matters, in the broad sense, are expenditure and revenue, and revenue includes a tax and also a borrowing. The detailed list is **Article 110**. He did not dictate it.

**Where, and how.**

| Step | What he locked |
|:---|:---|
| Introduction | **Only in the Lok Sabha (LS)**, and only with the **President’s recommendation** |
| Majority | **Simple** |
| Rajya Sabha (RS) | Does **not** have equal power. It **cannot reject** a Money Bill |
| RS options | Pass it, **recommend** amendments, or take **no action** |
| If RS passes | The bill returns to the originating house. That house sends it to the President |
| If RS recommends | LS may accept, reject, or ignore. Once LS passes the bill a **second time, in any form**, it is **deemed passed by both Houses** |
| If RS does nothing | Wait. Money is urgent. At the end of **14 days** the bill is **deemed passed**, brought back to the LS, and sent for assent |
| President | **Assent or withhold.** No suspensive veto, because a Money Bill cannot be returned. Lecture 2 holds the table |

The LS therefore proposes, must **consult** the RS once, and then decides. That is the overriding power. The **originating house** always completes the process and sends the bill to the President. For a Money Bill that house is always the LS.

## 2. A financial bill is introduced like a Money Bill and passed like an ordinary bill (POL-09-02)

A **financial bill** contains **both** money matters and non-money matters. Non-money is the ordinary subject.

Because money is in it, it is **introduced like a Money Bill**: only in the LS, and only with the President’s recommendation **at introduction**. Because ordinary matter is in it, it is **passed like an ordinary bill**: the RS has **equal** legislative power, the LS **cannot override**, and a deadlock is possible. The majority is still **simple**. Every bill except a constitutional-amendment bill is passed by a simple majority.

**“Type 1” and “Type 2” are not in the Constitution.** Books invented the labels, and they do not even agree with each other. He used them only so the class could see what the books are pointing at.

- What **he** called **Type 1** is the financial bill just described: known to contain money and non-money, introduced in the LS, recommendation at **introduction**.
- What **he** called **Type 2** is **Article 117(3)**. The sheet writes “Type I” beside Article 117(3). **Follow the spoken labels.** Clause 3 is his Type 2.

His picture of clause 3. A scheme says: one Indian Institute of Technology in every five square kilometres; each institute at least six departments; each at least fifteen professors. That text is an **ordinary** bill. Either House. No recommendation. Suppose it is introduced in the RS. During discussion the minister says five institutes next year and ten a year after that. The House then sees that **expenditure** will be needed someday. They are **not** asking to withdraw money now. That would be an Appropriation Bill, and the process would have to be scrapped and begun again.

Expenditure discovered in debate is common to almost any scheme. The Constitution does not make them start over. The President’s recommendation is taken at the **consideration** stage, not at introduction. **Only expenditure** gets this mercy. Any other money matter means scrap and start again.

Every bill has **three readings**, which are three stages: **introduction** (the general idea), **consideration** (clause by clause), **passage** (the vote). The recommendation under clause 3 comes at consideration, before the vote, because at introduction nobody yet knew money was involved.

The book line he rejected: “a Type 2 financial bill **can be introduced** in the Rajya Sabha.” When it was introduced, it was **not** a financial bill. The accurate line is that it **may have been** introduced in either House. If you already know it is a financial bill, you do **not** introduce it in the RS.

## 3. A constitutional-amendment bill is only an Article 368 bill (POL-09-03)

A bill that amends the Constitution is **not** automatically a constitutional-amendment bill. The name belongs to a bill that amends the Constitution **under Article 368**. An ordinary bill can also change the text. Article 3, the renaming of a State, is the example already used.

| Step | What he locked |
|:---|:---|
| House | **Either** House. No restriction |
| President’s recommendation | **Not** required |
| Powers of the two Houses | **Equal** |
| Majority in each House | **Not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting**, **and** a **majority of the total membership** of that House. “Special” is not an answer. Write both limbs |
| If the amendment affects the **division of power** between the Union and the States | Ratification by **not less than half** of the **State legislative assemblies**. Councils are not asked. More than half is welcome. Equal to half is enough |
| If it does not | Straight to the President |
| Inside each ratifying assembly | **Simple** majority, because nothing else is written |
| President | **Only assent.** No withhold, no suspensive veto. That duty was written in by the **24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**. Lecture 2 already holds this |

**How many assemblies, at the minimum.** States **28**. Assemblies in Union territories **3**. Total **31**. Half of 31 is 15.5. **15 is less than half, so it fails. The minimum is 16.**

## 4. Safeguards sit inside the system; the court is the last of them (POL-09-04)

Amending the Constitution changes the design of government, so a bad government can misuse it. He did not want **judicial review** written as the first safeguard. A court is the **last resort**, after the office itself has been asked to correct the wrong. In an answer the court comes late, not as point one.

The flow he wanted:

1. The **majority** is hard. Two-thirds is not a casual vote.
2. **Coalition politics since 1989.** Not one Union government in that stretch has been a single-party majority government. The present government is also a coalition. A party that does not even hold half the House must bring the **opposition** in if it wants two-thirds. If that wide agreement exists, the change has already been argued in public. Media and popular opinion follow. They are not themselves an office inside the system.
3. If all of that fails, **judicial review** can still test the **basic structure**. He has not taught that doctrine. It will take about half a class. The superficial list he allowed, and then called insufficient: **secularism, the parliamentary form, independence of the judiciary**. The **Preamble is not** that list. Do not write “Preamble” as an item of the basic structure.

Two examples, after the concept, not instead of it.

- **This year’s Budget session, the 131st Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2026.** He read it as a proposal to take the LS toward **850** seats, and he said it was **defeated**. Lecture 7 had already named the same bill as the failed item in the last three days of that session, tied there to women’s reservation and delimitation. Keep both wordings. Do not collapse them into one purpose.
- The **99th Constitutional Amendment**, the **National Judicial Appointments Commission**, struck down because it hurt the **independence of the judiciary**. The sheet writes “199th”. **He said 99th.** Do not learn 199th.

## 5. A bill lapses when its string to the Lok Sabha snaps (POL-09-05)

**Article 107.** Not every bill lapses. The LS is a ship that takes five years to sink. This House, in his sketch, is already two years in and will dissolve in **2029**. A bill tied to that ship sinks with it. A bill with **no** string to the LS does not.

A bill **only in the RS**, still under discussion there, has no string. Dissolution of the LS leaves it alive.

**Exception.** The President is the anchor on the shore. Once the President is in the picture, the bill does **not** lapse, even if it was tied to the LS. The **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986**, sat on the President’s table through a change of government. It remained.

| Where the bill is when the LS dissolves | Result |
|:---|:---|
| Introduced in the LS, still being discussed there | **Lapses** |
| Passed by the LS, now pending in the RS | **Lapses.** Reaching the RS does not cut the string |
| Passed by both Houses, lying with the President, assent not yet given | **Does not lapse** |

## 6. Deadlock, and a joint sitting that cannot touch Article 368 (POL-09-06)

**Article 108.** A deadlock is a disagreement, and it can arise only where the Houses have **equal** power. A Money Bill cannot deadlock: the LS overrides. An ordinary bill can. A financial bill can. A constitutional-amendment bill can **disagree**, but a **joint sitting cannot cure it**. Article 368 says the bill shall be passed by the two Houses **sitting separately**. A joint sitting would hand the result to the LS, which is about twice the size. Changing the Constitution needs both Houses actually agreed.

A joint sitting is therefore only for an **ordinary bill** and a **financial bill**.

Deadlock is **presumed** in three situations.

1. One House passes. The second **rejects**.
2. One House passes. The second **amends**. The originating House **disagrees** with those amendments.
3. One House passes. The second takes **no action for six months**.

Those six months are **six months of sittings**, not six calendar months. “No action” means the House was convened and did not take up the bill. The monsoon session, he said, ended on **13 August** and had **19 sittings**. A winter session of about ten days does not turn the gap into sitting time. A year of sittings, with a Budget session of about **30 to 40 days**, comes to about **60 to 80 days**. Six months of sittings is therefore a long stretch, about **two to three years**. He first said six years and then walked that back. Do not learn six years.

When sitting days are counted, an **adjournment of more than four days is not counted**. Two days (the weekend), three days, and four days **are** counted. Five days or more are not.

A joint sitting has happened **three** times. The decision is by **simple majority**, and each time the LS version won.

| Bill | What became of it |
|:---|:---|
| **Dowry Prohibition Bill, 1960** | **Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961** |
| **Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill, 1977** | Passed. He said the detail is fifty years old and not required |
| **Prevention of Terrorism Bill, 2002** | Became **POTA**, after the **2001** attack on Parliament. **Repealed in 2004**, because the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act** (he said “Unlawful Actors”) took the stronger provisions in **2004** and POTA was redundant |

Who presides stays in Lecture 6.

## 7. Delimitation is three jobs, and the seat numbers are still on 1971 (POL-09-07)

Redrawing boundaries is only the second job. People think it is the whole of delimitation because, for about fifty years, that is the only job that has actually been done.

**Article 82** for the LS. **Article 170** for State assemblies. **Only directly elected Houses.** A legislative council has no constituencies, so it is not delimited. The same limit applies to nominated seats when reservation is counted.

Three activities, all meant to give **equal representation**: one voter, one vote, **one value**. One person, one vote is already true. One **value** is not, if one constituency has 100 voters and another has 500. The vote in the smaller constituency is worth more. The real gaps are in lakhs, not in hundreds. The aim is political equality.

1. **Fix the number of seats**, and, inside the LS total, **allocate seats to States**. He used **543** for the LS, **70** for the Delhi assembly, **403** for the Uttar Pradesh assembly. Allocation among States is the controversy. Assembly totals are not: Tamil Nadu is not hurt because Uttar Pradesh has 403 assembly seats. It is hurt if Uttar Pradesh’s **80** LS seats become **100** while Tamil Nadu’s **39** become **40**.
2. **Readjust the boundaries** of those constituencies, on population.
3. **Reserve seats** for the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes**. Not every seat. Every delimitation commission so far has done this.

**The freeze, and the mess.** Population decides seats, so the north would gain and the south and the north-east would lose. He treated higher population growth as the result of literacy, employment, and the status of women, not as a virtue that should be rewarded with more voice. Southern parties are vocal. He named **Vijay** and, earlier, **Stalin**. North-eastern States are less vocal, and the hurt is still there.

From the **1970s** the seat allocation was not to be changed. Other work could continue. The **Delimitation Act, 1973**, tied the **number of seats** to the **1971** census. The **Delimitation Act, 2002**, created a commission and redrew **boundaries** on the **2001** census, and again refused to change the number of seats. So the numbers are 1971 and the map is 2001. He called even that an oversimplification.

The **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation, 2019**, separated **Ladakh**. New constituency boundaries there used the latest census by then, **2011**.

**Jharkhand, Nagaland, Manipur, and Arunachal Pradesh** did not get that last delimitation, mainly for **security** in the north-east. In those States both the **number of seats** and the **boundaries** are still on **1971**.

A **2024** LS election, as he described it: the **number** of seats from **1971**; the **boundaries** from **1971** in some places, from **2011** in some (Jammu and Kashmir), and from **2001** in most. He called it an utter mess, and not political equality. The Constitution’s own rule is that the **latest census** is used for all of it.

The freeze runs until the **first census after 2026**. That census is underway. The **reference date is 1 March**, and he said the count will be completed **next March**. Census as a topic he postponed. After the count, a delimitation Act, then a commission. The 2002 Act’s final order came in **2008**, and the first election on it was **2009**. People say **2029**. His guess, unless the government forces the work, is the **2034** election. It is a guess, not a decision.

**Women’s reservation** will be done in that next delimitation, and it has never been done before. The **106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023**, was **notified in April this year** (the class is October 2026, so **April 2026**). It is now to be implemented.

- **As nearly as may be one-third.** One-third of a House need not be a whole number, so one seat either way is allowed. “At least one-third” is the wrong tightening. Less than one-third is also wrong.
- Of the **directly elected** seats, in the **LS and in the assemblies**. A nominated seat is not in the count. The **Jammu and Kashmir** assembly has **5** nominated seats. **Puducherry** (audio “Kuddu Chiri”) has **3**.
- For **15 years**. After that, **Parliament by law** decides. He expects an extension, because withdrawing a reservation is, in his view, political suicide. The Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe reservation was written to end by **1960**, ten years, with Parliament to re-examine and extend if needed. It has been extended, ten years at a time. He does not accept a slogan of abolishing reservation, and he also wants a review of whether it reaches the people it was written for. The same review should apply to women. The text, though, leaves the decision to Parliament.

**The conflict to write, not a fake answer.** Democracy wants one value, so Uttar Pradesh must have more seats than Tamil Nadu. Federalism wants equity among the States in a Union of States. Complete democracy threatens the States. Complete equality of States threatens the value of a vote. A mains answer is asked to show that conflict and then a balance, not to announce the policy. A balance sacrifices a little of each side. A perfect answer on one principle kills the other.

His numbers, which the sheet repeats: an average LS constituency in **Kerala holds about 16 lakh** people; in **Bihar, about 33 lakh**. Bihar is under-represented. If a Kerala voter’s value is 1, a Bihar voter’s value is about half, and Bihar would need two members where Kerala has one. He later said “60 lakh” for Kerala. **That is a slip. Lock 16 and 33.** Bihar has not moved the Supreme Court. He said the Constitution would have allowed the challenge.

The handout, given earlier, has **five** ideas. He did not teach them. Two are foreign: the **United States**, and the **European Union’s degressive proportionality** (as population rises, people per seat also rise, so a large State does not take too many seats). His illustration of that idea, not a real European schedule: the first seat for every **10 lakh**, and later seats for every **20 lakh**.

The idea he called the most rational is the one the **Finance Commissions** already use, and which the States have largely accepted sixteen times. The **16th Finance Commission** is the one now working. Its recommendations **started on the first day of this year** and run to **2031**. The vertical share is **the same as last time, 41%** of the Centre’s tax revenue. Classroom rupee, not a Budget figure: the Centre earns **₹100**, **₹41** goes to the States, **₹59** stays. That 41% line, and the commission’s full formula, stay in the economy notes. Do not open a second recall row for them.

Conceptually, distributing ₹41 and distributing LS seats are the same problem. Income and tax effort are economic criteria and are **not** how a democracy counts people. The two criteria he carried across:

| Half of the House | Rule |
|:---|:---|
| **Population** | More people, more seats. Uttar Pradesh gains. Tamil Nadu does not like this half |
| **Demographic performance** | **Fertility rate** between the **1971 and 2011** censuses. Fertility rate, as he defined it: the average number of children a woman gives birth to in her lifetime. A higher rate is the worse performance and gets **less**. The south gains on this half. The window can be moved to **2027** when that census exists. The concept is the point, not these two years |

The government has spoken of a House around **816**, which is why **850** was in the air. **Shashi Tharoor** compared a House of that size to a Chinese party conference in which members do not know who else is in the room. He himself thinks even **543** is already too large for a discussion. Set that objection aside for the arithmetic. Split the new House in two. The sheet, and the sum he worked, is **800 = 400 + 400**. He also said **60–40** could be chosen later. The example to remember is the half-and-half. Uttar Pradesh takes more of the population 400. Tamil Nadu takes more of the performance 400. Each State’s total is the sum of its two slices. Each side wins some and loses some. What politics will actually do, he left open.

## 8. Three articles hold the purse (POL-09-08)

**Financial control** means Parliament’s control of the **executive**. The same three articles, in the same order, do the work for the States. He once said “260” and then taught **267**. **Lock 265, 266, and 267.**

**Article 265 is the core.** No tax shall be **levied or collected** except by **authority of law**. Levy means impose. The authority is an **Act**, not a resolution. Parliament makes that law. If the executive cannot take the money without Parliament, it also cannot spend what it cannot take.

**Article 266** does two things.

**Consolidated Fund of India.** Into it go **all revenue**, **all loans raised**, and **all other income** of the Government of India. Revenue is the tax and what sits with it. A loan is a borrowing. Other income is everything else the government earns by using its assets: **interest** on a loan it has given, a **dividend** from a public-sector company in which it holds shares. The fund is at the **disposal of Parliament**. No deposit, no withdrawal, and no **appropriation** without Parliament’s approval. Appropriation is the authorisation to spend later. Withdrawal is the spending, when it actually happens. This is the **largest** fund and the **regular** fund. There is **no limit** on what may be deposited or spent. Salaries, a Rafale purchase, a highway: the day-to-day work comes from here. **Every State has its own Consolidated Fund under the same article.** He warned that “CFI” is not a standard acronym. The full name comes first.

**Public Account of India**, in the **same article**. It is an **account**, not a fund, and it holds many funds. Into it goes **every other receipt**, meaning money the government holds but does **not own**. The owner is the public. His examples: a **provident fund**, the **Public Provident Fund (PPF)**, and the **Employees’ Provident Fund**. The government is a **custodian**, a trustee, as a bank is. This money must not sit in the Consolidated Fund, because that fund builds roads. The government may **borrow** from these balances into the Consolidated Fund. He put that borrowing at **up to about 7%**. The Public Account is at the **disposal of the executive**, not of Parliament. **Every State has a public account of its own.**

The PPF aside, which he gave as financial literacy and not as a budget head: **risk-free**, government-guaranteed, **tax-free at deposit, on the interest, and at withdrawal** (exempt, exempt, exempt). A return of **7.1%** is received as **7.1%**. A minimum of **₹500 a year**. Locked for **15 years**. Compounding, in his account, becomes visible around the **ninth or tenth** year. He first mentioned a 6% return that tax cuts to about 5%. That was the taxable contrast. **The PPF figure he then locked is 7.1%, and it is not taxed.**

**Article 267** does not itself create a fund. It **allows Parliament, by law, to create** the **Contingency Fund of India**, an emergency or **imprest**: money that can be spent **without prior approval**, because a bill may not be ready and Parliament may not be sitting. Parliament did create it. The **Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950**, opened it with **₹50 crore**. The corpus is raised from time to time. The last change he named is **2021**, to **₹30,000 crore**. The only source of the money is the Consolidated Fund. It is parked there so it can be used at once. It stands at the **disposal of the President**, which in practice is the finance ministry.

His replenishment sketch, not a real withdrawal: spend **₹10,000 crore**, and **₹20,000 crore** remains. The ₹10,000 crore must come back from the Consolidated Fund, and that needs **Parliament’s approval after the expenditure**. The only difference from the Consolidated Fund is **when** Parliament approves: **before** the spending there, **after** the spending here. Ultimately the contingency money is still parliamentary, because that is the only tap that refills it. **State legislatures may create a contingency fund, and every State has one.**

Do not mix these with the **Prime Minister’s fund** or a **national calamity relief fund**. Those are **voluntary** contributions. The Consolidated Fund is **constitutional**. The Contingency Fund is **statutory**.

**Charged expenditure** is expenditure **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**. Do not read “charged” as “paid out of”. Almost all expenditure is paid out of that fund. In the budget, expenditure is of **two** kinds.

| Kind | What happens |
|:---|:---|
| **Voted** | Parliament **votes**. Voting is a majority, so it is the ruling side’s control. A scheme — his pair was a bullet train **or** a highway — is voted, because it is a priority |
| **Charged** | **Not voted.** It may be **discussed**. Parliament does not exercise its regular control. Think of the approval as already given |

Two reasons something is charged.

1. **Independence.** If the legislature votes the salary, it can squeeze the institution. **Judges’ salaries and pensions**, expenditure of the **courts**, the **Comptroller and Auditor General**, the **President**, the **Speaker**. The **Chief Election Commissioner’s salary is not charged.** He said that is a demand they keep making, and that ideally it should be charged. It is not.
2. **Committed** expenditure, which does not depend on who is in power. **Interest** on a loan. His sketch: a **World Bank** loan of **₹1 lakh crore** at **1%** is **₹1,000 crore a year**. A government that skips the payment to shrink the fiscal deficit, which he defined here at the simple level as **expenditure minus revenue**, will face a dearer next loan. He used **2%** as that penalty. The loan is owed by the people of India, not by one ministry. Interest is therefore charged, not left to a vote.

The list itself is **Article 112(3)**. He set it as homework and named the items above. Do not add items he did not name.

**Taken in the next sitting:** the **budget in Parliament**, then the **ordinance**. With that, Part V closes and **Part VI** opens on the **Governor**. That sitting is Lecture 9.

At the door he answered two questions. On a **Rajya Sabha** count, if one seat is still open and **two** already elected candidates still hold surplus votes, **both** surpluses are transferred. He did not re-teach the system. On the Speaker: after the **tenure is over**, a Speaker who resigned the party to stay neutral **may rejoin that same party**, and may also choose not to. Lecture 7 still holds for the tenure itself: resigning and then joining **another** party is not the neutrality exception.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** A Money Bill **can** be a private member’s bill. The RS **cannot reject** it, and **14 days** of silence **deem** it passed. A known financial bill is **not** introduced in the RS. **Article 117(3)** is the recommendation at **consideration**, and only for **expenditure**. An Article 368 bill is passed by the Houses **sitting separately**. Ratification is **at least half the assemblies**, so **16 of 31**, not 15. A bill pending with the **President does not lapse**. The six months of a deadlock are **sittings**, and an adjournment **beyond four days** is left out. Joint sittings: **1960 / 1961, 1977, 2002**. Seat **numbers** are still the **1971** census. Women’s share is **as nearly as may be one-third of directly elected seats**, for **15 years**. **Article 267** is the Contingency Fund, corpus **₹30,000 crore since 2021**. The **Chief Election Commissioner is not** a charged salary. **99th**, not 199th, is the commission that was struck down.</span>
