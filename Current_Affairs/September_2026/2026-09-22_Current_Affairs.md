## Topic 1: India–New Zealand FTA — in force on 20 October

> **Micro-Topic ID:** `CA-260922-01`  
> **GS Paper:** **GS-II** (trade agreements) + **GS-III** (agriculture, MSMEs)

### 1. What is in the news

**Saptaparnno Ghosh.** The **Free Trade Agreement (FTA)** between India and **New Zealand** was **ratified on Monday (21 September 2026)** and **comes into effect on 20 October**.

**Piyush Goyal** (Union Commerce Minister): the aim is to **double bilateral trade to ₹35,000 crore** in the **next four to five years**. Entry into force is what turns that aim into outcomes.

**Todd McClay** (Minister of Trade and Investment, New Zealand): the deal lands in a time of global **uncertainty**, with **escalating tariffs and trade barriers**. It gives business communities confidence to grow, form relationships, and trade.

Goyal: New Zealand could support Indian companies, or **invest and set up manufacturing in India**, to capture global markets.

### 2. What each side gives (clip)

| Side | Clip |
|:---|:---|
| **New Zealand** (its foreign trade office) | Set to **erase import levies on all Indian goods** |
| **India** | **95%** of exports to India either **tariff-free** or at **“sharply” reduced** levies |
| **India’s red lines** | **Dairy** and sensitive agriculture stay protected. Named: **onions, almonds, chickpeas, peas, artificial honey, sugar**. **No** relaxation or concession to Wellington on these |
| **Who gains in India** | **MSMEs** (micro, small and medium enterprises), **farmers**, **handloom artisans**, **weavers** |
| **New Zealand’s offer beyond tariffs** | Technological expertise in **engineering** and other goods. The sentence continues on page 10 — stop there |

### 3. The present trade

India is New Zealand’s **ninth largest export market**. Bilateral trade in **FY 2024–25** stood at **$1.3 billion**.

The **₹35,000 crore** figure is the **doubling target** over four to five years, not this year’s trade.

### 4. One-liner

Ratified **21 September**; in force **20 October**. Double trade to **₹35,000 crore** in **4–5 years**. New Zealand drops levies on **all** Indian goods; India opens **95%**. **Dairy** and the named farm products stay shut. Present trade **$1.3 billion** (FY 2024–25). India = New Zealand’s **9th** export market.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** **₹35,000 crore** is the **target**, not FY 2024–25 trade. That year’s figure is **$1.3 billion**. **95%** is India’s opening to New Zealand’s exports, not “95% of Indian goods enter New Zealand” — New Zealand’s line is **all** Indian goods, levies erased. The **health annex** (7 Sep) is a different fact. Dairy is **protected**, not opened.</span>

## Topic 2: How Gaganyaan’s crew module deploys its parachutes

> **Micro-Topic ID:** `CA-260922-02`  
> **GS Paper:** **GS-III** (space, indigenisation)

### 1. What is in the news

**Unnikrishnan Nair S.**, director, **Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC)** and the **Indian Institute of Space Science and Technology (IIST)**.

A spacecraft re-entering Earth relies first on **atmospheric drag**. **Parachutes** do the last deceleration, including for a landing at sea. A crewed module needs **multi-stage**, **redundant** parachutes: high performance, packed into very little space.

### 2. Why more than one, and why in stages

| Piece | Job |
|:---|:---|
| **Apex chute** | A small parachute. It pulls out the wider **drogue** or **main** |
| **Drogue** | Early in the descent. Stabilises the module and makes the first cut in speed. The crew module has **two drogue** chutes. If one fails, an **independent chain** is still there |
| **Main** | Wider canopy. Final phase. Speed low enough for a **soft landing** |

The crew module’s **blunt** shape leaves a **turbulent wake** behind it. The parachute has to be **ejected through that wake** and open in the **far-wake** region. The energy of the ejection has to be enough to cross the wake.

Parachutes are deployed once the module is **below the speed of sound**.

In the lower atmosphere the module is still moving at about **170 m/s**. If the **full-size main** opened at once, the opening shock would **shred the fabric** or put a dangerous deceleration on the crew. Staging **increases the drag diameter step by step** and keeps the dynamic pressure manageable.

After **splashdown** or a soft landing, the canopy has to be **released at once** (pyrotechnic or mechanical **quick-release**). If it stays attached, wind can **drag** the module on land or **capsize** it at sea.

### 3. How they are tested

**Rail Track Rocket Sled (RTRS)** at the **Terminal Ballistic Research Laboratory (TBRL), Chandigarh**. A sled is accelerated along rails by small **solid motors**, and the parachute is deployed from the sled at a chosen speed.

Other modes in the clip: drop the test article from a **helicopter** or an **aircraft**, or use a small **rocket-powered** vehicle that releases the module from altitude.

### 4. Materials (clip only)

What is controlled: **tensile strength**, **thermal resistance**, **mass**. Modern aerospace parachutes use **synthetic polymers** cut to the design. **Nylon** is widely used in the high-load **suspension lines, risers, and structural reinforcement**.

The number of **main** canopies is **not** in the part of the page Nik sent. Do not invent it.

### 5. One-liner

**Drogue first** (two of them), **main last**. Full main at about **170 m/s** would shred or hit the crew too hard. Open only **below the speed of sound**, and **clear the wake**. Tested on the **RTRS** at **TBRL, Chandigarh**. Release the canopy the moment it has landed.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** **170 m/s** is why the **main** must not open first, not the speed at which the drogue is forbidden. **Nylon** in this clip is the **lines and risers**, not a claim that the whole canopy is nylon. **TBRL Chandigarh** is the sled range — not Sriharikota, and not the crew’s launch pad. This explainer does **not** give a new launch date. “Gaganyaan not done” stays the space lecture.</span>

## Topic 3: Article 22(1) — no grounds, no counsel, then release

> **Micro-Topic ID:** `CA-260922-03`  
> **GS Paper:** **GS-II** (fundamental rights, criminal justice)

### 1. What is in the news

**Krishnadas Rajagopal.** Supreme Court, judgment on **Monday (21 September 2026)**. Bench headed by Justice **Ujjal Bhuyan**, also comprising Justice **Atul S. Chandurkar**.

An arrested person kept **in the dark about the grounds of arrest**, and **without access to a lawyer**, is entitled to **immediate release**.

### 2. What Article 22(1) locks

The right to be told the **grounds of arrest in writing**, in a **language the person knows**, and the right to a **counsel of choice**, are fundamental rights under **Article 22(1)**. The Bench: they **“cannot be breached under any situation.”** Follow them **without exception**, and **at the earliest**.

Any breach means the arrest is **unconstitutional**, so the person is released **at once**. The Court will not treat Article 22 as a safeguard that can be tinkered with.

It is a **“mandatory constitutional safeguard.”** It applies **across the board**, including offences under the **Indian Penal Code** and special laws on **terrorism** and **money laundering**.

Agencies cannot call a breach a **“mere procedural lapse.”**

**Article 22(2):** the accused must be produced before a **Magistrate within 24 hours**.

### 3. What a breach does *not* do

| Holding | Clip |
|:---|:---|
| **Not bail** | Release for violating Article 22(1) is release from **illegal, unconstitutional detention**. He is **not enlarged on bail** |
| **Later papers do not cure it** | A **chargesheet** and a later **order of cognisance** do **not** validate that arrest |
| **No blanket immunity** | Some cases are grave. There is **no** immunity against a **future** arrest |
| **Second arrest** | Only under **stringent judicial oversight**. Police must **apply to the Magistrate** with **recorded reasons**. A **specific sanction** from that Magistrate must come **before** any second attempt to arrest a person whose earlier arrest was held illegal for violating Article 22(1) |

### 4. One-liner

**Bhuyan + Chandurkar, 21 September:** grounds **in writing**, in a **known language**, plus **counsel of choice** — **Article 22(1)**. Breach → **immediate release**, including in terror and money-laundering cases. That release is **not bail**. A later chargesheet does **not** repair the arrest. A second arrest needs the **Magistrate’s prior sanction**.

<span style="color: #e53e3e;">**Prelims trap:** This is **Article 22(1)** (grounds + lawyer), not **Article 22(2)** alone (24 hours) and not **Article 20(2)** (double jeopardy). Release ≠ **bail**. **Ujjal Bhuyan** on the **collegium** (26 Aug) is a different sitting. No case name is in the clip — do not invent one.</span>
